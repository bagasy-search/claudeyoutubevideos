My grandmother kept flour in a wooden bin in the corner of her pantry for fifty-one years, and in fifty-one years she never lost a single cup of it to a mouse. Not one. And you have to understand what kind of building that was. Stone foundation, laid up dry, no mortar in the bottom three courses. And a corn crib forty feet off the back door with eight hundred bushels in it. So forty feet from her pantry there was a mountain of food and a city of mice living in it, and they knew that mountain, and they knew that house, and they never got in.

She did not have poison. She did not have a cat, my grandmother did not care for cats. She had two pieces of metal, and one cost almost nothing and the other cost about the price of a chicken. That was the whole defense. I ran my hand along the bottom of that wall last fall and I could still feel it in there, hard as the day she packed it.

Two metals. That is what this video is. Two cheap metals you can buy this afternoon at any hardware store in the country, and between them there is not a mouse alive that gets into your house again. Not this winter, not in ten years. Because neither one rots, rusts away, dries out, or gets chewed.

Now. Before I go one more sentence I have to say two things, or I will have lied to you by the end of this.

The first. This does not work if you do it in the wrong order. If you seal your house up tight today and there are mice already living in the walls, you have not solved anything. You have built them a coffin, and in about nine days you are going to smell it, in a place you cannot reach, and it will stay with you three or four weeks. The right order is simple and I will give it to you later, but hear it now so you do not go start this backwards.

The second. There are three or four holes in your house you must never seal. People watch a video like this one, they get enthusiastic, and they go plug something that has to stay open. And one of them, if you screen it over, you have just built yourself a fire hazard. I will name every one of them and tell you what to do instead.

All right. What the two metals are, and then why they work, because the why is what keeps you from doing it wrong.

The first is copper. Not a sheet, not a pipe. Knitted copper mesh. It comes in a roll and it looks like a long gray-brown sleeve of loose knitting, like somebody made a scarf out of very fine wire. It is sold as copper mesh, sometimes copper wool, sometimes as a gutter guard. Twenty feet runs about twelve to fifteen dollars, and twenty feet is more than most houses need. You tear off a piece, you pack it into the hole, you are done.

The second is galvanized steel. Quarter inch hardware cloth, which is a terrible name because there is no cloth about it. It is a welded steel grid, stiff, square holes a quarter of an inch across, in a roll about three feet wide. Twenty five feet is around eighteen or twenty dollars.

So the whole defense of an entire house, copper for the small ragged holes and the grid for the big square ones, is about thirty dollars.

My neighbor paid a company four hundred and some dollars last year for what they called an exclusion service. He asked me to look at it afterward, and it was foam. Foam and steel wool. And I told him, that steel wool will be orange dust by the summer after next. And it was.

There is nothing clever here. It is just the correct material, and almost nobody uses it, because the correct material is never the one on the shelf at eye level.

My name is Claudio. I am Amish, out in Lancaster County. I do not have a pest control license and I will not pretend I do. What I have is thirty one years of sealing up buildings that hold food. Corn cribs, granaries, feed rooms, milk houses, and a chicken house that at one point was, honestly, overrun. I have done the wrong version of this and had it fail, and the right version and had it hold twenty years. The difference is not skill. It is four details.

So. Why the copper works, because when I explain this I always get the same argument, and the argument is wrong.

People say copper is a soft metal, and they are right. Press a mouse tooth and a piece of copper against each other and the tooth wins. A mouse incisor is harder than copper. It is harder than aluminum, and harder than lead, and mice chew through lead. So no. This is not about hardness.

Here is what actually happens, and it took me an embarrassingly long time to see it. A mouse does not bite through things. A mouse gnaws. The bottom two teeth come up and set into the material, they get a purchase, and then the whole jaw works and scrapes a sliver off. It is a chisel. That is exactly what those teeth are, a pair of chisels that never stop growing, about a third of a millimeter a day for the animal's whole life, which is why it chews whether it is hungry or not. And a chisel needs something to sit against. An edge, a face, a corner, something that holds still while the tooth pushes into it. Wood gives it that. So does drywall, so does plastic, so does foam, and so does steel wool once it has rusted into a hard orange cake.

Knitted copper mesh gives it nothing. It is a loose weave of fine round wire. The mouse sets its teeth on it and the strand rolls out of the way. It gets a strand between the teeth and pulls, and the whole knit stretches and comes with it, and springs back. No face, no edge, nothing that holds still. And every strand it does move is a bare wire end poking it in the gums. It is not that they cannot chew it. There is nothing there to chew. They try a while and they go somewhere else, and going somewhere else is the entire goal.

Then the second half, which decides whether this is still working in fifteen years. Copper does not rust. It goes dark, it goes green at the edges, but that is oxide sitting on a metal that is perfectly sound underneath.

Which brings me to steel wool, because steel wool is what everybody reaches for. It is not wrong exactly. It is temporary, and nobody tells you that. Steel wool is plain carbon steel with an enormous amount of surface area, which is the worst thing you can have where rust is concerned. Put it in a gap at the bottom of a foundation wall, the dampest spot in the whole building, and give it one winter. It goes orange in weeks. Then it swells, because iron oxide takes up more room than iron did, and it packs itself into a hard cake, and that cake has a face on it. That cake is chewable. And when it finally lets go it brings the mortar around it with it, so now your hole is bigger than when you started.

Twelve dollars of copper against four dollars of steel wool. Eight dollars difference, one time, on the whole house, and one of them is still working when your children own the place. Do not use steel wool. And if somebody already put steel wool in your house, go pull it out, and know the hole underneath is bigger than they told you.

Now the second metal, for a completely different kind of opening. Copper is for ragged little gaps. Where a pipe comes through a wall and there is a crescent of space around it. Where the sill plate sits down on the foundation and it is not quite flat. Something with an irregular shape that you need to fill. Hardware cloth is for openings that are supposed to be openings. A crawlspace vent. A gable vent. The gap under the bottom edge of the siding. A hole where something used to be and now is not. Anywhere air has to keep moving through but a mouse must not. And it works because it is a rigid welded grid fastened at the edges. Nothing to push, nothing to pull, nothing that flexes.

And the quarter inch matters. Not half inch. Not the screen on your windows. A quarter of an inch. Here is the number that should bother you, and it explains why you have mice in a house that looks perfectly sound.

An adult house mouse gets through a hole a quarter of an inch wide. About the width of a pencil. And that is not a mouse struggling, that is a mouse walking through. The skull is the limiting part and it is small, everything behind it is soft, and the ribs have a great deal of give. If the head fits, the mouse fits. A young one, six or seven weeks old and out looking for its own territory in the fall, which is exactly when your problem starts, gets through less than that.

So if you are standing in your basement looking for a mouse hole, you are looking for the wrong thing. You have a picture in your head from a cartoon, the little arch cut into the baseboard. It is nothing like that. It is the space where a wire goes through the rim joist. It looks like nothing at all. And half inch screen, or window screen, or the mesh on a standard foundation vent, all of that is a wide open door. Window screen they simply chew. It is aluminum or fiberglass and both give a tooth a clean edge to set against. It buys you a week.

Quarter inch galvanized. Nothing bigger. And galvanized just means coated in zinc, and the zinc is not decoration, the zinc is why it is still there in twenty years. Bare steel in a crawlspace vent is the steel wool problem again, only slower. It rusts through at the wire crossings and a whole section comes away in your hand. Get the galvanized, it is the same price. Twenty three gauge is plenty for a vent, nineteen gauge for something big.

Now, before the part that actually decides whether this works, one thing and then I will leave it alone. What I am telling you here is the same kind of thing that is in a book I keep on the shelf in my shop, called The Plain Almanac. About ninety plain household methods, the sort that used to get handed down and mostly does not anymore. Heating a room without electricity, moving water uphill without a pump, keeping a cellar dry. The link is at the top of the description. And I will say plainly, this mouse business is not in it, I looked. But it is the same spirit. Back to work.

So. The part that decides everything. I have watched a lot of people do this job and fail, and they almost never fail at the sealing. The copper goes in fine. The screen goes on fine. They fail because they sealed four holes and the house had eleven. Mice do not need the hole you found. They need any hole. So the real work here is not the patching, it is the hunting. Plan on the hunting being your whole first day and the patching being two hours on the second.

Three tools. The first is a flashlight, but not held the way you are holding it right now. Get down on the floor, get the light down level with the surface, and lay the beam flat along the wall so it rakes across. Almost parallel. That throws a long shadow out of anything that is not flat, and a quarter inch gap that is invisible under a ceiling light shows up as a black bar an inch long. Do the whole perimeter of the basement or crawlspace that way, slowly, on your knees. It is a miserable hour and it is the most valuable hour in this job.

The second tool is your eyes, and what you are looking for is a smudge. Mice have oil in their coat and they are creatures of terrible habit. They run the same route every night of their lives along the same wall, and where they squeeze past a corner they leave that oil behind. Over months it builds into a dark greasy smear, brown-black and shiny, right at the entry point. Droppings tell you a mouse was in the room. A rub mark tells you where the door is. When you find a smudge you have found a hole, every time.

The third is the one I like best, because it works while you sleep and costs nothing. Take flour, or corn starch, or talc, and lay a thin line along the base of the wall where you think they run. A dusting, not a pile, thin enough that a mouse walks through it without noticing. Do that at night, then look in the morning with your raking light. You will see tracks and a tail line, and you follow them back to where they come from. It will take you to holes you would never have found in a year.

Do all three. Mark every spot with pencil or a piece of tape, and do not fix anything yet. Because if you start patching while you are still hunting, you will get four in, feel accomplished, and stop.

Let me save you time and tell you where they actually are, because after thirty years it is always the same places.

One, and this is the big one. Where the wooden sill plate sits down on top of the foundation wall. That seam runs all the way around the building and it is almost never perfectly flat. On an old house it is not flat anywhere. Rake your light along it and I will bet you find three gaps.

Two, anywhere a pipe or a wire comes through a wall. Water line, gas line, electrical service, a hose bib, a dryer duct. Somebody bored a hole with a bit bigger than the pipe, because you always do, and the crescent left over is a mouse door. Under the kitchen sink is the most common one in the house.

Three, the corners of the garage door. Not the middle, the bottom corners. The rubber seal wears at the corners first and leaves a triangle of daylight, and if you can see daylight from inside with the door shut, that is a hole.

Four, crawlspace and foundation vents. Especially old ones with wide screen, and doubly so if the screen is torn at a corner, which it always is.

Five, the weep holes in brick veneer, if you have brick. Those little vertical slots in the mortar along the bottom course. Be careful here, because those are drains. They exist so water behind your brick can get back out. Do not fill them, do not mortar them, do not caulk them. Seal weep holes and you will rot the wall behind them, and that is an expensive mistake. What you do there is a loose plug of copper mesh, only copper, tucked in shallow so water still runs out through it.

Six, the gap at the bottom edge of the siding, especially vinyl, especially at a corner post. Those corner posts are hollow. That is a highway straight up into your wall.

Seven, up high, and people forget high entirely. Mice climb. A mouse goes up a rough block wall or a downspout with no trouble at all. Look at the soffit where it meets the wall, look at the gable vent, and anywhere a tree branch comes within a few feet of the roof.

Eight, and nobody wants to hear this one. The utility trench. Where the water line or the electric comes in underground there is sometimes a void alongside the pipe running all the way in from outside, and they come up inside the wall. If you have sealed everything you can find and you still have mice, that is where they are. Fill it from inside, copper packed deep, mortar over the face.

Now the ones you never seal. Your dryer vent. Never put mesh or screen over a dryer vent. Not copper, not hardware cloth, nothing. Lint catches in mesh, it builds up, and a plugged dryer duct is one of the more common ways houses catch fire. Instead, put on a proper vent hood with a flapper damper, the kind with a hinged door the air pushes open and that falls shut on its own. It closes when the dryer is off, and there is your mouse barrier, and the lint still gets out. If yours is missing its flap, which is very common, that is a five dollar part.

Your furnace and water heater exhaust and intake, the sealed combustion kind with white pipes through the wall. Those need to breathe and they were built with their own screens, leave them. The plumbing vent stack on the roof, leave it open. Weep holes, copper only and loose. And if you have soffit vents or a ridge vent, that is your roof breathing. Do not seal the attic airtight. You will trade a mouse for condensation and mold, and you would rather have the mouse.

Now back to the order, which is the mistake I warned you about at the start. Do not seal a house that has mice living in it.

Here is the sequence. First you hunt, and you mark, and you do not patch. Then you set traps inside. Plain wooden snap traps, more than you think you need, a dozen is not too many for a house, set along the walls because they travel the walls, baited with something sticky, not cheese. Peanut butter, a very small amount, so they have to work at it. Run those until you go three nights with nothing caught. Then you seal, and you are closing an empty house.

If you must do it all in one weekend, because winter is coming, then seal every hole except the one or two with the biggest rub marks. Leave those open with traps on both sides for a week and close them last. Do it the other way around and you will have animals dying inside your wall cavities, and there is no fixing that except waiting. Four weeks, sometimes six in cool weather. I have smelled it.

Now the job itself, which is the easy part. Four details.

Detail one. Clean the hole out first. Old caulk, old foam, crumbled mortar, rusted steel wool, a rag somebody stuffed in. Get it out and keep going until you are down to something solid. Wire brush it. You cannot pack into loose material, and every repair that failed on me early on failed because I packed against something soft.

Detail two. Pack the copper hard. This is the one people get wrong most. They lay a piece over the hole, or tuck a wisp into the mouth of it. That is not it. Take more than you think you need, fold it over on itself, drive it in with a screwdriver, and keep adding until the hole is full and tight through the whole depth of it, not just at the face. If you can pull it out with two fingers, a mouse can pull it out with its teeth, and it will. Pack it until it fights you.

Detail three. Cap it. Once the copper is packed, cover the face of it. On masonry, mortar. A little bag of mortar mix, mixed stiff, pressed over the copper and tooled flat. On wood or siding, a good exterior caulk or a hardening putty. The copper stops the mouse. The cap stops the weather and hides the copper from a curious animal. Both, not one.

And this is where somebody always says to just use expanding foam. Foam on its own is not a mouse barrier and I will be blunt. They chew foam like it is bread. Happily. It is soft, it is uniform, it takes a tooth beautifully, and I have pulled foam out of a rim joist that was tunneled through in half a dozen places with a nest in the middle of it. Foam is fine as an air seal on top of copper. Foam by itself is a decoration.

Detail four. Fasten the hardware cloth properly. Cut it two inches bigger than the opening on every side so you have an overlap to fasten through. Cut it with tin snips, not scissors, and wear a glove because those edges will open your hand up. Then fasten every two inches all the way around. Screws with washers into wood, masonry screws into block. Not staples. Not three screws in the corners. Every two inches, because a mouse will find the one spot where the edge lifts a quarter inch, and that is all it needs.

That is the entire method. Hunt, trap, clean, pack, cap, fasten.

Two last things, and one is about your health. In North America deer mice can carry hantavirus. It is rare, but the way people get it is by breathing dust from dried droppings and nests. So do not sweep and do not vacuum. Both put it in the air, which is exactly what you must not do. Mix one part household bleach to ten parts water, spray the droppings until they are wet, leave it ten minutes, then pick everything up with paper towels wearing rubber gloves, and put it in a bag and tie it shut. Wet, wipe, bag. Not sweep. And wear gloves with the copper mesh too, those wire ends go into your fingers and you will be picking them out for a week.

And on poison, I am not going to lecture anybody. I will only tell you what I have seen. A poisoned mouse dies wherever it happens to be, and that is very often inside a wall, which is the smell problem all over again. And it moves up. A cat, an owl, somebody's dog that eats a poisoned mouse gets a dose of it too, and out here the barn cats are the actual pest control. The metal does not have that problem. The metal just closes the door.

This is also not instant. The night after you finish you will probably still hear something, and that is normal. Sound carries oddly in a wall and there may be one or two still inside. Give it two weeks. Somewhere in there you notice you have not heard anything in a while, and then a month goes by, and then you stop thinking about it entirely. That is what winning looks like here. It is not dramatic. It is just quiet.

And it stays quiet, and that is what is different about it. A trap is a thing you tend forever. Poison is a thing you buy forever. Foam and steel wool are a thing you redo every couple of years. Copper and galvanized steel you do once, correctly, on your knees for one long Saturday, and then it is done, and the building is closed, and it stays closed while you go on and think about other things.

That is what my grandmother had. It was not vigilance. She was not out there fighting mice. She had, at some point long before I knew her, closed the house, and after that there was nothing left to fight.
