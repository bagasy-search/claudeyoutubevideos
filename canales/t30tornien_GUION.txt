Look out the window at any airport in the world. It doesn't matter what country, it doesn't matter what airline, it doesn't matter if the plane is forty years old or rolled out of the factory last month. Almost all of them are white. White from nose to tail, with a little color on the tail fin and the airline's name down the side. And you'd think it's just taste. That somebody, a long time ago, decided it looked classy, and everybody copied them.

It isn't. That white is one of the most carefully calculated decisions in all of aviation. It has to do with the sun, with weight, with money, with cracks you can't see with the naked eye, and with a detail of modern airplanes that very few passengers have ever heard about. And at the end of this video, I'll tell you about the airlines that dared to paint their planes black, and what they had to do to make it work.

Let's start with the most obvious reason, which also happens to be the most important. The sun.

An airplane spends a big part of its life sitting still. Waiting at the gate, in the middle of summer, at airports in hot places, sometimes for hours, with the sun beating straight down on its roof. And the roof of an airplane is a huge surface, made of metal or composite materials, soaking up everything that hits it.

Anyone who has ever touched a black car parked in the sun at noon knows what happens. It burns. A white car, in the same parking lot, at the same time, you can put your hand on. And the difference isn't small. White reflects most of the sunlight that hits it. Black absorbs almost all of it and turns it into heat.

Now think about that at the scale of an airplane. If the fuselage heats up, the cabin heats up. And if the cabin heats up, somebody has to cool it down. With the main engines off, on the ground, that job falls to a small auxiliary engine in the tail, or to airport equipment that pumps cold air in through a big hose. All of that burns fuel or uses power. All of that costs money. A white airplane simply starts the day with less heat inside. And when you multiply that by thousands of airplanes and every single day of the year, it adds up to an enormous amount of money.

But heat doesn't just affect passenger comfort. It affects the airplane itself. And this is the detail I promised you.

For decades, airplanes were built almost entirely out of aluminum. Aluminum handles the heat of the sun pretty well. But the newest airplanes, the long-range jets designed in the last couple of decades, use more and more composite materials. Carbon fiber held together with resins. They're a lot lighter than metal, and that saves fuel on every single flight. But they have a weakness. The resins that hold them together don't love extreme heat. If the surface gets too hot, again and again, for years, the material can lose some of its strength.

That's why the manufacturers of these airplanes give guidance on which colors can be used, and how much of the surface can be painted dark. It's not a matter of style. It's a maintenance rule. On these airplanes, white isn't just convenient. On large parts of the fuselage, it's practically required.

Reason number two. Weight.

It sounds crazy, but paint is heavy. A coat of paint on a small surface weighs nothing. But a big passenger jet has thousands of square feet of surface. And painting the whole thing can add hundreds of pounds. On some big airplanes, the paint alone weighs about as much as several passengers with their luggage.

And in aviation, every pound counts. Every extra pound has to be lifted off the runway on every takeoff, and carried through every hour in the air. That's fuel. An airplane flies thousands of hours a year, for twenty or thirty years. A little extra weight, over that whole lifetime, turns into a staggering amount of fuel.

So what does white have to do with weight? Colorful designs need more layers. If you want a deep red or a bright blue, a lot of the time you need a base coat first, then the color, then another coat of color, then the clear protective layer on top. Complex designs, with fades, with pictures, with lots of different shades, pile on layers, and they pile on pounds. A white fuselage, with the color focused on the tail and a few stripes, is simpler. And lighter.

Some airlines went all the way to the other extreme. They left their planes unpainted, showing the bare aluminum, shiny and silver. That way they saved the weight of the whole paint job. But that comes with a cost. Bare metal has to be polished regularly to keep it from corroding and to keep it looking good. And polishing an entire airplane is a job that takes days. Over time, nearly all of those airlines went back to painting. And when they did, they chose white.

Reason number three, and this is my favorite, because it's the one nobody sees. Cracks.

An airplane gets inspected constantly. Before every flight, somebody walks all the way around it, looking it over. And every so many hours, the airplane goes into a hangar for a much more detailed inspection. One of the things they're looking for is any sign that something's off. A tiny crack around a rivet. A dent where something hit it. A streak of oil or hydraulic fluid leaking from somewhere it shouldn't. Rust or corrosion starting to show through.

On a white surface, all of that shows up. A dark line, a stain, a shadow, it jumps right out at you. On a dark surface, or one covered in pictures and colors, those same signs can hide. White works like a clean sheet of paper. Anything that shows up on it, you notice.

And on an airplane, catching something small early is the difference between a simple repair and a serious problem. That's why aircraft mechanics, generally speaking, prefer light-colored planes. It makes their job easier, and it gives them more peace of mind.

Now let's talk about a reason almost nobody mentions. Money, but not fuel money. The money involved when an airplane changes hands.

A lot of people assume airplanes belong to the airlines that fly them. Very often, that's not true. A huge share of the world's airplanes are leased. There are companies that buy airplanes and rent them out to airlines for a few years at a time. When the lease ends, the plane goes back to the leasing company, and the company rents it out to another airline, sometimes on another continent.

And every time an airplane switches airlines, it has to be repainted. The old paint has to come off and the new one has to go on. That's nothing like painting a wall. It's a job that can take more than a week, with the plane parked in a special hangar, not flying, not making any money. Every day that airplane sits there is a day lost.

If the airplane is already white, the switch is a lot simpler. A lot of the time, you just change the tail, the name, and a few details. The rest of the fuselage stays as it is. That's why leasing companies want their planes to come back white. And airlines, who know that one day they'll be returning that plane, or selling it, want the same thing. A white airplane is easier to lease and easier to sell.

There's one more reason, and it has to do with time. The sun doesn't just heat things up. It also fades them.

An airplane flies miles up, above a big part of the atmosphere that protects us down here on the ground. Up there, the sun's radiation is stronger. And over the years, that radiation eats away at colors. Reds turn pink. Blues turn gray. Dark colors go dull and blotchy. An airplane painted in bold colors can look old and neglected after just a few years.

White ages too, but you can barely tell. A slightly worn white still looks white. And that means the airplane can go longer without needing a new paint job. Less time in the hangar. Less money spent.

And here's one last reason, a very practical one. When a white airplane gets dinged, scratched, or needs a part replaced, finding paint that matches is easy. White is the most common color in the industry. Matching a custom color, on the other hand, can be a headache. And a patch in the wrong shade, right in the middle of the fuselage, looks terrible.

And while we're talking about paint, let me tell you what it actually takes to paint an airplane, because almost nobody has ever seen it done.

First, the plane gets rolled into a special hangar. Not a regular one. A paint hangar, with huge fans and filters that pull the air through constantly, and with the temperature and humidity kept just right. Paint on an airplane is picky. If it's too cold, it won't cure. If it's too humid, it can cloud up or fail to stick properly. So the whole room is controlled like a laboratory.

Then comes the hardest part. Getting the old paint off. Crews cover everything that can't get wet or scratched. The windows, the sensors, the little probes on the nose that measure speed, the landing gear, the engines. All of it gets masked off with paper and tape, by hand. Then the old paint is stripped, either with chemical strippers or, more and more these days, by sanding it down carefully. On composite airplanes, they have to be especially gentle, because you can't damage the material underneath.

Once the old paint is gone, the metal or composite skin gets cleaned, inspected, and treated so the new paint will grip. And here's something interesting. This is one of the best moments in an airplane's life to look for problems. With the paint gone, every rivet, every seam, every little patch is right there in the open. Mechanics often take advantage of it.

Then the primer goes on. Then the base color. On a typical airliner, that base is, you guessed it, white. Then the crews mask off everything again to paint the tail, the stripes, the airline's name. Every color is another round of masking, spraying, waiting, and unmasking. And finally, a clear coat on top to protect it all.

The whole job, start to finish, often takes somewhere between one and three weeks, depending on the size of the plane and how complicated the design is. Dozens of people. Hundreds of gallons of paint and primer. And through all of it, the plane isn't flying a single passenger.

Now you can see why every airline wants to keep that process as short and as rare as possible. And why a simple white fuselage, with color only where it really counts, makes so much sense.

So put it all together. White keeps the airplane cooler and saves energy on the ground. It protects the composite materials in modern airplanes. It allows simpler, lighter paint jobs. It lets mechanics see cracks, stains, and corrosion. It makes it cheaper and easier to move a plane from one airline to another. It fades less. And it's easier to touch up. It's not one reason. It's seven reasons, all pushing toward the same color.

And now, what I promised you. The airlines that dared to go black.

Because in spite of all this, every now and then an airline comes along that decides to break the rule. It wants its planes to stand out at any airport on earth. So it paints one black, or a blue so dark it's almost black. And they look spectacular. In photos, on social media, on the runway. People stop to take pictures of them.

But to make it work, there are a few things you have to do. On metal airplanes, the main problem is the heat and the extra fuel it takes to cool the cabin, and the airline basically treats it as an advertising expense. It's cheaper than a lot of ad campaigns. But on composite airplanes, it's more serious. There, the dark paints have to be special formulas that reflect part of the sun's heat even though they look dark. And even then, the airline has to follow the manufacturer's guidance about which parts can be painted and which can't.

If you take a close look at one of those dark airplanes, a lot of the time you'll notice it's not completely black. The top, the part that gets the most sun, is sometimes lighter. Or the black is only in certain areas. Every line of that paint job was thought through by engineers, not just designers.

So when an airline rolls out a black airplane, what it's really doing is paying to be noticed. It pays in fuel, it pays in maintenance, it pays in special paint. And some of them decide it's worth it. But they're the exceptions. The rule, the one that rules every airport in the world, is still white.
