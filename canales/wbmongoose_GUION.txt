In eighteen eighty-three, a few sugar planters in Hawaii had a rat problem. So they shipped in a small hunter from Jamaica. Seventy-two of them, by one common account.

Today that hunter lives on most of the main islands. It eats the eggs of a goose that once dropped to about thirty birds on the whole planet.

And the damage runs about fifty million dollars a year, by a 1999 estimate. That bill is paid by everybody, except the men who ordered the animals.

The rats? Still there.

Nobody ever really stopped the rats, and nobody ever stopped the mongoose. That's the part I can't get over.

Here is the question that holds this whole file together. How did a group of smart men with good intentions do this? And why did nobody stop them?

Today I'm opening the whole file. Who did it, what happened year by year, and who is still paying for it. And at the end, the part nobody tells you: what is actually working now.

You know me, or you will soon. I'm Walt. I spent forty years in the field, and I've seen this same kind of mistake twice. The coffee in this mug is older than some of you, and the specimen jars on that shelf have been looking at me for a long time. Every file on this channel is a true story, and the sources are in the description, all of them.

Let's start with the problem. Hawaii's sugar industry was built on cane, and cane is sweet. Sweet things attract rats. A rat can eat a lot of cane, and a rat can carry disease. The planters had a real problem, and I don't want you to think they were stupid.

If you had a field full of cane and rats eating it, you might have done the same thing. Be honest with yourself about that.

The planters looked for a solution. In 1883 they didn't have much to choose from. There was no modern rat poison, no quick way to clear a field of rodents, and no one to tell them that a rat, once it settles in, is hard to get rid of.

So they went looking for a predator. The animal had a reputation. It was small, it was fast, and it had a name for killing snakes and rats in the places it came from.

The animal was the small Indian mongoose. It is native to India, and it reached Jamaica as a rat killer before it ever reached Hawaii. The Hawaiian planters took Jamaica's use of it as a recommendation.

The sales pitch was good, too. An 1883 Honolulu newspaper called the mongoose a famous ratter, better than the cat or the ferret. Nobody on the islands had ever watched one hunt in their own fields. That didn't stop the advertisement.

Now, the numbers. One account, quoting an 1898 newspaper, says seventy-two mongooses were brought from Jamaica to Hawaii in 1883. Other accounts give different numbers, and different people who paid for the trip. Accounts differ on this, and the paperwork is thin. What is not in dispute is the year, and the place. The animals went out on the Big Island, on the Hilo side, into the cane fields.

Now the part most people get wrong. You will hear that the plan was doomed from the start, because rats come out at night and mongooses hunt by day, so the two never met. It's a neat story. The Bishop Museum in Hawaii calls it a myth, and says the mongoose was actually an excellent cane field ratter. So the plan did something. The problem is what it did next.

Here is the thing about a rat. It breeds fast, it eats almost anything, and it doesn't care about your sugar contract. A plan that kills some rats is not a plan that wins. At best it buys you one season, and then you're back where you started, with a new animal on the island.

The mongoose did not stay in the cane fields. It wandered into the lowlands, and then up into the forests. A small animal with no natural enemies and a lot of territory to explore does not stop at the edge of a sugar field.

And there was nothing to stop it. Hawaii's only native land mammals were bats. The birds, the snails and the insects had never had to deal with a hunter that walks on the ground and eats eggs. They did not have time to adapt.

Picture a whole chain of life built on that one fact. No land predators for the birds. No reason for a nest to hide. Everything on the ground trusted the ground, and it had every reason to.

Think about what that means for a bird. It has spent thousands of years nesting where nothing could reach it. Then one animal arrives, and the bird has no instinct for it at all. Nobody gave it a warning.

So what did the mongoose eat, once the rats were not enough? It ate what was easy to find. Ground-nesting birds are easy to find. Their nests sit on the ground, and the eggs don't run away.

The nēnē is the bird at the center of this story. It is Hawaii's state bird, a goose that lives only on those islands. It nests on the ground. For a mongoose, that is an easy meal.

The Bishop Museum says the mongoose preys on ground-nesting birds and their eggs, including seabirds and the nēnē. In 1979, a paper in the Hawaii Audubon Society's journal reported a mongoose as the probable predator of a nēnē in Hawaii Volcanoes National Park. The female was killed, and the male abandoned a nest with three eggs in it.

Think about that for a second. One nest, three eggs, one bird gone.

Birds don't get a second season for free. When a nest fails, the bird doesn't file a complaint and try again next year with a better plan. It just doesn't come back to that spot. Multiply that by every nest in every season, and you start to see how a species can disappear.

Ask any field biologist what a lost nest looks like. It's an empty bowl of grass. That's all it is, and it's the whole loss.

Some sources also say the mongoose takes sea turtle eggs on the beaches. That claim comes from secondary text, so I'll tell you it's reported, not measured.

Let's go year by year, because the story is not one big mistake. It's a long string of small ones.

First, 1883. The release. The planters reported fewer rats in their cane, and that's what they wanted to hear. The early reports were good news, at least for the sugar. One compiled account says the sugar producers saw a marked drop in rats, and an early Hilo report said the rat damage went down as the mongoose population went up. Those were the reports that kept the animal in the fields.

Then the mongoose kept multiplying. It spread across the Big Island, and then to Maui, then Molokai, then Oahu. No firm year is on record for each of those moves, so I won't invent one. What they agree on is the order, and that the spread never came back.

Second, 1949. The nēnē is down to a handful. A territorial biologist starts a captive breeding program with a very small founding group. The birds the program raises are first released in 1960. That is the rescue, and it's a good story, so keep it in mind.

Those first releases were small. The birds had to survive the same ground, the same predators, and the same years of argument about what to do next. Rescue is slow work, and it always costs more than the first mistake did. The rescue was happening at the same time the animal it was up against was still spreading.

Third, 1967. The nēnē is listed as endangered by the federal government. The bird that once dropped to about thirty is now on the official list. The mongoose is not on any list, and nobody is calling it back.

A federal listing is a legal word. It doesn't mean the bird is safe. It means somebody has finally written down that it isn't.

Fourth, 1976. A female mongoose is found dead along a road near Kalaheo, on Kauai. That's the first record I could find on the island that was supposed to be clean. A dead animal on a road is not a big story, but it's a warning, and the people who work on Kauai treat it like one.

Fifth, 2012 to 2023. Kauai starts catching them. The Kauai Invasive Species Committee records live captures at Lihue, at Nawiliwili Harbor, and in the cargo area of the airport. The most recent one I found is from April 2023, at Nawiliwili. Five live animals on an island with no established breeding population is five reasons to keep watching.

Here is what Kauai did right, and I want you to notice it, because it's rare. Crews didn't wait for a population. They responded to every sighting, and they set traps when someone reported a mongoose. The island that is still mostly clean did not do anything clever. It just decided early that it would not wait and see.

Notice what that means. Kauai is the one place in this story where somebody got to act before the damage was done. Most of the other islands never got that chance, because nobody was watching for the first animal.

Now the money. The most quoted number is about fifty million dollars a year, for damage in Hawaii and Puerto Rico together. It comes from the Cornell ecologist David Pimentel, in 1999. Later groups quote the same figure without re-measuring it. Fifty million a year works out to about one hundred thirty-seven thousand dollars a day, every day, for a little animal nobody meant to bring. That's a 1999 estimate, and I'll say it that way every time.

The same thing happened on the other side of the Pacific. Puerto Rico also got mongooses in the late 1800s, supposedly to kill rats in the cane. Two islands, one bad idea, and one estimate that covers both.

Who pays? The farmer pays. The bird pays. The public pays for the traps, the fences, the staff, and the years of work to keep the animal out of places it hasn't reached yet.

Here is a number that should bother you more than the money. The nēnē was down to about thirty birds at its lowest point. Thirty birds would not fill one school bus. Captive breeding brought it back. Today the federal government counts it as threatened, not endangered, and there are roughly two thousand eight hundred birds on the islands. That's about ninety times the low point, and it's still a bird that has to get its nest through the season.

That is a good story, and I'm not going to ruin it.

A recovery is not the same as a safe bird. Think of the nest, the season, the road, and the predator, all at once. That's the list a nēnē is working through every spring. But adding a predator that eats eggs to a species that was once thirty birds is not a small problem. The Ohio State science text I checked lists eight of Hawaii's federally endangered birds as suffering heavy nest predation. Most of those birds are few, and their nests are on the ground. Every nest counts.

Every one of those birds was a living thing with a nest and a season. The mongoose didn't read the plan. It did what a mongoose does.

Now, the warning. Did somebody warn them in writing? That's the question I always come back to. I could not confirm a written warning that reached the Hawaiian planters, so I won't put a letter on your screen that I can't show you. What I can say is that the animal already had a reputation, and the planters went ahead anyway. That's enough to make the point.

So far, here's the file in brief. Seventy-two animals, maybe more, maybe fewer. A rat problem the animal only partly solved. A nest that never stood a chance. And a bill that landed on people who had nothing to do with the decision.

Here's what the record does show. It shows a mongoose that was let go on purpose, by people who had a reason they believed in. It shows a population that spread faster than anyone tracked. It doesn't show a single person who sat down and asked what happens when the rats are gone. That question was never on the table.

Behind me, on that wall map, there are pins from several decades. Every one marks a place where somebody decided to bring something in. Some pins are red, some are blue, and the notes in my handwriting are getting harder to read. The pins don't come out easily, and neither does a species.

Now, what is being done about it? The honest answer is mixed. Control is possible, but it is slow, expensive, and never finished.

Before we get there, the recap, because it matters. A planter's rat problem. A small hunter from Jamaica. Nests on the ground. A goose that nearly vanished. And a bill, about fifty million a year, on a 1999 estimate. That's the file so far. Now let's look at what it takes to pay less.

Crews set traps. They use bait stations. They protect nests when they can. And they keep watching for the animal in places where it isn't supposed to be.

Some of that works. Trapping can knock down local populations, and early detection on Kauai has kept the animal from taking hold there. Protecting a nest can give a bird a better chance in a single season.

The cheapest tool on the list is the one nobody puts on a poster. Notice every animal, and act the same day. The Kauai crews do that, and they've been doing it for decades, on one island.

What doesn't work is pretending the problem is solved. Removing the animal from a large island, with forest and rough country, is extremely hard. Anyone who says it's easy is selling something.

What I want you to understand is that this work is not glamorous. Nobody gets a medal for a trap line that caught nothing this month. Nobody writes a headline when a nest survives a season. That kind of work is still the work, and the people who do it know it.

If you want to see what that looks like, you'd find it in a small office, a map with pins, and a stack of reports nobody reads twice. That's where the real file lives.

So the honest answer is: keep the animal out where it isn't, control it where it is, and protect the birds that can't protect themselves. Boring, unglamorous, and expensive. It's the only approach I've seen hold up.

I want to be fair to the planters for one minute, because they're easy to hate and it doesn't help anybody. They had a real rat problem, and they were trying to save their crop.

But fair is not the same as right. A plan that works for a field in the first season can still cost an island for the next century. The planters didn't have to count the birds, and they didn't. Nobody asked the mongoose what it wanted for dinner, either.

If there is a monument to 1883 on those islands, I haven't seen it. I've seen the birds, the traps, and the people paying for both.

Let me put the whole thing in plain words, the way you'd explain it to a twelve-year-old and a seventy-year-old at the same time. Somebody wanted fewer rats. They got a predator that hunted the wrong things, in the wrong places, for a very long time. The rats were never the whole problem, and the mongoose was never a fix.

And here's the lesson, in one sentence, the way I'd say it to a student in the field. Nature doesn't read your business plan.

That line gets nodded at in every planning meeting. The trouble is that the people nodding are rarely the ones who live with the outcome. The birds live with it. The farmers live with it. And the next generation inherits it, with no vote.

If you take only one thing from this file, take that. You can't write a clever plan that controls a living animal on an island that has no defense against it. The animal will do what it does, in the place where it was put.

Here's a rule I'd give any student who walked into my field station. Before you bring in something to fix a problem, ask what it eats when your problem is gone. Then ask who pays when it keeps eating.

Write that down before you buy the animal. Then do the harder part, which is actually asking the question out loud, before anyone gets a shipping crate.
