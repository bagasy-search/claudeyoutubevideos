So let's go back to the beginning. Christmas, nineteen sixty-nine.

Before that, Norway was not rich. It was a country of fishermen, farmers and sailors. Many families had relatives who had emigrated to America because there was not enough here.

Foreign oil companies had been drilling off the coast for years and finding very little. Some were ready to give up. And then, just before Christmas, nineteen sixty-nine, the last exploration well in one block of the North Sea was plugged, with the knowledge that they had found something huge.

The field was called Ekofisk. Norwegians still call it the country's big Christmas present.

Production started about two years later. And from that moment, Norway's story changed forever.

What Norway did next is the part everyone admires, and fairly. It didn't spend it all.

Other countries that found oil had a party. Big palaces, big debts, and when prices crashed, a big hangover. Economists even have a name for it, the resource curse. Norway looked at that and decided to do something very boring and very clever.

In nineteen ninety, the parliament passed a law creating a fund. The idea was simple. The oil money would not go into the normal budget. It would be invested abroad, in shares, bonds and property, so it would keep earning long after the oil was gone.

The first deposit arrived in May nineteen ninety-six. Just under two billion kroner. A small cheque, by today's standards.

Then came the rule that makes it work. Governments are only supposed to spend about the expected real return of the fund each year. Today that's estimated at three percent. Not the fund itself. Just roughly what it earns. The rest stays, for the children, and their children.

That's why its official name is the Government Pension Fund Global. Most people just call it the oil fund.
