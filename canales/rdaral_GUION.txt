In nineteen sixty, the Aral Sea was the fourth biggest lake on Earth. Sixty-eight thousand square kilometers of water, out in the desert of Central Asia. Bigger than the whole state of West Virginia.

By two thousand fourteen, the Aral had lost about ninety-six percent of its water. That summer, the whole eastern basin of the sea dried up. Gone. Sand where the waves used to be.

The fishing town of Muynak, a port, ended up a hundred and fifty kilometers from the water. That's over ninety miles. The fishing boats are still sitting there in the sand, rusting, pointed at a sea that isn't coming back.

Three numbers. Forty-eight thousand tons of fish in a single year. Then zero. Around sixty thousand jobs tied to that fishery, by one Soviet-era count. Then none. And the people who stayed got the dust. Salt and farm chemicals, blowing off the old sea floor, into their lungs.

Fishermen woke up and the sea was gone. That's how it felt to them. It actually took about thirty years. But it was decided long before that, on paper, in offices in Moscow.

First, you have to understand the people who made this plan. They weren't cartoon villains. They had a problem, and the problem was the desert.

The Aral sits between two countries today, Kazakhstan to the north and Uzbekistan to the south. Back then, both were part of the Soviet Union, run from Moscow.

It's hot, dry country. Big skies. Not much rain. But two great rivers run through it, down from the snow in the mountains. The Amu Darya and the Syr Darya. "Darya" just means river.

Those two rivers ran across the desert and emptied into the Aral. The Aral had no outlet. It held its level because what the rivers brought in, the sun took back out. Year after year, it stayed in balance.

And that sea was busy. It had a real fishing industry, carp and bream and sturgeon. It had boat routes. It had ports at both ends, Aralsk in the north and Muynak in the south.

People had been farming along those rivers for at least three thousand years. Irrigation canals, small fields. And for most of that time, it didn't hurt the sea, because a lot of the water ran back into the rivers.

The Soviet planners wanted cotton. A lot more cotton. And cotton loves exactly what that desert had. Hot sun, a long season, and river water you can lead into the fields.

So in the nineteen fifties and sixties, they planned a huge expansion of irrigation. More canals, more fields, more water out of both rivers.

And here's the part I want you to hear clearly. They knew what it would do to the sea.

A geographer named Philip Micklin, who spent his career on the Aral, laid it out in the journal Science in nineteen eighty-eight. During that planning, it was predicted that the irrigation would reduce the flow to the sea and substantially shrink it.

And at the time, a number of experts called that a worthwhile tradeoff. Their math was simple. A cubic meter of river water on a cotton field was worth more than the same water sitting in the Aral.

Some of them went further. In Micklin's words, shrinking the Aral down to a leftover brine lake, with all its water going to farming, was viewed as both desirable and inevitable.

Desirable. That's the word. On paper, that's a fine trade. Paper doesn't have to breathe.

They even planned for the fish. By the nineteen fifties, fishery experts could see the sea would get saltier and the native fish would disappear. So the plan was to bring in salt-tolerant species ahead of time.

They had a plan for the fish. They didn't have one for the fishermen.

And they had answers for every worry. Some scientists said the sea didn't affect the weather much, so drying it wouldn't change the climate. They said salt from the dry bottom wouldn't blow around, because a hard crust would form and hold it down.

Some optimists even said the dried sea floor would make good farmland. It's been sixty-some years. I'm still waiting on that harvest.

Now, who said stop? According to Micklin, a small number of scientists warned of serious damage. They were not heeded.

Years later, an environmental researcher from the Aral region, Yusup Kamalov, who said he had seen the maps of the plan, put it plainly to a Washington Post reporter. "They decided that the sea was not necessary," he said, "that the value of the cotton would be greater than the loss of the fish."

So that's the setup. A desert, two rivers, a plan for cotton, and a sea that the planners had already written off.

The water started going to the fields in the early nineteen sixties. In nineteen sixty, the surface of the sea stood about fifty-three meters above sea level, where it had stayed, give or take a few meters, for a couple of hundred years.

The irrigated land in the basin was around five million hectares. The plan was to keep making it bigger. And it worked. That's the thing. The fields grew.

But every gallon that went onto a field was a gallon that didn't reach the sea. And the sun over the Aral didn't take a day off.

Let me take you through it year by year.

The mid-nineteen sixties. The first damage shows up where you'd least expect anyone in Moscow to look. In the shallow bays where fish lay their eggs. The water there got saltier first, and by the late sixties, spawning was getting worse.

Nineteen seventy-one. The level is down more than two meters. The open sea is getting saltier, and now the adult fish start to suffer. They grow slower. Their numbers fall sharply.

Nineteen seventy-five. Satellites pick up something new. Big storms of salt and dust, blowing off the dry sea floor. Between nineteen seventy-five and nineteen eighty-one, Soviet scientists confirmed twenty-nine of them. In some years, up to ten.

So much for the hard crust that was going to hold the salt down.

By the middle of the seventies, the fish of the Aral could no longer breed in the sea at all. No young fish coming up. The old ones were the last ones.

And by nineteen eighty-one, the Aral Sea had completely lost its fishery. Twenty of its twenty-four native fish species were gone by the early eighties. The commercial catch, forty-eight thousand tons back in nineteen fifty-seven, fell to zero.

Forty-eight thousand tons a year is about a hundred and thirty tons of fish every single day.

The big fish canneries in Aralsk and Muynak didn't close right away. They kept running on fish brought in from far away. Micklin listed the Atlantic, the Pacific and the Arctic. By nineteen ninety-two, the Muynak cannery was packing fish hauled more than two thousand miles from the Baltic Sea.

Picture that. A fish cannery in a fishing town, shipping in fish from the other end of the country, because the sea it was built on had walked away.

Meanwhile, what about the cotton? This is the part that would be funny if it weren't so sad.

In the nineteen eighties, Uzbekistan's party bosses were promising Moscow enormous harvests. Six million tons of cotton, they pledged.

Then the investigators came. In nineteen eighty-eight, the Moscow News reported that in one year alone, nearly a million tons had been added by distorting the results. Cotton that only existed on paper. In one deal alone, a quarter of a billion rubles was stolen.

So they drained a sea for cotton, and some of the cotton wasn't even real.

Nineteen eighty-six. There was one big rescue plan on the table. Turn some of the great rivers of Siberia south, and send that water two thousand five hundred kilometers into Central Asia.

The first phase was close to being built. And in nineteen eighty-six, Mikhail Gorbachev stopped it. Scientists and writers had fought it for years, afraid of the damage it would do up north.

Nineteen eighty-seven. The sea is down about thirteen meters from nineteen sixty. It has lost around forty percent of its area. And by nineteen eighty-nine, it splits in two. A small sea in the north, fed by the Syr Darya. A large sea in the south, fed by what's left of the Amu Darya.

In nineteen eighty-eight, Micklin wrote in Science that the planners had ignored warnings of dire consequences from some scientists. He said the Aral could become several leftover, lifeless brine lakes early in the next century.

He turned out to be right about that too.

So let's recap. They chose cotton over the sea, on purpose. The fish went first, then the jobs, then the sea split, then a whole basin of it dried away. Now the question is who's still paying.

Start with the dust. The dry sea floor left behind is a new desert. People there call it the Aralkum. By two thousand three, the European Space Agency put it at forty thousand square kilometers of white salt ground. That's bigger than the state of Maryland.

The storms that come off it today can cover more than a hundred thousand square kilometers and reach over five hundred kilometers downwind. That's more than three hundred miles.

Back in nineteen eighty-eight, Micklin passed on an estimate that forty-three million tons of salt a year were being blown off the old sea floor and dropped on the land around it. And that's not table salt. Satellite researchers at the European Space Agency say the storms carry pesticide residue, and have been linked to high rates of breathing illness and certain cancers in the region.

Now, think about where that salt lands. On the fields. On the same farms that took the water. NASA says the salty dust settles onto the fields and wears out the soil, so the farmers have to flush them with more and more river water. Which means less water for the sea. Which means more dust.

The weather changed too. In a strip up to about a hundred kilometers wide along the old shore, summers got hotter and winters got colder. Spring frosts come later, fall frosts come earlier. The growing season got shorter. The sea used to be the air conditioner. Now it's gone.

So the planners were wrong about the climate. They were wrong about the salt. And the dry sea floor never did turn into that farmland.

And then there are the people. The ones who lived on the south shore, in a region called Karakalpakstan, and around Aralsk in the north.

Scientists who've studied it found people around the sea suffering from serious breathing and stomach illnesses, from the salt and dust they inhale, and from poorer diets after the fish, their main protein, disappeared.

In nineteen ninety-two, the Washington Post reported infant deaths of nearly a hundred per thousand births in some districts of Karakalpakstan. That's about one baby in ten.

And people left. Micklin reported that more than forty thousand had left the districts along the east and northeast shore alone, and that whole fishing villages were completely abandoned.

Those were the people who did exactly what they were supposed to do. They fished. They canned. They fed the country. Nobody asked them. Nobody in Moscow ever had to taste that wind.

After the sea split, the small northern sea, on the Kazakh side, kept leaking its water south into the big one. In nineteen ninety-two, the local authorities built a dike of earth to hold it back. A makeshift dam.

It worked for a while. It breached and they patched it, more than once. And in April nineteen ninety-nine, a wind storm broke it apart. Two people died.

So they tried again, properly this time. Kazakhstan and the World Bank built a real dam. It's called the Kok-Aral. A thirteen-kilometer earth dike, with a concrete structure and gates to let water through when there's too much. It was finished in two thousand five.

The whole project, with the work along the river, cost about eighty-six million dollars. The World Bank loaned about sixty-four and a half million of it, and Kazakhstan paid the rest.

And the northern sea came back faster than almost anybody expected. By early two thousand six, the water was up and holding at about forty-two meters. The salt went down. The native fish swam back in from the river.

In two thousand five, the northern catch was six hundred ninety-five tons. By two thousand sixteen, it was six thousand. Today, Kazakhstan's water ministry says it's around eight thousand tons a year, with twenty-two fish species back.
