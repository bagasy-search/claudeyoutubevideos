Today that same vine is the most famous weed in America. You've heard what they call it. The vine that ate the South.

My name's Ruth Dalby. I grew up on a farm in Kansas, and I've spent my whole life reading soil. That's my kitchen table, buried in old government maps, and a jar with a bug in it. We'll get to the bug.

Every story on this channel is a true story. The sources are in the description, all of them.

Kudzu didn't start in Georgia. It started in Philadelphia, in eighteen seventy-six, at America's hundredth birthday party, the Centennial Exposition. Japan built a pavilion, and in it, as a pretty ornamental plant, was kudzu.

The newspapers said it grew a mile a minute. It doesn't. It's closer to a foot a day in a good summer. Which is still plenty, if it's your fence.

A few years later it showed up at the big exposition in New Orleans. That's how the South first met it. As a nice shade vine for a front porch.

Then, in the early nineteen hundreds, a couple named Charles and Lillie Pleas, in Chipley, Florida, noticed their cows and hogs would eat it. In nineteen oh five they opened a mail-order nursery and sold kudzu as the coming forage of the South. Forage, that's just feed for livestock.

And here's my favourite detail. The post office opened a fraud case against them, because nobody believed a plant could grow like they said. The investigators came, looked at the vine, and closed the case.

Even the government's fraud inspectors were sold. That should have been the first warning.

Around the same time, a government plant scientist in Washington named David Fairchild planted kudzu around his own place. He'd later write that it took root with a vengeance, climbed over the bushes, and smothered his pines. Getting rid of it cost him over two hundred dollars.

Remember that name. Fairchild. He comes back.

To understand why anybody would plant this thing on purpose, you have to understand what the Southern land looked like by then. And I'll be honest with you, this is the part that gets me.

For more than a hundred years, the Southern hill country was planted in corn, cotton and tobacco, over and over. The fields were plowed clean, bare dirt between the rows, every spring.

That country, they call it the Piedmont, the rolling land between the mountains and the coast, has soils that wash away easy. And the rain down there doesn't fall gentle.

Researchers estimate that land lost somewhere between five and ten inches of topsoil between seventeen hundred and nineteen seventy. Topsoil, that's the dark top layer, the only part of the ground that really grows anything.

Five to ten inches doesn't sound like much. Put a ruler next to your hand. That's the whole farm. Gone downhill into the creeks.

Whole hillsides were cut into gullies you could stand a man in. And then, in nineteen thirty and thirty-one, Georgia had the worst drought in its history. All of it on top of the Great Depression.

Worn-out land, broke farmers, men out of work. If you'd been standing in one of those gullies, with the bank calling, you might have grabbed anything green that promised to hold the dirt down. I know I would have.

Out in my part of the country, the problem was wind instead of rain. The Dust Bowl.

In nineteen thirty-three, the government set up an agency to fight soil erosion, run by a soil scientist named Hugh Hammond Bennett. And in the spring of nineteen thirty-five, a dust storm from the Plains rolled all the way to Washington, D.C.

The story goes that Bennett was testifying to senators that day, and he walked them to the window, pointed at the brown sky, and said, gentlemen, that is Kansas blowing by.

Whether he said it exactly like that, I can't promise you. But Congress passed the Soil Conservation Act without a single vote against it, and the President signed it on April twenty-seventh, nineteen thirty-five. Bennett's outfit was now the Soil Conservation Service.

And his people had already been testing a plant that held soil better than anything they'd seen. Kudzu.

At their station in Watkinsville, Georgia, land under kudzu had nearly eighty percent less water running off, and more than ninety-nine percent less soil washing away, than land under cotton.

Those numbers are real. They weren't stupid. Kudzu really does hold a bank together. Bennett himself asked, what, short of a miracle, can you call this plant?

So: a ruined South, a depression, a brand new agency, and a plant that really did stop the dirt from leaving. Here's where it goes wrong.

In nineteen thirty-five, the Soil Conservation Service launched a planting campaign across the South. They built nurseries in Virginia, North Carolina, Alabama and Georgia, and grew kudzu by the millions.

How many millions, the books don't agree. Seventy million, eighty-five million, one encyclopedia says a hundred million, between nineteen thirty-five and nineteen forty-two.

Take the middle number. Eighty-five million plants. The whole country had about a hundred twenty-seven million people back then. That's two kudzu plants for every three Americans alive. Babies included.

But Southern farmers weren't fools. Plenty of them looked at a vine that climbs anything that stands still and said, no thank you.

So the government sweetened it. Up to eight dollars for every acre you planted. In the Depression, that was real money for a farmer with nothing growing.

And young men in the government work camps, the Civilian Conservation Corps, planted kudzu all across the South. The government was paying people to plant it, and paying other people to plant it for them.

And what did the official paperwork say about the risk? One government bulletin told farmers there was no danger that kudzu will become a pest.

No danger. In writing. With a government seal on it.

Meanwhile, remember David Fairchild? In nineteen thirty-eight, right in the middle of all this planting, he published his memoir. It's called The World Was My Garden. And in it, he wrote about his own kudzu, and what it cost him to get rid of it.

So the warning was in a bookstore, in plain English, from one of the government's own plant men, while the government's nurseries were shipping that vine by the trainload. Nobody had to guess. They just had to read.

Here's what happened next, year by year.

Nineteen thirty-four. Before the big campaign, there were about ten thousand acres of kudzu in the whole country.

Nineteen forty-three. Channing Cope, a newspaper columnist and radio host from Covington, Georgia, starts the Kudzu Club of America.

Cope broadcast his farm show every morning from his own front porch. He sold what he called front porch farming. Plant kudzu, let the cows eat it, sit on your porch. His club had about twenty thousand members, and their goal was eight million acres.

They had festivals. They crowned a Kudzu Queen. One magazine called kudzu cash on the vine. It was a craze, like a hula hoop you couldn't put back in the closet.

By the mid nineteen forties, depending on which report you believe, somewhere between half a million and three million acres had been planted on Southern farms. A lot of those plantings died off. A lot didn't.

Nineteen fifty-three. The Department of Agriculture quietly takes kudzu off the list of cover plants it would pay farmers to grow. Eighteen years after it started paying them.

Nineteen sixty-two. The Soil Conservation Service tells landowners to plant it only far away from houses and developed land. That same year, Channing Cope, the father of kudzu, dies of a heart attack.

By then the complaints were piling up. Railroads, because the vine grew over the tracks. Power companies, because it climbed the poles. Highway crews, because it swallowed the road signs. Foresters, because it pulled down young trees.

Then, around nineteen seventy, the very same Department of Agriculture that paid farmers to plant it listed kudzu as a common weed. Some sources say nineteen seventy-two. Either way, it took them about thirty-five years to change their mind.

One government forester put it pretty well. He said kudzu was of no value other than keeping things green.

And by nineteen seventy-six, kudzu had grown over most of Channing Cope's old farm. Including the house, and the barns.

Nineteen ninety-seven. Congress puts kudzu on the federal noxious weed list. Later it came off again. Today it's a noxious weed in thirteen states.

So, recap. The government paid people to plant it, then stopped paying, then called it a weed, then a noxious weed. The vine never changed. Only the paperwork did.

Now here's where the story you know goes off the rails. Somewhere along the way, kudzu stopped being a plant and turned into a legend.

You've heard the numbers. Kudzu covers seven million acres. Kudzu eats a hundred fifty thousand new acres every year.

Then the Forest Service actually went and measured, with survey plots all across the Southern woods. In twenty ten, they found kudzu on about two hundred twenty-seven thousand acres of forest, in twelve Southern states. And it's spreading by about two thousand five hundred acres a year. That's sixty times smaller than the scary number.

Forest Service people figure there's maybe another half million acres in cities and suburbs. Fine. Add it in. You're still nowhere near seven million.

So why does everybody believe it ate the South? A naturalist named Bill Finch made the point. Kudzu loves sun. It grows best on the edges, along roads, railroad cuts, the edge of a field. Exactly where you are when you look out a car window.

You drive past a wall of green on the highway, and you figure the whole forest behind it looks like that. It doesn't. It's mostly a roadside show.

So kudzu didn't eat the South. Good. Now let me tell you why the real story is worse.

First, the dirt. Kudzu took the blame for land that was already ruined. The gullies, the washed-out hillsides, the topsoil sitting at the bottom of the creeks. That was the disaster. Kudzu was the bandage they slapped on it.

And when people look at those old gullies today, covered in green, they don't think, this is where the farm washed away. They think, look what that terrible vine did. The vine gets the blame, and a hundred years of bad farming walk away clean.

Second, the woods. While everybody was writing poems about kudzu, other plants from overseas were quietly moving into the Southern forest.

That same Forest Service survey found Japanese honeysuckle on about ten point three million acres of Southern forest, spreading by about sixty-five thousand acres a year.

That's about forty-five times more forest than kudzu, spreading about twenty-six times faster. Foresters also name a shrub called Chinese privet as a bigger headache than kudzu.

Ever heard a song about honeysuckle eating the South? No. It smells nice and it doesn't climb billboards. So it got a free pass. Everybody watches the front door, and the real burglar walks in the back.

Third, the bug. And yes, this is the one in my jar.

In the fall of two thousand nine, somebody found a little insect from Asia living on kudzu near Atlanta. About the size of a pea. They named it the kudzu bug.

At first it stayed on the kudzu. Then it found the soybeans. Soybeans are in the same plant family, and the bug likes them just fine.

In Georgia field trials from twenty ten to twenty twelve, soybean fields that weren't sprayed lost about twenty percent of their crop on average. One trial lost about seventy-seven percent.

One bushel in five, gone. In a bad field, three out of four. So the farmer pays again. For spray, this time. The vine the government planted to save the farms ended up feeding the bug that eats the farmer's beans.

Fourth, the air. In twenty ten, a team of scientists published a study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. They compared soil with kudzu and without it in Madison County, Georgia.

Kudzu pulls nitrogen out of the air and puts it in the soil. Sounds good. But too much, and the soil starts giving off a gas called nitric oxide. That gas helps make ozone, the stuff in smog that's hard on your lungs.

In midsummer, the kudzu soil gave off a hundred twenty-seven percent more of that gas. More than double. And in their worst-case computer model, parts of the region got about seven extra high-ozone days a year. The scientists called that scenario extreme. But it's not nothing, if you've got asthma.

And fifth, the money. A study for Congress in nineteen ninety-three put kudzu's damage at about fifty million dollars a year in lost farm and timber production.

So who's paying? The farmer, for the spray. The power company, which passes it on in your bill. The road crews, paid with your taxes. The same taxpayers who paid eight dollars an acre to plant it in the first place.

You paid for it going in, and you're paying for it coming out. That's what I call a government program with a long tail.

So what actually works? I'll give you the honest version.

Getting rid of kudzu across the whole country isn't going to happen. The extension scientists at Auburn University say so in writing: you can control it on your land, but wiping it out of the United States is not possible right now.

The root is the problem. A big kudzu root can go down about seven feet and weigh four or five hundred pounds. Cut the vine, and it comes right back.

What does work, on your own place, is being stubborn. Keep stripping the leaves off, over and over, for three or four years, and you starve the root.

And the cheapest way to strip leaves? Goats. Goats and sheep will eat kudzu down to the stems and come back for more. It's the one solution in this whole story that doesn't need a government check.

And the real fix, the one nobody puts on a postcard, is the soil. Don't strip the land bare. Keep something living on it. Don't wait for a miracle plant to fix what bad farming broke.

I'll give the Soil Conservation Service its due. They were right that the soil was the emergency. They were just in a hurry, and they were paying, and when you're paying, nobody wants to hear the bad news.
