On Sunday, April fourteenth, nineteen thirty-five, it was a nice spring afternoon in western Kansas. Clear sky. Warm. People were out after church.

And then, around four o'clock, the birds started flying south. Fast. Thousands of them. And behind the birds came a wall. A black wall of dirt, maybe a thousand feet high, rolling across the prairie like the end of the world.

When it hit, people couldn't see their own hand in front of their face. In the middle of the afternoon. They called it Black Sunday.

That dirt was the Great Plains. Our topsoil. The best grass country on God's earth, picked up and thrown across half the nation.

And here's what nobody wants to say out loud. It didn't just happen. Somebody told the farmers to plow it up. The railroads told them. The land men told them. And the federal government told them, and paid them to do it.

Plant wheat, they said. Wheat will win the war. Rain follows the plow. And when the sky turned black for the better part of ten years, that same government came back and said the farmers did it to themselves.

Today I'm putting the whole thing on my kitchen table. Who sold the plains, who paid for the plowing, who warned them, and who paid when the bill came. And at the end, the part that still makes me mad, because it's still going on.

My name's Ruth Dalby. I grew up on a farm in Kansas. This one isn't a story I read about. This is my grandparents' dirt we're talking about. That's my kitchen table, covered in old maps, and a jar of Kansas dirt my grandmother kept on a shelf. We'll get to the jar.

Every story on this channel is a true story. The sources are in the description, all of them.

Let me start with what was there before the plow. Because you can't understand what was lost if you don't know what it was.

The southern Great Plains, western Kansas, the Oklahoma panhandle, the Texas panhandle, eastern Colorado, was grass. Short grass. Buffalo grass and blue grama, mostly. Not pretty. Not tall. But tough.

That grass had roots that went down deep and wove the ground together like a rug. It could take a drought. It could take wind. It had been taking both for thousands of years.

Because that's the thing about the plains. It's dry. Not always. But often. It goes through wet years and dry years, and the dry years can last a long time. Anybody who's ranched out there will tell you. The grass knew that. The people coming in didn't.

In eighteen sixty-two, Congress passed the Homestead Act. A hundred sixty acres of free land, if you'd live on it and farm it for five years.

A hundred sixty acres is a fine farm in Ohio. In western Kansas, it's barely enough to starve on. So for a long time, a lot of the high plains stayed in grass, and cattle.

And then somebody came up with an idea that's one of my favourite pieces of nonsense in American history.

Rain follows the plow.

The idea was, if you break the sod and farm the land, the climate itself will change. More plowing, more rain. Some of the men pushing it were real scientists, for their day. One booster, a land promoter named Charles Dana Wilber, wrote it down in plain words in eighteen eighty-one. The rain, he said, follows the plow.

And you know what? For a while, it looked like it was true. The plains had a run of wet years. Crops came up. Towns went in. The railroads, which had land to sell, printed brochures with that idea all over them.

Then the dry years came back, like they always do. And a lot of those people went broke and left.

But the idea didn't die. It just waited.

In nineteen oh four, Congress passed the Kinkaid Act, for western Nebraska. Six hundred forty acres. And in nineteen oh nine, the Enlarged Homestead Act, three hundred twenty acres, for the dry country. Land nobody could irrigate.

Three hundred twenty acres, free, for dry farming. Your government was telling you, in law, that this land was fit to farm. That's how a lot of families read it. Why else would they give it away?

And people came. Tens of thousands of them. Into country that had been grass since before anybody kept count.

Then came the war. And this is where it really took off.

When the First World War started in Europe, the wheat fields over there turned into battlefields. Somebody had to feed the armies, and feed Europe. That somebody was the American farmer.

In nineteen thirteen, wheat sold for about eighty-seven cents a bushel. By the time America entered the war in nineteen seventeen, it was over two dollars.

And then the government did something it had never done before. It set the price. In nineteen seventeen, President Wilson set the price of wheat at two dollars and twenty cents a bushel, and the government stood ready to buy the crop to hold it there.

The posters said, food will win the war. The message to the farmer was simple. Plant every acre you can. It's your patriotic duty. And Uncle Sam guarantees the price.

You tell a farmer the price is guaranteed and it's patriotic, what do you think he does? He plows.

And he had something new to plow with. The tractor. With a team of horses, a man could barely turn three acres of tough prairie sod in a day. With a tractor, he could do fifty. Then came the one-way disc plow, which cut and turned the ground fast, and left it fine and loose.

Fine and loose. Remember that. That's the problem in two words.

Then the war ended. And in nineteen twenty, before the harvest, the government took the price guarantee away. That summer wheat fell by about half.

Now, here's where a sensible person would say, well, the boom is over, let's go back to grass. That's not what happened.

What happened is, the farmers had borrowed money. For land. For tractors. For seed. The bank still wanted its payment. And when the price per bushel falls by half, there's only one way to make the same payment. Grow twice as many bushels.

So they plowed more.

I want you to sit with that for a second, because it's the whole trap. When prices were high, they plowed to get rich. When prices were low, they plowed to keep from going under. Either way, the answer was more plowing.

Between nineteen oh nine and nineteen twenty-nine, farmers on the Great Plains broke something like thirty-two million acres of sod.

In just the five years from nineteen twenty-five to nineteen thirty, on the southern plains alone, another five million acres of native grass went under the plow.

In some counties, wheat acreage went up four hundred percent. In some, a thousand percent.

They called it the Great Plow-Up. Grass that had held that country together for thousands of years, turned upside down in about twenty.

And it worked, for a few years. The late twenties were wet. Nineteen twenty-six and nineteen thirty-one had huge wheat crops. Men who'd come out with nothing were buying cars.

But a huge crop, everywhere at once, does one thing. It crashes the price.

By nineteen thirty-one, wheat was down around a quarter a bushel. Less than it cost to grow. Some farmers left it standing in the field, because it wasn't worth the gas to cut it.

And that summer, nineteen thirty-one, the rain stopped.

Now picture the land. Millions of acres, the grass gone, the roots gone, the dirt pulverized fine by the disc plow. Some of it planted, some of it abandoned. Nothing holding it down.

And on the plains, the wind always blows.

The first big dust storms came in nineteen thirty-two. Fourteen of them that year, by the weather bureau's count. Thirty-eight the next year. They called them black blizzards.

I've heard the stories my whole life. Wet sheets hung over the windows, and the dust coming through anyway. Lamps lit at noon. Kids sent to school with wet rags over their mouths. Static electricity so strong it could short out a car, and men dragging chains behind their cars to ground them.

People swept dirt out of their houses with a shovel. Cattle went blind. Some died with their stomachs full of dirt. Jackrabbits came down out of the dead fields by the thousands, looking for anything green.

My grandmother kept a jar of that dirt on a shelf, all her life. When I asked her why, she said, so I don't forget what we did to it. Not what it did to us. What we did to it.

And then, in May of nineteen thirty-four, the dust went east.

On May eleventh, a storm picked up the plains and carried it all the way across the country. Dust fell on Chicago. It fell on New York and on Washington. Ships three hundred miles out in the Atlantic Ocean had plains dirt settling on their decks.

The New York Times wrote that the cloud would weigh three hundred million tons.

All of a sudden, the people in Washington didn't need a report to know about the Dust Bowl. It was on their windowsills.

And then came that Sunday in April of nineteen thirty-five. Black Sunday.

That storm came down out of the Dakotas and Nebraska and rolled south through Kansas, Oklahoma and Texas. The writer Timothy Egan says it carried twice as much dirt as was dug out to build the Panama Canal. The canal took seven years to dig. The storm did it in an afternoon.

The next day, a reporter from the Associated Press, writing about that storm, used two words. Dust bowl. And the name stuck.

Now, the government's response. And I want to be fair here, because some of it was good.

There was a soil scientist named Hugh Hammond Bennett. He'd been warning about soil erosion for years. Most people thought he was a crank.

In the spring of nineteen thirty-five, Bennett was in Washington, asking Congress for a permanent agency to fight erosion. And the story goes that, during the hearings, a dust cloud from the plains came over the capital and darkened the sky. And Bennett walked the men to the window, and showed them.

Some say he knew it was coming, and timed it. Whether it happened exactly like the story says, I can't promise you. But Congress passed the Soil Conservation Act, and on April twenty-seventh, nineteen thirty-five, the President signed it. Bennett ran the new Soil Conservation Service for the next sixteen years.

And those people did real work. They taught contour plowing, following the shape of the land instead of plowing in straight lines. They taught strip farming, and cover crops, and listing the ground to catch the dirt.

The government planted trees, too. The Shelterbelt project. A wall of windbreaks running down the plains from Canada to Texas. Over the next several years, they planted around two hundred twenty million trees. Some of those rows are still standing. I drive past one every week.

So I'll give them that. When the dirt was in their own windows, they moved.

But here's the part that gets me.

In nineteen thirty-six, the government made a movie about it. A real one, made by the government, called The Plow That Broke the Plains. Some of the footage you've been seeing comes from films like that.

And in that movie, and in the official reports that came out the same year, the government told the story of what went wrong. A committee of experts said the basic cause was trying to force a kind of farming onto the plains that the plains weren't made for.

They were right about that. But listen to how the story got told. The farmers broke the plains. The farmers plowed too much. The farmers were greedy.

Not a lot of words about who wrote the Homestead Acts. Who gave away three hundred twenty acres of dry country and said, go farm it. Who set the price of wheat at two dollars and twenty cents and said, it's your patriotic duty. Who pulled the price out from under them in nineteen twenty, with the loans still on the books.

The farmers did plow it. Nobody's saying they didn't. My own people plowed it. But they were doing exactly what they'd been told and paid to do.

And here's the part that'll make you laugh, if you don't cry. In nineteen thirty-three, the new farm program in Washington started paying farmers to plant less. To take land out of production.

So for ten years, they'd been told plant more. And now they were being paid to plant less. Same government. Same land. Same farmers.

How long did it last? The drought hung on through most of the nineteen thirties. The worst years were the middle of the decade. In the fall of nineteen thirty-nine, the rains finally came back in earnest.

Ten years, more or less. A whole childhood. My grandmother was eight when it started, and she was a grown woman when it ended.

By some estimates, about two and a half million people left the plains states during the thirties. Some went to California, and you've heard those stories. A lot more just went to the next town over, or the next state, and started again.

And the ones who stayed, like my family, held on by their fingernails. Some of them died of what the doctors called dust pneumonia. Old people, and little ones, mostly. I've seen the graves.

So let's add it up. The government gave away land that should never have been plowed. Then it paid a guaranteed price to plow it. Then it pulled the price. Then the farmers plowed even more to pay the bank. Then the rain stopped and the wind came. And then the government made a movie explaining that the farmers did it.

Now I want to bring it up to today. Because you might think, well, that was ninety years ago, we've learned our lesson.

We did learn some of it. Out here we've got the conservation programs. Since nineteen eighty-five, the government's paid farmers to put the worst land back in grass, through what they call the Conservation Reserve Program. A lot of the most fragile ground in Kansas is back in grass because of it. That's good.

But those are contracts. When they run out, and the price of corn or wheat goes up, a lot of that grass goes right back under the plow. Every year, somewhere on the plains, native grass that's never been broken is still getting turned over. When the price is right, and the program pays, it happens again.

And when the dry years come back, and out here they always come back, you can see the dirt start to move. I've seen it in my own lifetime. Not like the thirties. But enough to make an old woman nervous.

So here's the lesson, from a farm woman who's watched a lot of government programs come and go. The government can set the price of wheat. It can't set the price of rain.

And when somebody in an office tells you the rules of the land have changed, because the market changed, or because the program changed, go stand in the grass and look at the sky. The land never got the memo.
