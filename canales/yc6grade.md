It's a Tuesday morning in September, 1962. You're eleven years old. The radiator is clanking, the windows are open a crack, and the whole room smells like pencil shavings and floor wax. Your teacher walks down the rows and sets a test face down on every desk.

This is not a made-up quiz. Every question you're about to see is built from real American textbooks and tests of the 1950s and 1960s, and many of them are printed here word for word. Arithmetic books, spellers, history readers, the same pages millions of eleven-year-olds worked through with a number two pencil.

Here are the rules. Twenty-five questions. After each one, you get five seconds, just like the clock on the wall. Keep count of how many you get right, and at the end, we'll grade you the way a 1962 teacher would.

And fair warning. The early questions are easy. Question twenty-one comes straight out of a 1956 arithmetic book, and it stops a lot of grown-ups cold.

Welcome to Yesterday's Classroom. Turn your paper over. Let's begin.

Question one. You buy four three-cent stamps and two six-cent air mail stamps. How much change should you get from a dollar bill? Is it A, seventy-six cents? B, sixty-four cents? Or C, eighty-two cents?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is A, seventy-six cents. Four three-cent stamps and two six-cent stamps come to twenty-four cents. And those were real prices. A letter cost three cents until the summer of 1958, and air mail was six. Arithmetic books set their problems at the post office, the grocery and the filling station, because an eleven-year-old was expected to count the change for his parents.

Question two. In 1962, which was the newest of the fifty states? Is it A, Alaska? B, Puerto Rico? Or C, Hawaii?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is C, Hawaii. It became the fiftieth state in August of 1959, just seven months after Alaska. Every classroom map and every flag had to be replaced. The forty-nine star flag flew for only one year. The kids in this classroom were the first to learn fifty states as a settled fact.

Question three. The first ten amendments to the Constitution are called what? Is it A, the Articles of Confederation? B, the Bill of Rights? Or C, the Declaration of Independence?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is B, the Bill of Rights. In the middle of the Cold War, civics teachers taught it as the line between America and the Soviet Union, the list of freedoms no government was allowed to take away. The 1965 textbook put it in one plain sentence, and every sixth grader was expected to know it.

Question four. Spelling. Which is the correct spelling of the word for a man-made object that circles the Earth? Is it A, S A T T E L I T E? B, S A T E L I T E? Or C, S A T E L L I T E?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is C. One T, two L's. After Sputnik went up in October of 1957, space words landed on spelling lists almost overnight. The 1964 sixth grade speller told teachers to point out the single T and the double L in satellite, right next to astronaut, rocket and countdown.

Question five. In the Basic Four food guide taught in 1960s classrooms, the groups were Milk, Meat, Bread and Cereal, and which other group? Is it A, Vegetable and Fruit? B, Sweets? Or C, Fats and Oils?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is A, Vegetable and Fruit. The government's Basic Four replaced the old wartime Basic Seven in the late fifties, and it hung on classroom walls until 1979. The guide said teenagers should drink four or more cups of milk every single day.

Question six. Russell's father bought twelve and a half gallons of gasoline at thirty-one point two cents a gallon. How much change should he get from five dollars? Is it A, a dollar ninety? B, a dollar ten? Or C, eighty-eight cents?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is B, a dollar ten. The gas came to three dollars and ninety cents. And yes, gas really cost about thirty cents a gallon in the fifties, with that tenth of a cent painted right on the pump. This problem is word for word from a 1956 sixth grade arithmetic book. Multiplying decimals with money was a sixth grade skill, and a child might check the sum for Dad at the pump.

Question seven. In 1954, Congress added two words to the Pledge of Allegiance. Which two words? Is it A, one nation? B, with liberty? Or C, under God?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is C, under God. President Eisenhower signed the change on Flag Day, June fourteenth, 1954. Kids who started school before that learned one pledge in first grade and had to relearn it partway through. By 1956, school guides were printing the new version on their very first page.

Question eight. Fill in the blank. On our class trip we visited the blank, the building in which Congress meets. Is it A, Capitol with an O? B, capital with an A? Or C, capitle, with an L E?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is A, Capitol with an O. The capital with an A is the city. The Capitol with an O is the building. That exact sentence comes from the homonym unit of a 1964 sixth grade speller, which paired capital with Capitol and principal with principle, the traps that caught more kids than any other words.

Time for a quick check. Count your right answers. If you have seven or eight, you're sitting at the top of the class. Five or six is a solid start. Keep counting, because the next eight get harder.

Question nine. Rubber trees grew wild along which great South American river, before their seeds were carried to plantations in Asia? Is it A, the Orinoco? B, the Amazon? Or C, the Parana?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is B, the Amazon. In many schools, sixth grade social studies meant Latin America and Canada. Rubber was a favorite lesson, because the tires on the family car traced all the way back to the Amazon jungle.

Question ten. On February twentieth, 1962, who became the first American to orbit the Earth? Is it A, Alan Shepard? B, Yuri Gagarin? Or C, John Glenn?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is C, John Glenn, in Friendship 7. Schools wheeled television sets into classrooms so children could watch the launch. Alan Shepard had flown the year before but never went into orbit, and Gagarin was a Soviet cosmonaut. Two years later, Glenn's flight was already a reading passage in the sixth grade speller.

Question eleven. Mr. Jackson drove twelve thousand, nine hundred and three miles in eleven months. On average, how many miles did he drive each month? Is it A, one thousand one hundred seventy-three? B, one thousand one hundred thirty-seven? Or C, one thousand two hundred seventy-three?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is A, eleven seventy-three. It divides out exactly. This problem comes from a real 1963 chapter test. Long division without a calculator was the heart of sixth grade arithmetic, and you were expected to check every answer by multiplying it back.

Question twelve. When you diagram a sentence, the vertical line that cuts all the way through the base line separates what? Is it A, the verb from the direct object? B, the subject from the predicate? Or C, an adjective from its noun?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is B, the subject from the predicate. The line for a direct object stops at the base line and never crosses it. Sentence diagramming, invented back in the 1870s, filled hours of chalkboard time in 1960s classrooms, and plenty of people watching this can still draw one from memory.

Question thirteen. A bushel holds four pecks. What fraction of a bushel is three pecks? Is it A, one third? B, three eighths? Or C, three quarters?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is C, three quarters. Every arithmetic book printed the dry measure table. Two pints make a quart, eight quarts make a peck, four pecks make a bushel. Families bought apples and potatoes by the peck at roadside stands, so this was not abstract. It was Saturday shopping.

Question fourteen. In 1867, the United States bought Alaska, which critics mocked as Seward's Icebox. Which country sold it? Is it A, Russia? B, Great Britain? Or C, Spain?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is A, Russia, for about seven point two million dollars. With Alaska freshly made a state in 1959, the foolish purchase that paid off was a favorite story. Some senators laughed at it, right up until the gold and the fisheries proved them wrong.

Question fifteen. In 1962, how many planets did sixth graders learn were in our solar system? Is it A, eight? B, nine? Or C, ten?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is B, nine. Pluto was discovered in 1930, so every child of the fifties and sixties learned nine planets. A 1956 sixth grade arithmetic book had students round off Pluto's diameter at three thousand six hundred miles. The real number is closer to fourteen hundred. And in 2006, Pluto was demoted to a dwarf planet.

Question sixteen. There are sixteen and a half feet in one rod. How many feet are in four rods? Is it A, sixty-four feet? B, forty-eight feet? Or C, sixty-six feet?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is C, sixty-six feet, which is exactly one surveyor's chain. American farmland was measured in rods and chains. Fences, property lines and country roads were all laid out this way, and a farm kid needed to know it to read a deed.

Second report card. Count them up. If you've got thirteen or more out of sixteen, you'd be a straight A student in 1962. Ten to twelve is a good solid B. And now we're heading into the questions that separate the class.

Question seventeen. Ceylon, the island 1960s schoolchildren knew for its tea, is known today by what name? Is it A, Sri Lanka? B, Myanmar? Or C, Taiwan?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is A, Sri Lanka. It changed its name in 1972. And here's the trick. All three choices were on the same classroom map under different names. Myanmar was Burma, and Taiwan was called Formosa.

Question eighteen. In the sentence, Mr. Albertson, our neighbor, bought forty acres, what is the phrase our neighbor? Is it A, a direct object? B, an appositive? Or C, a predicate nominative?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is B, an appositive. It renames the noun right next to it, and it's set off by commas. Leaving out those commas was one of the mistakes teachers marked in red ink more than almost any other.

Question nineteen. On the day John Glenn orbited the Earth in February of 1962, how many amendments did the Constitution have? Is it A, twenty-two? B, twenty-seven? Or C, twenty-three?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is C, twenty-three. The twenty-third amendment was ratified in March of 1961. The twenty-fourth wouldn't come until 1964. The answer kept changing all through those years, so a child who learned twenty-two in fifth grade could be marked wrong in sixth.

Question twenty. Which word correctly completes the sentence? The old dog has blank on the porch all morning. Is it A, lain? B, laid? Or C, layed?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is A, lain. Lie, lay, lain means to rest. Lay, laid, laid means to put something down. And layed isn't a word at all. Sixth graders memorized these principal parts like multiplication tables, and lie versus lay is still tripping people up sixty years later.

Question twenty-one. Here it is, the one from the 1956 arithmetic book. Mr. Albertson bought a piece of land eighty rods by eighty rods. How many acres did he buy? Is it A, sixty-four acres? B, forty acres? Or C, one hundred sixty acres?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is B, forty acres. Eighty times eighty is sixty-four hundred square rods, and there are one hundred sixty square rods in an acre. Anyone who grew up in farm country knew that table, because land was sold in forty, eighty and one hundred sixty acre pieces. The classic forty is exactly eighty rods on each side.

Question twenty-two. Which state in the lower forty-eight reaches farthest north? Is it A, Maine? B, Washington? Or C, Minnesota?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is C, Minnesota. A little notch called the Northwest Angle, on the Lake of the Woods, pokes above the forty-ninth parallel. When high school seniors near Boston were tested in 1948, only about one in three got this right. Common wall maps made New England look farther north than it really is.

Question twenty-three. In which month is the Earth closest to the Sun? Is it A, January? B, July? Or C, the distance never changes?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is A, January. It surprises almost everyone. Our seasons come from the tilt of the Earth, not the distance. On that same 1948 test, fewer than half of the high school seniors knew it.

Question twenty-four. Thomas Jefferson's name does not appear among the signers of the Constitution. Why not? Is it A, he refused to sign in protest? B, he was in France serving as the American minister? Or C, he was already President and did not need to sign?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is B. Jefferson was in Paris as the American minister to France while the Constitution was being written in 1787. A 1965 sixth grade textbook sent students to find that out for themselves. And there was no President at all until George Washington took office in 1789.

Question twenty-five. The last one. If a pilot flies due south from Detroit, Michigan, what is the first foreign country he will fly over? Is it A, Mexico? B, Cuba? Or C, Canada?
[[PAUSE]]
The answer is C, Canada. Windsor, Ontario sits directly south of downtown Detroit, right across the river. On that 1948 test, fewer than one in five high school seniors got it right.

Pencils down. Here's how a 1962 teacher would grade you. Twenty-three to twenty-five, an A, and a gold star on your paper. Eighteen to twenty-two, a B, very good work. Twelve to seventeen, a C, and a note to practice your rods and pecks. And under twelve, well, that meant a note home and a talk after class.
