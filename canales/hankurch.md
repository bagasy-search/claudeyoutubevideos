[[H1]] Alright. I'm up on the Mendocino Headlands in Northern California, and I want you to look down into that cove. Ten years ago, there was a forest down there. Now it's bare rock. And it's purple. [[/H]]

That purple is sea urchins. More than you could ever count. Little spiky balls, some as big as a tennis ball, covering the bottom of the ocean like a carpet.

And California went to war with them. The state's ocean council paid out-of-work divers to pull them off the reefs. Down south, polluters' money has paid divers to crush them since 2013. Up here, volunteers went down with hammers and did it for free.

All together, divers have killed an estimated seven to eight million sea urchins off California.

So did it work? What happened to the kelp is not what anybody expected. And the reason this whole mess started turned out to be a mystery it took scientists twelve years to solve.

[[H2]] I'm Hank. I'm not a marine biologist. I'm just a guy from Louisiana with an old truck and way too many questions. And when I heard California was paying people to smash sea urchins, I had to drive out here and see it. [[/H]]

To understand this story, you have to know what used to be under this water.

Along this coast, there was a forest. Bull kelp. A giant seaweed that grows from the rocky bottom all the way up to the surface, with a big round float on top and long ribbons spreading out on the water. It can grow as much as two feet in a day. It can reach sixty feet tall in a few months.

That forest was home to everything. Rockfish. Red abalone. Red sea urchins that fishermen sold to sushi restaurants. It was one of the richest places in the Pacific.

And the forest had a bodyguard. The sunflower sea star. The biggest sea star in the world. More than three feet across, with up to twenty-four arms, and it can move about three feet a minute. And what does a sunflower sea star love to eat? Sea urchins.

Then, in 2013, the sea stars started to melt.

It was a disease. Their arms twisted up and fell off. Their bodies turned to mush in about a week. It spread from Mexico all the way to Alaska. More than five billion sunflower sea stars died. In California, more than ninety-nine percent of them were gone. The species is now listed as critically endangered.

And in 2014, a second hit came. A huge patch of warm water parked itself off the West Coast. Scientists called it the Blob. In some spots, the ocean ran about seven degrees warmer than normal. Warm water carries fewer nutrients, and the kelp got weak.

So the bodyguard was dead, and the forest was sick. And the urchins moved in.

According to a study by scientists at UC Davis, purple sea urchins on this coast went up about sixty times in a single year, in 2015. And they changed how they behave. Normally, urchins hide in the cracks and eat bits of kelp that drift by. But when the food ran out, they came out in the open and started eating the living kelp. Scientists watched dozens of them crawling up a single stalk and taking it down.

Bull kelp along more than two hundred miles of this coast dropped by more than ninety percent. A Stanford scientist said, this is basically the equivalent of losing the rainforest. Except we don't see it.

And there was one more guard missing up here. Sea otters. Otters eat urchins. Some otters eat so many purple urchins, their teeth and bones actually turn purple. But otters were wiped out on this coast by the fur trade, a long time ago. Today, California's sea otters live farther south, from about San Mateo County down to Santa Barbara County. About three thousand of them. A federal study in 2022 said bringing otters back up here is feasible. But it hasn't happened.

And here's the creepy part. When there's nothing left to eat, the urchins don't die. They just wait. A diver with Reef Check called them zombies. They can last a long time without eating, she said, they just live.

[[H3]] This is Noyo Harbor, in Fort Bragg. Look at this place. This used to be a busy urchin town. Boats, divers, dock crews. Now look at it. [[/H]]

When the kelp died, the red urchins that fishermen sell starved too. They turned empty inside, worth nothing. The North Coast red urchin fishery had been worth about three million dollars a year. It collapsed. The federal government declared a fishery disaster.

The red abalone went next. A beloved fishery, around thirty-one thousand divers on this coast. About eighty to eighty-five percent of the abalone died. The state closed it in 2018. In December 2025, they extended the closure again, all the way to 2036. An abalone needs about twelve years to grow big enough to take.

A diver named Grant Downie told a reporter, since this whole disaster, I'm hardly making a quarter of what I used to.

So what do you do with a coast full of zombie urchins, and a harbor full of divers with no work?

[[H4]] Well, somebody had a pretty simple idea. Pay the urchin divers to go get the urchins. [[/H]]

In 2018, local donors and county fish commissions raised about a hundred and thirty thousand dollars and hired nine commercial boats. The organizers said they pulled up about one point two million urchins that year and sent them to be composted.

In 2020, the state got involved. California's Ocean Protection Council paid about six hundred and seventeen thousand dollars through a nonprofit called Reef Check to hire sixteen commercial urchin divers. Five to seven hundred dollars a day to clear the reefs.

Some of them strapped rakes to their hands, like Wolverine, and filled mesh bags the size of a small car. Here at Noyo Bay, they pulled out more than forty-five thousand pounds of urchins.

And the kelp came back. Grant Downie remembered the first time he swam over the cleared patch. He said, I kind of freaked out. I'm at the site, and I'm driving over kelp.

It came back to about twenty percent of what used to grow there. And then the money ran out after two years.

Grant Downie didn't quit. He designed his own urchin traps, baited with kelp that washes up on the beach. With The Nature Conservancy, they've trapped more than twenty-three thousand purple urchins. His record for one trap is more than three hundred.

And a little way down the coast, at Albion Cove, divers kept clearing, and researchers tried something new. For the first time in California, they watched bull kelp grow from seed all the way to an adult on ropes set out on the open coast. Some of the urchins pulled out of that cove were even given to a studio that uses them to dye cloth.

[[H5]] Now this spot is famous. Caspar Cove. This is where regular people came with hammers. And I mean regular people. Weekend divers, fishermen, folks from town. [[/H]]

In February 2020, the state made a special rule for this one cove. Anybody with a fishing license could take unlimited purple urchins, by hand or with a hand tool. Divers started going down that summer, with rock hammers strapped to their wrists. They crushed the urchins right there on the bottom.

The water is about fifty-two degrees. There's surge. You come up with spines in your hands and your knees.

By the summer of 2023, a hundred and ten volunteer divers had made two hundred and forty-one dives and smashed an estimated hundred and thirty thousand urchins. Clearing one single acre took three years.

And the kelp came back where they hammered. A retired state official who dove the cove in 2024 said, this is the most kelp I've seen here since 2014. I wasn't sure that would happen.

The state extended the Caspar Cove rule through 2029.

[[H6]] Down here in Monterey, they ran the same experiment, and this time the scientists measured everything. [[/H]]

At a spot called Tanker Reef, a hundred and eighty-seven volunteer divers made more than thirteen hundred dives. They crushed an estimated six hundred and thirty-three thousand urchins.

In the cleared patch, urchins dropped from about seven per square meter to less than two. Giant kelp stalks went up about four times. In the patch next to it, where nobody culled, the kelp stayed at practically zero.

It wasn't a full forest yet. But it was a forest starting.

Then, in 2024, the state let the Tanker Reef rule expire. On purpose. They wanted to see if the kelp could hold on by itself. A year later, the kelp was still there.

And here's something that'll make you scratch your head. Parts of this bay are protected marine areas. And the rules protecting the wildlife protected the urchins too. The man who started the Tanker Reef project said those protected areas had turned into urchin barrens. Desolate places.

But the real millions happened somewhere else.

Down in Southern California, off the Palos Verdes peninsula near Los Angeles, a nonprofit called The Bay Foundation has been paying a team of commercial urchin divers since 2013. They crush the urchins in place. Over thirteen years, they report about five point eight million urchins and around eighty acres of kelp forest brought back. Urchins went from about thirty per square meter to about two. And the red urchins there, the ones fishermen sell, now have much bigger roe.

And who pays for it? Money from a pollution settlement. Decades ago, a chemical company dumped DDT off that coast. The settlement money is managed by federal and California agencies. And part of it now pays fishermen to crush urchins.

The head of the Bay Foundation put it simply. We were paying the fishermen to put back the forest, and then they could go back in and fish from there again.

[[H7]] This is Big River, at the mouth of Mendocino Bay. For years, this was the spot everybody pointed to. The kelp that never died. And then, in 2023, it did. [[/H]]

Big River had held on while almost everything else went bare. Then the urchins found it. In one year, more than ninety percent of the kelp there vanished. A Nature Conservancy scientist said they dove the site in 2023, and it was a straight up front of purple urchins.

So they threw everything at it. Commercial divers pulled out more than eighty-two thousand pounds of urchins from five acres. Then they fed the urchins that were left with lab-grown kelp, like throwing hay onto a pasture, so they'd leave the living plants alone. They even grew kelp on floating ropes.

By 2025, the kelp canopy at Big River was up more than nine hundred percent from the low point. The same scientist said, we're seeing so much kelp. Massive, massive amounts.

It cost about one point six million dollars.

And while all this was going on, scientists were still trying to answer the biggest question of all. What killed the sea stars?

For twelve years, nobody knew. In 2014, researchers blamed a virus. It turned out to be wrong. Healthy sea stars carried it too.

Then, in August 2025, a team of scientists from Canada and the United States published the answer in the journal Nature Ecology and Evolution. They exposed healthy sea stars to sick ones in the lab, and they looked at the fluid inside their bodies instead of their skin. There was basically one thing different. One of the lead scientists said, we all had chills.

The killer was a bacterium called Vibrio pectenicida. It had been hiding in plain sight, because it doesn't show up on the usual microscope slides. And here's the kicker. Its name means scallop killer. It was first discovered back in 1998, killing baby scallops in France.

One of the researchers called it the discovery of the decade.

Now that they know the killer, they can fight back. In December 2025, scientists put forty-eight baby sunflower sea stars, raised in a lab, into cages in Monterey Bay for a month. Forty-seven of them survived. They haven't released them for good yet. And a few wild ones have turned up. In December 2022, the crew of a commercial urchin boat spotted a sunflower sea star off Mendocino, the first one seen in these waters since 2014. So for the first time in over a decade, the bodyguard might be coming home.

The sunflower sea star still isn't protected under federal law, by the way. The government proposed listing it as threatened in 2023. As of this summer, there's still no final decision, and conservation groups have sued.

[[H8]] So here I am, back on the Headlands at sunset. Look at that. That cove has kelp floating in it. And the next one over is still bare rock. [[/H]]

Here's the honest scoreboard. Where people went down and fought for it, the kelp came back. A few acres here, a few acres there. But across the whole North Coast, it hasn't recovered. As of 2025, the state says bull kelp in Sonoma County is still down ninety-six percent, and in Mendocino, ninety-one percent. And another marine heat wave has already hit this coast.

They didn't save the ocean. They proved it can be saved. One cove, one hammer, one paycheck at a time. Now somebody has to pay for the other two hundred miles.

Not everybody agrees that we should. A Pomo man told a reporter, I think that humans are a big part of the kelp loss, and I think we just need to stay out of it. And one scientist reminded everybody that the urchins aren't invaders. They belong in the kelp forest as much as the kelp does. The problem was never the urchin. It was losing everything that kept it in check.
