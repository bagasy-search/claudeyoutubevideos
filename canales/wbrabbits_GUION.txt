In two thousand and fourteen, the Australian government declared Macquarie Island free of rabbits, rats and mice. The clean-up took about seven years, and it cost roughly twenty-five million Australian dollars.

The biggest blow came from the air. In May two thousand and eleven, poison bait was dropped over the island to reach about one hundred and fifty thousand rabbits.

This is Macquarie Island. It's in the Southern Ocean, south-east of Tasmania, and it belongs to Australia. It is reported to cover about one hundred and twenty-eight square kilometres of grass, tussock, cliffs and burrows. Seabirds nest here, in the burrows and on the cliffs.

Cats arrived around eighteen twenty. Rabbits came in eighteen seventy-eight. Nobody arriving on that island was thinking about a hundred years later.

Here's the thing about an island like this. It has very few animals to begin with. No big predators, no deer, no foxes. The plants and birds evolved without anything eating them from the ground. When a new animal shows up, the local life has no defence at all.

The cats were not the only predators to arrive. Rats came too, and they are reported to have preyed on the chicks and eggs of seabirds that were already in trouble. Some petrels are reported to have moved their nests out to offshore rock stacks, where the rats could not reach them. Think about what that means for a bird that spent its life on the main island.

A rabbit on an island like this is not a cute animal. It's a very efficient machine for eating grass and digging holes.

For a long time, the cats and the rabbits lived side by side, and the cats kept some of the rabbits in check. Then the cats were removed. And the rabbits, which had never really had to behave, went to work.

Nobody set out to turn an island into a lawn. It happened one burrow at a time, over years, while people were busy with other things.

Committees love a one-animal fix. It looks very tidy on a slide. Islands do not come in slides, and they do not come in single animals.

The rabbits didn't need a plan. They just needed the cats to be gone. Scientists call the chain reaction a trophic cascade. It means that when you take one animal out of a food chain, everything below it changes what it eats, where it hides, and whether it survives. Bergstrom's team says that's what happened on Macquarie, across the whole landscape, not in one corner of it.

Governments are excellent at paying for the second problem, once it arrives on time. The birds were not on any committee, and they still got the bill.

A risk plan with one line for the cats and no line for the rabbits is not a risk plan. It's a shopping list. And a shopping list is very good at getting you what you wanted, and very bad at telling you what it will cost.

Now the sources, because this is where people usually get sloppy. The main scientific paper on the rabbits came out in 2009, in the Journal of Applied Ecology. The lead author is Dana Bergstrom. Her team combined rabbit population numbers, plant surveys on the ground, and satellite pictures of the island.

Here is why the satellite pictures matter. A plot survey looks at small patches of ground, one at a time. Satellite images see the whole island at once. When the two agree, you stop arguing about one hillside and start arguing about the island. That's what Bergstrom's team did, which is why their claim is about the whole landscape and not a single patch.

What did they find? The island's plant cover changed a lot. In an interview, Dana Bergstrom estimated that close to forty percent of the island had been changed by the rabbits, and about twenty percent of it moderately to severely. Changed here means tall, complex vegetation was replaced by short grazed grass and bare dirt.

That number is the lead author's own estimate, and it is not a line in the paper's summary. Forty percent of one hundred and twenty-eight square kilometres is about fifty-one square kilometres. That's a lot of island to lose to lawn.

Not everybody agrees with the paper. In the same journal, a team led by Dowding is reported to have argued that the rabbit increase had several causes at once. They pointed to a virus used against rabbits earlier, to food that had grown back after decades of recovery, to the cats being gone, and to the weather. Bergstrom's team wrote back. They said the virus mattered a little, but the cats being gone mattered a lot.

So the scientists are arguing about how much blame goes where. They're not arguing about whether the rabbits did damage.

Pay attention to the year nineteen seventy-eight. By then, the rabbits had already been under management for ten years, and they still reached one hundred and thirty thousand. Managing a population is not the same as holding it down. If you're a farmer, a gardener, or anybody who has ever let a "helpful" animal loose, you already know this trap. Every removal is a trade, and the bill arrives later.

What I'd want you to hold onto from that is simple. Predators don't just eat animals. They also keep their prey from eating the place bare. Take the predator out, and the prey doesn't say thank you. It multiplies.

Twenty eleven, May. The government drops bait from the air. The reported target is about a hundred and fifty thousand rabbits, along with a huge number of rodents. That tells you how far the thing had spread.

Then the hunt for survivors. The reported team is twelve people with eleven hunting dogs and their handlers, and they spend more than a year searching the whole island, reported at about twelve thousand seven hundred hectares. Think about what that involves: walking the same rough ground for months, keeping the dogs on task, and checking every burrow. The people doing it were not glamorous. They were careful, and they had to be, because one rabbit left behind would have started the cycle again.

In my experience, the people who get eradication right are the ones who count before they cut. The rest count afterwards, and the counting is painful. Every rabbit that survives can breed back the whole population in a single season. So the margin for error is basically zero.

The cost of the whole project is about twenty-five million Australian dollars, paid by the Australian and Tasmanian governments.

That's a real win, and I want you to hear it clearly. People did something hard, and it worked.

Put the money next to the land and you can see the shape of the problem. Twenty-five million dollars across one hundred and twenty-eight square kilometres comes to about one hundred and ninety-five thousand Australian dollars for every square kilometre. That's what it cost to undo a decision made with good intentions.

Bergstrom's abstract puts the cost of the further conservation work at more than twenty-four million Australian dollars. The government's clean-up came in at about twenty-five million. Both figures point to the same bill, and somebody else paid it.

Was removing the cats worth it? The birds lost their cover, and the rabbits took it. The only reason that trade can be called a success is that the rabbits could be removed later. That's an uncomfortable answer, and I don't like giving it.

Now the part you can't put in a budget. Every burrow that collapsed, every chick that died in the open because it had no cover, every colony that stopped nesting on the main island. Nobody sends an invoice for those. They just go missing from the report.

Burrow-nesting petrels are a good example. A petrel nests underground. It needs grass and tussock to hide the burrow and hold the soil together. If the rabbits graze the grass down to the dirt, the burrows collapse and the rain washes the soil away. The bird didn't do anything wrong. It lost its roof.

So the birds that the cat removal was supposed to protect ended up with less cover, not more. That's the bitter part of this story. The people did the right thing for the wrong animal.

Look at the timing, too. The cats were a problem for a century, and the decision came down in a few years. Nobody on the island got a second century to fix it. Rabbits breed fast, so the rabbits had a head start on any plan made after the cats were gone. A decision that takes a few years to make can lock in a result that takes a generation to undo.

Every fix on an island is a bet about what the animals around the fix will do. The people in charge made a bet about the cats. They didn't make a serious bet about the rabbits. Bergstrom's paper argues that managers should have assessed those indirect effects before the cats were removed. I think that's a fair charge.

Nature doesn't read the minutes of your meeting.

A fair plan would have looked like this, in one sentence. Count everything that eats the grass before you remove the thing that eats the rabbits. It's not glamorous, and nobody gets a medal for it. But it would have saved a lot of birds.

And eradication is not the same as control. Control means you reduce a population. Eradication means you remove every single one. You can miss one rabbit and start the whole job over again.

So who pays today? Taxpayers first, through two Australian governments' budgets. Then the birds, whose recovery is slow and still going. Then the people who run the island, who still have to keep the rabbits, the rats and the mice from coming back on the next boat. Biosecurity is not a one-off job. It's a standing one, and it never gets a ribbon.

Now, what does the island look like today? This is the part I like best, and I'll tell you why. The island is recovering.

Surveys carried out in twenty twenty are reported to show strong recovery of native plants and wildlife. The same surveys are reported to find more native animals on the island now than at any point in the last hundred and fifty years. Burrow-nesting birds such as Antarctic prions and petrels are reported to have come back. Tussock grass is growing again, and it gives the chicks a place to hide.

But recovery is not the same as repair. The island has been changed, and some of those changes will last. Ecologists are reported to say it could take up to twenty years to settle into a new balance. And biosecurity never stops, because one stowaway on one boat can start the cycle again.

I don't want you to walk away thinking the answer is never remove anything. Some of those cats were doing real damage, and the birds needed protection. The mistake wasn't the wish to help. It was the lack of a plan for what would happen next.

Here's a question I get asked a lot, usually by people who have never had to count a burrow in the rain. Why not kill everything at once and be done with it? Because the real cost of a big project is never in the first season. It's in the years afterwards, when the thing nobody planned for turns up. That's the part the planning documents tend to leave out.
