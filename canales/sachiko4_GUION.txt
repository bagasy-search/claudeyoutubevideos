[STORY] In Osaka, in the rainy season, which we called tsuyu, everything was wet. The clothes did not dry. The walls were damp. If it smelled sweet and clean, she knew that too. She taught me to use my nose before my hands.
[STORY] In the 1950s, when I was a girl, the market was just three streets away from our house. The fish man called out in the morning. The vegetable woman had a little cart with a wheel that squeaked. And the rice shop had sacks stacked to the ceiling, with the names of the farms written in brush. My mother would walk there with me holding her sleeve, and she would touch the rice in the sacks. She rubbed a few grains between her fingers and smelled them. She said good rice feels a little cool and a little dry, and it makes a small sound when you pour it. I did not understand her then. I understand her now.
[STORY] In 1970 I came to America with my husband. We rented a tiny apartment with thin walls and a kitchen the size of a closet. The rice here was different. The grains were longer, and the rice cooker in our first apartment was a cheap one. I cooked rice every day, and every day it was not right. My husband was too polite to complain. He ate it anyway, and I could see that it bothered him. So I wrote letters to my mother in Osaka, and in her answers, she told me what she did, step by step. That is where I learned most of what I am about to show you.
[WASH] Number one is washing. Most people think washing is one quick rinse. It is not. Put the rice in a bowl, and cover it with cold water. Use your hand, not a spoon. Move your fingers through the grains in a slow circle, and the water turns cloudy. That cloudy water is the starch and the dust from the outside of the grains. Pour it off carefully, without losing the rice. Add fresh cold water, and do it again.
[WASH] Repeat this three or four times, until the water is mostly clear. Not perfectly clear. Mostly clear. Rice from a store is never perfectly clean, and if you chase clear water forever, you waste the water and the rice gets tired from all the handling. Three or four times is what my mother did, and it is what the old chef in my first job taught me too. I will tell you about him at the end.
[WASH] Why does this matter? When rice is cooked, the starch on the outside of each grain turns into a sticky paste. If you leave that starch on, the rice clumps together in one big mass. That is why your rice comes out wet on the top and gluey in the bowl. Washing takes away the loose starch, so each grain stays separate and clean. That is the whole reason. It is not magic. It is just taking away what does not belong.
[WASH] When you pour the cloudy water away, be careful at the end. The last spoon of water often has the most fine powder in it, and it is also where tiny pieces of grain fall. Stop when you see the cloud getting thick again. I save the cloudy water, and I will show you why in another video.
[WASH] After the last rinse, drain the rice in a strainer for ten minutes. Do not let it sit in the strainer for an hour, because it starts to dry. Ten minutes is enough, and then you move to the next step, which is soaking.
[SOAK] Number two is soaking. Put the drained rice back in the pot, or in a bowl, and cover it with cool water. Let it sit for at least thirty minutes. In the hot summer, thirty minutes is enough. In the cold months, when the kitchen is cool, let it sit for a full hour. Soaking lets each grain take in water slowly, from the outside to the middle. Then the inside cooks evenly, with no hard white center.
[SOAK] This is the step that most people skip, because they are hungry and they want to push the button right now. I understand. I was hungry too. But when you skip the soaking, the outside of the grain is soft while the inside is still hard, and you get that hard little dot in the center of each grain. You will see it when you bite into the rice, and you will know you were in a hurry.
[SOAK] After soaking, drain the rice again for a few minutes. You want the grains to be wet, but not floating. The water you add for cooking must be the right amount, and extra water on the rice changes the measure. Drain well, and then measure the water.
[WATER] Number three is the water. This is where most people make the biggest mistake. They put in water by eye, or they fill the pot to the top and hope. In a Japanese rice cooker, there are lines inside the pot. Use those lines. They are made for the rice that comes from the store where you live, and the cooker knows the rest.
[WATER] If you cook on the stove, or you use a cooker without lines, use the same cup for the rice and the water. For Japanese short grain rice, a good starting point is one cup of rice to about one and one fifth cups of water, by volume. By weight, it is close to one part rice to about one and one fifth parts water. Write this down on a card and keep it next to the pot, because you will forget it on the first busy day.
[WATER] Here is what happens if the water is wrong. Too much water, and the rice turns soft, wet, and heavy, almost like porridge at the bottom. Too little water, and the rice stays hard in the middle, with dry spots on top. The water is not a guess. It is the most important measure in the whole pot, so measure it like it matters, because it does.
[WATER] Now the rice goes in the pot with its water, and you put the lid on. If you use a stove, use a heavy pot with a tight lid, and bring the water to a boil with the lid on. When it boils, turn the heat to the lowest setting, and let it cook for about twelve minutes. Do not lift the lid to look. My mother would slap my hand for that, and she was right. Every time you lift the lid, the steam escapes, and the rice cooks unevenly.
[COOK] Number five is the rest, and the rest is what the restaurants do that most home cooks never do. When the rice is done, turn off the heat and leave the lid on. Wait ten minutes. Some cooks wait fifteen. The rice is still cooking gently in its own steam during this time. The water inside the grains spreads out evenly, the grains become soft all the way through, and the sticky surface settles down.
[COOK] If you open the pot straight away, you lose that steam, and the top of the rice dries out while the bottom stays wet. That is one of the reasons people think their rice is bad when really, they only needed to wait. So set a timer. Ten minutes, with the lid on, and nothing else to do. Use the time to wipe your counter. Your rice will thank you.
[COOK] In the old restaurant kitchens, the cooks did the rest in a wooden tub, a hangiri, which is a wide wooden tub with a rim. That is the bowl you see in the picture. The wood pulls the extra steam out of the rice, so the grains stay separate and shiny. You do not need a special tub for home cooking. The lid and the ten minutes are enough. But I like wood, because wood breathes, and rice likes to breathe.
[FLUFF] Number six is the paddle, and this is the one my husband laughed at. The Japanese rice paddle is called a shamoji. It is flat and wide, usually made of wood, and it is the most important tool in the kitchen after the pot. Do not use a metal spoon, and do not stir the rice like soup. Stirring crushes the grains. The grains get mushy on the outside, and they lose their shape.
[FLUFF] Instead, put the paddle in at the edge of the pot, and cut straight down through the rice. Then lift gently, from the bottom, and turn the rice over onto the top. Cut, lift, turn. Cut, lift, turn. Go around the pot slowly, and let the steam come up between the grains. Do this for about thirty seconds. You will see the rice fall apart into separate, shining grains, with no clumps and no broken pieces.
[FLUFF] My husband said I was making a show of it. I said the paddle is the difference between rice and porridge. He tried it once, with his own paddle, and he stopped laughing. He said the rice tasted lighter, and it did. Lighter does not mean less. It means each grain is separate, so the flavor reaches your tongue cleanly, one grain at a time.
[FLUFF] Once the rice is fluffed, leave the lid open for a minute, so the extra steam can leave. Then put the rice in a wooden or glass bowl, not a metal one, because metal cools the rice too quickly on the outside. Serve it warm. The best rice is warm, not hot, and not cold. You can taste the sweetness when it is warm.
[VARIATIONS] Now I want to show you the other things that come out of rice, the ones my mother made on busy days, and the ones I learned in the restaurant kitchen. These are not complicated. Each one uses the same rice and the same water, and each one has a measure and a short memory, the way my mother taught me.
[VARIATIONS] Number seven is the seasoning that the restaurants use for sushi rice. It is the secret behind the shine. The rice is cooked the same way, but while it is still hot, you spread it in the wooden tub and sprinkle a mixture of rice vinegar, sugar and salt over it. For about two cups of uncooked rice, you need about five tablespoons of rice vinegar, two and a half tablespoons of sugar, and about one and a third teaspoons of salt. Warm the vinegar mixture a little first, so the sugar and salt melt.
[VARIATIONS] Then you cut the seasoning into the rice with the paddle, the same cutting motion as before. Do not stir. While you cut, fan the rice with a piece of cardboard or a folded paper fan. The fanning cools the rice quickly, and it gives the grains a shine. The cook in my first restaurant job fanned his rice for five minutes without stopping, and I thought his arm would fall off. But the rice had the shine you see in the best sushi shops. That is the whole trick.
[VARIATIONS] Keep the sushi rice covered with a damp cloth, and use it within a few hours, not the next day. Vinegar rice is not the same after it has cooled for a long time, and you should not keep it out on the counter while you watch television. Make it fresh, use it, and enjoy it.
[VARIATIONS] Number eight is the rice ball, the onigiri. In every Japanese lunch box, you find one, wrapped in a strip of seaweed. In my mother's house, we made them for school, for the train, and for the rainy days when nobody wanted to cook. Here is the rule. Wet your hands with cold water, and rub a small pinch of salt on your palms. The salt flavors the outside of the rice ball, and it helps the rice hold its shape.
[VARIATIONS] Take about a half cup of warm rice in your hand, and press gently in a triangle or a round shape. Do not squeeze hard. If you squeeze, the rice becomes hard and dense, and it tastes like a brick. You want the grains to hold each other, not to be crushed. Three turns with your hands, and it is done. If you want a filling, press a small piece of cooked salmon or pickled plum into the center before you close it. Keep the filling small, and use only what is already cooked.
[VARIATIONS] My mother always made the onigiri with her hands in cold water, even in winter. She said the cold hands kept the rice from sticking to her fingers. Your hands will be a little wet and a little salty. That is good. That is what the rice wants.
[VARIATIONS] Number nine is the simple rice porridge, which we call okayu. When you have leftover rice, and you are tired, this is the best meal in the house. Take one cup of cooked rice and put it in a small pot. Add about four cups of water, and bring it to a gentle boil. Stir it slowly now, because the rice is already soft and it can stick to the bottom.
[VARIATIONS] Let it simmer for about fifteen minutes, until the grains break apart and the porridge looks creamy. Add a little salt, and a few drops of soy sauce if you like. My mother added a little grated ginger when someone in the house had a cold. Ginger is warming, and a warm bowl is good when you are tired. You will be surprised how much comfort comes from one small pot.
[VARIATIONS] Number ten is the fried rice made from yesterday's rice. Yesterday's rice is actually better for fried rice, because the grains have dried a little in the refrigerator, and they do not turn into a mass in the pan. Break up the cold rice with your fingers first. Heat a little oil in a wide pan. Add the rice and move it around until each grain is hot and a little golden, about five minutes. Then add a beaten egg, a little salt, and a spoon of soy sauce, and keep moving the rice until the egg is cooked through.
[VARIATIONS] The rule is the same as always. The leftover rice must already be cooled and kept in the refrigerator, and it must be cooked to steaming hot before you eat it. We will talk about that rule in the safety part. For now, use the cold rice from the refrigerator, and make the pan very hot.
[VARIATIONS] One more from my mother, for a child who was sick. She made a thin rice soup with a little water and a pinch of salt, and she called it the soft rice. She cooked it until it was almost liquid. Then she let it cool to warm and spooned it into my mouth, one spoon at a time. I did not know it then, but it was the same comfort that the rice porridge gives you now. Rice is always the first food in a Japanese house when someone is not well.
[MISTAKES] Now let us talk about what goes wrong, because it will go wrong, and it is not you. Let us start with the most common one. Your rice comes out wet and mushy at the bottom, and it looks like porridge in the pot. If it comes out mushy, it was too much water, or the rice was not drained well after soaking, or the lid was lifted during cooking. It is not you. It is the water line, and the lid.
[MISTAKES] Here is how to save mushy rice. Spread the rice on a wide tray or a flat plate, and let it sit with the lid off for five minutes. Fan it gently with a paper fan, and use the paddle to lift and turn it. The extra moisture will leave the grains. Do not add more heat, because that makes the bottom burn. Just let the air do the work.
[MISTAKES] The second problem is the opposite. The rice is hard in the middle, with a little white dot in each grain. If it comes out hard, the water was too low, or the rice was not soaked, or it cooked too fast with the lid open. It is not you. The rice needs more water and more time. Here is what I do. Add two tablespoons of hot water on top, put the lid back on, and let it sit on the lowest heat for five more minutes. Then rest it for ten minutes with the lid closed. The grains will soften, and the hard center will go away.
[MISTAKES] The third problem is the burnt bottom, the crust we call okoge in Japanese. Some people love it. My husband loved it. It has a toasted, nutty taste, and it is good if you make it on purpose. But if it is burnt black and bitter, the heat was too high for too long. Use the lowest heat when the rice is simmering, and use a heavy pot that spreads the heat evenly. If the bottom is only a little brown, scrape it off gently and enjoy the crust on its own, as a snack.
[MISTAKES] The fourth problem is the rice that turns yellow or gets a bad smell. This is the one that worries me most, and I want you to pay attention. Rice that smells sour, or looks yellow, or feels slimy, should not be eaten. Throw it away. Do not taste it to be sure. Your nose and your eyes are enough. A good rule is that rice that looks or smells wrong is not worth any risk.
[MISTAKES] The fifth problem is the reheated rice that comes out dry and hard. This happens when the rice sat in the refrigerator and then was heated with no water. The grains dry out and become rubbery. The solution is simple. Put the cold rice in a bowl. Sprinkle one or two spoons of water on it. Cover it with a damp paper towel. Microwave it in short bursts. Stop when it is hot all the way through, and fluff it with the paddle. The water and the steam bring it back.
[MISTAKES] I want to say one thing about the reheating, because it is the rule that keeps people safe. Reheated rice must be steaming hot all the way through. Not warm. Steaming. If you cannot see the steam coming out of the middle, heat it a little longer and stir it once in the middle to check. A safe temperature is one hundred and sixty five degrees Fahrenheit, which is seventy four degrees Celsius. Use a thermometer if you have one. If you do not, use your eyes and make sure the middle is steaming.
[MISTAKES] The sixth problem is the one people never expect. Rice tastes too bland. This happens when the rice is not seasoned at all, or the water was too much, so the flavor washed out into the water. A pinch of salt in the cooking water changes everything. Add it when you add the water, and stir it once. You do not need much. A quarter teaspoon for a cup of rice is enough to wake up the grains.
[MISTAKES] So that is six mistakes. In every one, the answer is the same. Measure the water, rest the rice, use the paddle, and keep the lid closed when it should be closed. Rice is patient. It will forgive you almost everything, except the cold water and the impatience.
[SCIENCE] Now let me explain why this works, the way I would explain it to a child who is twelve years old and asks too many questions. Rice is mostly starch. A starch is a long chain of sugar molecules, folded up tight like a ball of wool. When you put the rice in water, the grains begin to drink. The water moves into the grain, and the starch chains loosen a little. That is why soaking helps.
[SCIENCE] When you heat the rice in water, the starch swells and softens, and the grain becomes tender and sticky. Scientists call this gelatinization. You do not need to remember the word. You only need to remember the idea. Heat and water open the starch, and the grain becomes soft. Too much water makes it mushy. Too little leaves the middle hard. The water line is a measure for the starch, not a suggestion.
[SCIENCE] The sticky feeling on the surface of cooked rice comes from loose starch that was washed off the outside of the grain, and the grains stick to each other when that starch is not removed. That is why washing matters. It is a simple chemistry lesson, and it is in the sink, not in a book.
[SCIENCE] The rest with the lid closed works because the steam keeps the heat even and the water spreads evenly through the grain. Rest is not wasted time. It is part of the cooking. And the paddle works because cutting separates the grains, while stirring squeezes them together. These are not my ideas. They are what the starch and the water do, and the old cooks found them by watching carefully for a very long time.
[SCIENCE] Number eleven is the one I want you to remember every day, so listen carefully. Cooked rice can carry a germ called Bacillus cereus. This is a common bacterium that lives in soil and dust. Its spores can survive cooking. If cooked rice is left at room temperature for many hours, the spores can grow and make a toxin. Reheating does not always destroy that toxin. This is why the cooling rule matters so much.
[SCIENCE] The rule is this. Cool cooked rice within one hour after cooking, and put it in the refrigerator. Spread it in a shallow dish, so it cools quickly. Do not leave it in the big pot on the stove, and do not leave it on the counter while you eat dinner and talk for two hours. Keep the rice in the refrigerator at forty degrees Fahrenheit or colder, and eat it within one day. Reheat it until it is steaming hot all the way through.
[SCIENCE] And never leave cooked rice out overnight. I know some families do it, because the rice is easy to keep warm in a rice cooker. But the warm setting on a rice cooker is not the same as the refrigerator, and rice left out for many hours is the most common way to get sick from rice. In my mother's house, we cooled the rice in a wooden bowl by the window, and we put it away before we went to bed. That is the rule she taught me, and it has kept my family safe for fifty years.
