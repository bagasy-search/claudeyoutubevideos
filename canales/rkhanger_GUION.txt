So let us start with what is inside the door, because once you see it, you will never look at that little keypad the same way again.

A safe door is held shut by bolts. Round steel pins that slide out of the door and into the body. On a good safe they are thick, and there are a lot of them. On a cheap one there are two or three, and they are still steel, and they are still stronger than they need to be.

The bolts are not the problem. They almost never are. The question is what holds the bolts.

On a keypad safe, the bolts are held back by a very small part. Most often it is a solenoid. A little electric coil with a pin in the middle, about the size of the end of your thumb. You type the code, the keypad sends a pulse, the coil pulls the pin in, and the bolts are free to move.

That is the whole lock. Not the handle. Not the steel. A pin the size of a pencil eraser, held in place by a small spring.

Think about that for a second. You paid for a steel box. And the thing standing between that steel box and anybody who wants in is a spring about as strong as the one in a ballpoint pen.

Now, on a well made safe, that little part is buried. It sits behind a hardened plate, it is wrapped in steel, and there is no path from the outside to anywhere near it. Nobody can reach it, so it does not matter how small it is.

On some of the cheapest ones, there is a path. A gap around the door. A hole left over from the way the bolts were fitted. A reset button inside the door, put there so the owner could change the code, and sitting closer to the edge than it should. On some models, a thin wire through a gap no wider than a pencil can reach the one part the keypad was supposed to be in charge of.

That is the coat hanger. It is not skill. It is geometry. Somebody built a box with a second door in it, and never meant it to be a door. It is the same story as the water bottle and your front door latch. The strong part is fine. The little part next to it is not.

Now here is the part that surprised a lot of my customers, and I think it is the most important thing in this whole video.

Most of the small safes people buy are fire safes. It says so right on the box, usually in big letters, with a picture of flames. Thirty minutes. One hour. And that rating is real. It is a real test, and it is a real number.

But it is a test about heat. A fire safe is built to keep the inside cool enough that paper does not char. So it is made of thin sheet steel, and inside that sheet there is a thick layer of insulation, a kind of cement with water locked up in it. In a fire, the water turns to steam and carries the heat away from your papers.

That is clever engineering. It is exactly what you want for a birth certificate and the deed to your house. And it has almost nothing to do with keeping out somebody who came with tools.

A fire rating is not a burglar rating. They are two different tests, for two different jobs. A safe can pass one with flying colors and never once have been asked about the other.

First question. Can you pick it up? If you can lift it off the shelf and carry it to the door by yourself, so can anybody. Anything under about thirty pounds, loose, is not a safe. It is a suitcase.

Second question. Look at the edge of the door with a flashlight. Is there a gap you can see light through, or a small hole anywhere near the keypad or the hinges? You do not need to put anything in it. Just look. A gap is a path.
