Grass you could walk on, if you were dumb enough. And the thing that turned it into this? Is swimming right over there. [[/H]]

Every winter, from November twentieth to the end of March, a small white trailer opens up at the edge of the Louisiana marsh. Men walk in with garbage bags. A worker counts what's inside, one by one, and the state of Louisiana writes a check for every single one. Six dollars apiece.

What they're counting are tails. Rodent tails.

Last season, one hunter, one guy, brought the state sixty-three thousand, one hundred and eighteen of them. Louisiana has been paying for tails like this for twenty-three years. More than seven million tails. About forty-two and a half million dollars.

And here's the part that got me. It worked. It worked better than anybody thought it would. And that is exactly why it can never, ever stop.

[[H2]] I'm Hank. I'm not a scientist, I'm not a biologist, I'm just a guy from Louisiana with an old truck and way too many questions. So I went and looked. I read the state reports, the old newspapers, the history books. And this is the craziest wildlife story in my own backyard. [[/H]]

To understand how a state ends up paying people for rat tails, you gotta meet the animal.

Its name is nutria. Some folks call it a swamp rat, which it isn't, exactly. It's a big, water-loving rodent from South America. Up to twenty pounds. Webbed back feet. And teeth the color of a traffic cone. Bright, glowing orange. That's iron in the enamel, and it makes those teeth hard enough to chew through just about anything a marsh can grow.

And a female can have up to three litters a year.

So how did a South American rodent end up in a Louisiana bayou? If you grew up around here, you probably heard the story. The Tabasco family, down on Avery Island, brought nutria up from Argentina, and a hurricane in 1940 smashed the pens and let them all loose.

It's a great story. It's also not true.

A Louisiana historian named Shane Bernard went through the old business records, and here's what really happened. Back in the nineteen-thirties, fur farms were a get-rich-quick idea, and nutria were the hot new animal. In 1933, a couple in Abita Springs started a nutria farm, found out nobody wanted to buy the fur, and just let the rest of them go.

The Tabasco heir, E. A. McIlhenny, came later. In March of 1938, he bought twenty nutria, not from Argentina, but from a farm in St. Bernard Parish, for a hundred dollars. And on June first, 1940, two months before that famous hurricane, he let twenty-one of them go into the marsh. On purpose. Years later, he wrote a letter claiming he'd imported them from Argentina himself. His own records say otherwise.

And he wasn't even the only one who thought it was a good idea. The state's own fur official had suggested turning nutria loose on a state wildlife refuge.

So nobody lost them. People released them. More than once. And the marsh? The marsh loved them.

And it wasn't just Louisiana. Nutria came to California as early as 1899, and in the nineteen-thirties fur farms popped up in states all over the country. When the farms failed, the animals went out the back door. Some state and federal agencies even moved nutria on purpose, into ponds and lakes, as living weed cutters, to eat the vegetation choking the water. Today, feral nutria live in at least seventeen states.

Here's where the story gets strange. For a while, nutria were the best thing that ever happened to Louisiana trappers.

In 1946, the state made them a protected furbearer. By the early sixties, German furriers had figured out how to use the soft underfur, and suddenly Louisiana was shipping more than a million nutria pelts a year across the ocean. By 1962, nutria had passed the muskrat as the king of Louisiana fur.

At the peak, in 1976, trappers took one point eight million nutria, worth fifteen point seven million dollars. Whole families along the coast paid their bills with it. In the 1980-81 season, a good pelt brought eight dollars and nineteen cents.

[[H3]] And here's the line in the state report that I can't stop thinking about. During all those years, when the fur market was strong, there were no reports of marsh damage from nutria. None. The trappers were the dam. They were holding the whole thing back and didn't even know it. [[/H]]

Then the dam broke.

In the eighties, fur went out of style. European fur farms flooded the market. Fashion went to leather. Animal-rights campaigns grew. The stock market crashed in 1987, and in 1991 Washington slapped a luxury tax on furs. The price of a nutria pelt fell to four dollars, then two sixty-four, then less. The Russians came in and bought for a couple of years, and then in 1998 the Russian economy collapsed too.

By the 1999-2000 season, Louisiana trappers brought in twenty thousand, one hundred and ten nutria pelts. Down from one point eight million. Fewer than a thousand trapping licenses were sold in the whole state. The trappers went to work in the oil fields.

And here's the part that'll make your head spin. Back in 1958, when the fur was worthless the first time, the state had already declared nutria a pest and put a twenty-five-cent bounty on them in sixteen parishes. Nobody ever put up the money. It was a bounty that never paid a cent. Then in 1965, when the fur market came back, the legislature made nutria a protected animal again. Protected, then a pest, then protected. It depended on the price of a coat in Germany.

And nobody told the nutria.

[[H4]] Look at this. See how the grass just stops? That's what they call an eat-out. A nutria doesn't just nibble the top of the plant. It digs down and eats the roots. And the roots are the only thing holding this marsh together. [[/H]]

Nutria eat about a quarter of their body weight every day, and the state says they destroy about ten times more than they actually eat. Once the roots are gone, the soft marsh soil has nothing holding it. The tide comes in, scours it away, the ground sinks a little lower, and the grass never comes back. The marsh turns into open water. Permanently.

In the nineties, the state started flying helicopters low over the coast, two hundred feet up, counting the damage. In 1999, they estimated one hundred two thousand, five hundred and eighty-five acres of marsh damaged by nutria. That's almost as big as all the dry land in the city of New Orleans.

And that's a low number. The way they measure it, a helicopter flies a hundred and fifty-five straight lines across the coast, north to south, about two miles apart. Two people look out the windows and write down the damage they can see, and then they multiply to estimate the whole coast. Anything under the trees, anything too small to spot at eighty miles an hour, doesn't get counted. The real damage was bigger.

And this was happening in a place that was already losing ground. Coastal Louisiana has lost about two thousand square miles of land since 1932, from all causes put together. About a quarter of its land. At one point, it was losing a football field's worth about every hundred minutes. And that marsh is the speed bump between the Gulf of Mexico and every town behind it when a hurricane comes in.

So the state tried everything it could think of.

First, they said, let's eat them. In the late nineties, Louisiana paid for a marketing campaign. Flyers said nutria had more protein and less fat than turkey or chicken. There was talk of calling it ragondin, the French word, or bayou rabbit. A French chef named Philippe Parola and Enola Prudhomme, Paul Prudhomme's sister, cooked up nutria à l'orange for the cameras.

The trouble was, nobody wanted to eat it. As one trapper told a reporter, it looks like a rat. It's not a pretty animal. And at one point, the whole state had exactly one licensed nutria meat processor.

Then somebody said, let's wear them. A New Orleans designer started a line called Righteous Fur. Guilt-free fur, she called it. There were fashion shows. A model walked in a nutria-fur bikini.

Somebody else turned them into dog treats. A brother and sister in Baton Rouge started Marsh Dog, nutria jerky and biscuits, and they said their long-term goal was to go out of business.

A sheriff outside New Orleans had SWAT snipers ride in the back of pickup trucks along the drainage canals at night, shooting nutria that were digging into the levees. Down in Venice, folks started a Nutria Rodeo, with a nutria toss. In 2022, sixty-two teams brought in one thousand nine hundred and thirty-four nutria in a single weekend. The heaviest weighed twenty-one point seven pounds. And the winners had to pass a lie detector test. I'm not making that up.

It was all very Louisiana. And none of it was anywhere near enough. One famous New Orleans chef put it best. She said nutria meat is really the zucchini of the animal world. Meaning, it tastes like whatever you cook it with. It was a great line. It didn't sell a lot of nutria. Nutria were eating the coast faster than anybody could cook them, sew them, or feed them to a dog.

So in 2002, the state tried something a lot simpler. Forget the meat. Forget the fur. Just bring us the tail.

It's called the Coastwide Nutria Control Program. You register, you get permission from the landowner, and from November twentieth through the end of March, you hunt nutria anywhere in coastal Louisiana. You cut off the tail, you bring it to a collection station, and the state pays you. Four dollars a tail to start. The goal was four hundred thousand nutria a year.

[[H5]] So this is what one of those stations looks like. It's a trailer. A freezer, a table, a guy with a clipboard, and a whole lot of garbage bags. You sign on a little screen, and your check comes in the mail. That's it. That's the whole weapon. [[/H]]

Most of the hunting happens at night, from mud boats and airboats, with a spotlight and a small rifle, on private land. The tails get counted, bagged, and trucked off to a disposal pit.

And the first season, hunters brought in three hundred and eight thousand tails. In one winter, more than the entire fur harvest of the previous three years put together.

Then, in 2005, Hurricane Katrina hit. That season, the harvest fell to a hundred and sixty-eight thousand tails. Trappers had lost their homes, their boats, their camps. Nobody was out hunting nutria. But the next year, the state raised the bounty to five dollars, and the hunters came back.

Now, you can guess the other thing that happens when you pay cash for a body part. People try to cheat. The program has to watch for fraud all the time. In 2023, a man in Patterson was arrested and accused of cutting the tails off live nutria and letting the animals go, so they could keep breeding more tails. The state says a nutria has to be dead before the tail comes off. A bounty only works if you keep an eye on it.

Then the helicopters went back up.

Eighty-two thousand acres of damage in 2003. Fifty-three thousand in 2005. Twenty-three thousand in 2008. Eight thousand in 2010. By 2014, four thousand, one hundred and eighty-one acres. From over a hundred thousand.

A state biologist told a reporter, much to our surprise, it worked very well.

It had worked so well that by the middle of the 2010s, it almost looked like the problem was solved. And that's when something happened that nobody had planned for.

People stopped showing up.

The bounty had been five dollars a tail since 2006. Gas went up. Shells went up. Fewer hunters were willing to go out in the cold marsh at night for five bucks. In the 2017-18 season, the harvest fell to a hundred and seventy thousand tails. The lowest since the program began.

And the marsh answered almost overnight. In one year, the damage went from five thousand eight hundred and sixty-six acres to sixteen thousand four hundred and twenty-four. Nearly three times as much. And about nine hundred and ten acres had already turned into permanent open water. Gone. You can't pay a hunter to bring that back.

So in the summer of 2019, the state raised the bounty to six dollars a tail.

So they won, right?

Here's the twist. They didn't win. They can't.

Up in Maryland, at the Blackwater National Wildlife Refuge, nutria were eating the marsh too. But Maryland's problem was small enough to finish. Trappers and detector dogs removed about fourteen thousand nutria. The last one was caught in May 2015, and in 2022, Maryland declared itself nutria-free.

Out in California, nutria turned up again in 2017, after everybody thought they'd been wiped out decades ago. California has been hunting them ever since, with dogs and traps, and has talked about radio-collared Judas nutria to lead trappers to the rest.

Louisiana is different. Millions of acres of marsh, swamp, and canals, much of it nearly impossible to reach. And a rodent that can have three litters a year. The state's own biologist said it plainly: eradication would be nice, but it's really not possible.

So think about what that means. The nutria aren't beaten. They're being held down. One tail at a time. Only for as long as somebody keeps paying. The first year Louisiana stops, the numbers in that helicopter survey start climbing again. We already watched it happen, in 2018, in a single year.

It's the same thing those fur trappers were doing back in the seventies, when nobody even knew they were saving the coast. Think about the whole circle. The state helped bring them here. Then protected them. Then called them a pest. Then protected them again. Then paid people to eat them, wear them, and finally, just to bring back the tail. Eight dollars and nineteen cents for a pelt in 1981. Six dollars for a tail today. The only difference is who's paying. Back then it was fancy ladies in Germany buying fur coats. Now it's everybody in Louisiana.
