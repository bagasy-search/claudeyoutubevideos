Number one. Is it bolted down? Most people think a safe is safe because it is heavy. Weight is not an anchor. The morning after, I found a floor safe that had been set on the carpet, on top of a rug pad, the way you would set down a cooler at a picnic. Nothing under it. Nothing through it. In the morning the whole safe was gone, and the only thing left was a flat, neat square pressed into the rug. Here is why that matters. Burglars do not always open a safe where they found it. Sometimes they take the whole thing somewhere private and take their time. Police crime-prevention guides say it plainly: make sure the safe is bolted to the floor. Here is the check, and it takes ten seconds. Walk around the safe once with a flashlight before you touch it, and keep that picture in your head. Then put both hands on the safe and try to rock it. If it moves, it is not anchored. The fix is a small set of anchors that goes through the holes the manufacturer built into the bottom of the safe for exactly this job. That cost is on your screen. It is less than you think, and it is much less than the safe. Anchors are not hard to buy, and a person with a drill can set them on a Saturday, as long as what they go into is really what you think it is.

I want to be honest about something, because a lot of people hear "bolted down" and relax too early. Bolting a safe down slows a thief. It does not make the safe unbreakable, and nobody who sells you one should tell you it does. What the bolts do is take the easy option away. A safe that cannot be carried out the door is a safe the thief has to work on inside your house, with the noise, the time and the risk that comes with it. That is why the police advice is so simple. It makes the easy plan impossible, and the people who break in are almost always looking for the easy plan.

Number two. Where is it? The bedroom is where most people keep the good things, and it is where most burglars look first. Police crime-prevention guides say the same thing: do not put the safe in the bedroom, because that is the first place a burglar will look. Nightstand drawers and the space under the mattress are the next places they check. The morning after that closet, I had another call, this time a master bedroom with a safe tucked behind a dresser, and the wife told me she had been completely sure nobody would think of it. Everybody thinks of it. Check tonight: stand in your bedroom, put yourself in the doorway, and ask where you would look first if you were not the owner. Your honest answer is the answer a stranger will have. The fix costs nothing but the decision. Pick a spot that is hard to see from the door, out of sight from the windows, and set it there before you bolt anything down. A closet is not a plan, and neither is a nightstand. Moving a bolted safe is the expensive way to learn this lesson.

Number three, and it is the most important fix in this whole video, even though it is not the lock. Bolted to what? I have seen safes bolted into drywall. Of all the things I have fixed, drywall is the part that gives first, every single time. I have seen safes bolted to a thin plywood subfloor with the plastic plugs that came in the box. One morning I found two plastic plugs still sitting in the wall, and the bolts were lying loose on the carpet, like somebody had pulled a stubborn weed and dropped the root. Plastic is not made for pulling. A plastic plug is not a real anchor. Use a real anchor, made for concrete. A floor safe belongs in a slab. It is set in during construction, or in a prepared pit, and that job goes to a professional. If there is no slab, ask the installer where the bolts go, and make sure it is into studs, not just drywall. Check tonight: look at what is under the safe and what the bolts actually go into. Get down on the floor. If you cannot say the word concrete out loud and be sure of it, you have a problem. The fix on your screen for this one is the professional tier. It costs the most of the three, and it is the one that matters the most.

Here is how to look without tools, so you do not have to start pulling things apart tonight. Lift the edge of the rug or the carpet pad around the safe, and look at what is underneath. Concrete is gray and hard, and it does not give when you press a fingernail into it. Plywood has layers, and you can see the edge of every sheet. Drywall is soft and white, and it crumbles at the rim of the hole. If you find a plug, count how many there are, and look at whether they are the plastic kind that came in the box, or real anchors with a nut and a washer. A safe with two little plastic plugs in a wall is not a safe that is bolted down. It is a safe that is waiting for the wrong afternoon.

Number four is the key ring. In the picture, look at the carpet right beside the safe. That is the family's key ring, and it was there the night before, three feet away from the safe, on the carpet, where anybody could see it from the closet door. A key kept by a safe is not a safe. It is the second lock, and it has a welcome mat. I have seen one taped under a drawer of a bedside table, and I have seen one hanging on a nail inside the closet door, right where a person walks in to get dressed. Check tonight: where is the key that opens your safe right now? Do not answer with where you think it is. Go and look. If the answer is in the same room, or in a drawer, or on a hook by the door, move it. The fix costs nothing. Keep the key on your person, or somewhere in another part of the house, and if your safe uses a code instead, memorize the code. Do not write it on a sticky note inside the closet door. I have found that note more times than I want to tell you.

Number five is the rating, and this is the one most people never read. A safe is a safe, is what most people believe, and it is not true. Two things on the door or in the manual tell you what the box really is. Look for a burglary rating printed on the label. It comes from the UL testing for burglary, and it tells you how a door is built to resist tools. A fire safe is a different thing. A TL rating says nothing about fire at all. Many policies have a section on theft. Read yours tonight, because it may cap jewelry and cash, and it may ask for a rated safe. Check tonight: find the label on the door, or the model number on the back, and read it out loud. Say it out loud, because the eye skips over numbers that it has seen a hundred times. If you cannot find a rating, you do not have a burglary safe. You have a box with a lock on it. The fix is a rated safe, which is the top tier on your screen, and it is also the tier you should only buy after you have finished fixes one through four, because a rated safe sitting on the carpet is still a safe that can be carried away.

Let me explain the difference in plain words, because a lot of people buy the wrong box and then feel safe for years. A fire rating tells you how long the inside stays cool enough to save paper when the house is burning. A burglary rating tells you how long the door resists people with tools. They are two different tests, and a box can pass one and fail the other. A fire test does not stop a grinder. A burglary test does not stop a fire. If you keep your jewelry in a box you bought for your papers, that is a decision you made without knowing it. Read the label, and then ask yourself what you are protecting, and which kind of test that thing needs.

Number six is the paperwork, and it is the one the insurance company asks about first. The morning after the bedroom call, the wife had a shoebox of receipts in the nightstand, and the rest of the list was in her head. The adjuster asked her for serial numbers, and she had none. She had photos of the house, but not of the rings. Here is the honest part. Police crime-prevention guides say to engrave or permanently mark your valuables, photograph them, and log the serial numbers. The same guides say to keep a detailed inventory with a description, the date of purchase, the value and the serial number, and a copy somewhere away from the home. Check tonight: could you list everything in your safe, with a photo of each item, on a phone that still works after a fire? If you cannot, do it this week. Put the list in an email to yourself, or give a copy to a relative. I have never once seen a list that was too long. The fix costs nothing but an evening. Ten minutes of paperwork is worth more than any lock I have ever installed, because it is what turns a bad morning into a claim that gets paid.

The adjuster came back to me a few weeks later and asked what the wife had written down about that morning, and the honest answer was almost nothing. She had a sentence. She had her word about the ring, and her husband's word, and the husband was already doubting himself. That is what happens when there is no paper. People argue. A report gets written with the words "no forced entry" in it, and the claim gets stuck in the middle, and somebody who did nothing wrong ends up on the phone for weeks. The paperwork is not a formality. It is the only thing you have when the story has no broken door in it.

Number seven is the one you can do before the weekend. The portable lockbox. Many people think the small box on the closet shelf is a safe. It is a container. In my experience, a small portable lockbox keeps out a casual look, and somebody can carry the entire box away in one trip. The lockbox on the shelf is the easiest thing in the house for a burglar to take, and that is the whole problem. Check tonight: pick up the box you keep your valuables in. If you can carry it in one hand, you have found the problem. The fix is one of two things. Bolt the box to something solid, or move the valuables into an anchored safe, which is the middle tier on your screen.
