Let me start with a story, because it's why I care about this.

A few years before I retired, I got a call from a man in his seventies. Retired machinist, very proud of his workshop. He had a nice wooden shed out back, full of tools he'd collected over fifty years. Lathes, a drill press, hand tools his father gave him. And on the door, a combination padlock he'd bought at the supermarket checkout for six dollars.

Every tool he could carry was gone. He kept saying to me, Ray, I never told anybody the numbers. And he hadn't. He didn't need to.

So let's talk about why.

A combination padlock works with a set of little wheels inside, stacked one behind the other. When you turn the dial to the right numbers, notches in those wheels line up, and the shackle, that's the U-shaped part on top, is free to pop up. Simple. Clever. And on a good lock, very hard to get around.

The trouble is that on a cheap lock, those wheels are made loosely. There's slop in them. The parts don't fit tight. And that slop is something a person can feel with their hands, even through the dial. Somebody who knows what to feel for can find the numbers in a few minutes without ever knowing them. That's the whole problem, and I'm going to leave it right there.

The second weakness is the shackle. On a lot of cheap padlocks, the shackle is held in by a little spring latch that you can get around without touching the dial at all. Again, I'm not going to tell you how. But it's been all over the internet for years, and every thief knows it.

And the third weakness is the simplest one. Cheap steel. A lot of cheap padlocks have a shackle you can cut with a pair of bolt cutters in about two seconds. Or a body made of soft metal that cracks if you hit it with a hammer. A padlock is only as strong as its weakest piece.

So that's three ways in. Feel the numbers. Get around the latch. Or just cut it. And the six-dollar lock usually loses on all three.

Now, here's the good news. You can spot a good padlock in about thirty seconds, standing in the aisle at the hardware store, without opening the package. Let me give you the five things I look for.

Number one. Look at the shackle. The best ones have a shrouded shackle. That means the body of the lock comes up around the sides of the shackle, so there's only a little bit of it showing. Bolt cutters need something to grab. If there's barely any shackle showing, there's nothing to grab.

If you can't get a shrouded one, at least get a short shackle. The less shackle sticking up, the harder it is to get a tool in there. A long shackle is easier to use, but it's also easier to cut.

Number two. Look at what it's made of. The package will usually tell you. You want a shackle made of hardened steel or boron alloy. Hardened is the important word. Plain steel cuts like butter. And you want a solid body, steel or solid brass, not a thin shell wrapped around something light. Pick it up. A good padlock is heavy for its size. If it feels like a toy, it is.

Number three. Look for ball bearing locking. This is a little more technical, but it's usually printed right on the package. It means the shackle is held in on both sides by steel balls instead of one little spring latch. That's the thing that stops the trick I mentioned that doesn't use the dial at all. If you see the words dual ball bearing locking, or double locking, that's a good sign.

Number four. For a combination lock, look for anti-shim or shim-resistant on the package, and for a resettable lock with a solid feel to the dial. A good combination lock dial turns smooth and tight, without wobble. If you're in the store and there's a display model, give the dial a turn. If it feels loose and rattly, put it back.

And number five. Look at the price. I don't like telling people to spend money, and I'm not going to tell you to buy a hundred-dollar lock for a garden shed. But a good padlock for most homes costs somewhere between fifteen and forty dollars. The six-dollar one at the checkout counter isn't a lock. It's a decoration that says please don't.

And let me clear up three things I hear all the time in the hardware store aisle.

The first is that a bigger padlock is always a better padlock. Not true. I've seen big shiny padlocks the size of a fist that were mostly air inside, with a long thin shackle that cuts in one bite. Size doesn't matter. What it's made of, and how much shackle is showing, matters.

The second is that a brass padlock is strong because brass is heavy. Solid brass is good against rust, which is why people use it outside and near the ocean. But brass is softer than hardened steel. A solid brass body with a hardened steel shackle is a good lock. A brass shackle is not.

And the third is that a weatherproof cover means a better lock. A rubber cover is nice. It keeps rain and grit out of the keyway, and that does make the lock last longer. But a rubber cover on a bad lock is still a bad lock. Look underneath the cover at the five things.

Now, keyed or combination? People ask me this all the time.

A good keyed padlock is usually harder to get around than a good combination lock, especially at the same price. But combination locks have one big advantage. You can't lose the key. For a gym locker, for a shed that three people in the family need to open, for a gate the dog walker uses, combination is handy.

So here's my rule. For the shed or the garage, where the stuff inside is worth real money, get a good keyed padlock with a shrouded shackle. For the gym locker or anything you open every day, a good combination lock is fine. Just not the cheapest one.

Now here's the part almost nobody thinks about. The padlock is only one piece. The hasp is the other.

The hasp is the metal hinge-and-loop thing on the door that the padlock goes through. And on most sheds I ever looked at, the hasp was the weakest part of the whole setup. It's a thin piece of metal held on with four short wood screws that go into soft, old wood. You could have the best padlock in the world on there, and somebody with a pry bar pops the whole hasp off the door in about five seconds, padlock and all.

So go look at your shed tonight. Look at the hasp. Are the screws showing on the outside? Then somebody can just unscrew them. Is it held on with little short screws into old wood? Then it's coming off with one good pull.

The fix is cheap. A heavy-duty hasp with a hinged cover that hides the screws when it's closed costs about ten to fifteen dollars. Mount it with carriage bolts that go all the way through the door, with a washer and a nut on the inside. A carriage bolt has a smooth round head on the outside, so there's nothing to grab and nothing to unscrew. Ten minutes with a drill, and now the hasp is as strong as the door.

And if the shed door itself is thin plywood, or the hinges are on the outside with screws showing, that's the next thing to look at. A thief doesn't care which part gives. He takes the easiest one.

Let me give you a few quick ones for the places you probably have a padlock right now.

The backyard shed. Good keyed padlock, shrouded or short shackle, hardened steel. Heavy-duty hasp with carriage bolts. And if you have expensive tools in there, write down the serial numbers and take pictures of them tonight. That's what the insurance company and the police will ask for.

The gym locker. A good combination lock is fine here. Don't use your birthday or your house number as the combination, and don't use the same combination as your bike lock or your phone. And never leave your car key in the locker with a cheap lock. That's the key to the car in the parking lot.

The suitcase. Those little locks on luggage aren't really meant to stop anybody. They're meant to keep the zipper from opening by accident, and to show if somebody got in. That's fine. Just don't pack anything you can't afford to lose in a checked bag.

The side gate. If it's just keeping kids and dogs in, a simple latch is fine. If it's keeping people out of the backyard, a good padlock on a gate latch that can't be reached over the top from the outside. Most gates I've seen can be opened by reaching over. Check yours.

And the storage unit. Most storage places will tell you to use a disc lock, the round kind with almost no shackle showing. That's good advice. Buy a good one. Your whole life might be in that unit.

And the bike. A lot of people use a cheap cable lock or a combination cable on a bicycle that cost them four hundred dollars. Those thin cables cut in a couple of seconds with small cutters that fit in a backpack. For a bike, the rule is the same as the shed. Spend a fair share of what the bike is worth on the lock. A solid U-shaped bike lock, hardened steel, locked through the frame and the back wheel to something that can't be lifted over or unbolted. And look at what you're locking to. I've seen a lot of good locks on bikes chained to a skinny sign post that came right out of the ground.
