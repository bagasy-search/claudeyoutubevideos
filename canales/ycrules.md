Picture a Monday morning in October, 1956. A nine-year-old boy runs the last block to school with one shoelace flapping, and he is two minutes late. The big front doors are already shut. And standing just inside them, waiting, is the principal. He doesn't say a word. He's holding something flat and wooden in his right hand, with holes drilled through it. And every kid in that building knows exactly what it's for.

That was one rule. There were hundreds. They were printed in pupil handbooks, posted on classroom walls, and enforced without a single question. Today, some of them would get a teacher fired. One of them can get a school employee charged with child abuse. And one of them, believe it or not, is still legal in seventeen states, right now.

So I went back to the real rule books, the court cases, and the laws that ended them. Twenty-five rules every kid followed, counted down from the mildest to the ones that changed American history. And for every single one, I'll tell you honestly where it stands today. Illegal. Banned in most states. Against school policy. Or just gone.

Welcome to Yesterday's Classroom. Let's open the book.

Number twenty-five. No chewing gum. Ever. In 1952, the student handbook at the Montreat School in North Carolina put it in plain type: chewing gum is not to be used in classes. A decade later, a Connecticut high school banned it from the building at any time. Teachers had eyes like hawks for a moving jaw. Get caught, and the stories are famous. You'd spit it into the wastebasket in front of everyone, or spend recess scraping old gum off the bottoms of the desks. The verdict: just gone, mostly. No law ever touched it, and plenty of schools still ban gum today. But in most American classrooms, nobody is checking jaws anymore. And somewhere out there is a 1956 desk with forty layers of gum underneath it, still holding.

Number twenty-four. Walk in single file, keep to the right, and don't say a word. At St. Petersburg High School in Florida, the 1950 handbook wrote it like a traffic law: traffic must be single file to the right of these lines. The school painted green lines down the middle of its back stairways. Going up, you used one side of the front stairs. Coming down, the other. Monitors stood in the halls and checked for passes, and if you were out of class without one, your name went on a list. You'd be surprised how many grown-ups still keep to the right in every hallway they walk down, seventy years later. The verdict: just gone, or at least half gone. The silent single-file line has mostly disappeared. But the hall pass survived. In a lot of schools today, it's an app on a tablet.

Number twenty-three. Boys use the boys' door. Girls use the girls' door. Walk past an old brick schoolhouse today and look up. On hundreds of them, the words are still carved in stone above two separate doors. Boys on one side. Girls on the other. At the Elmwood School in East Bridgewater, Massachusetts, a woman from the class of 1954 remembered the teacher ringing a hand bell at the girls' door, and the children lining up two by two, and being scolded if they didn't come in quietly. By the fifties, newer schools had stopped building separate doors, but kids in the old buildings still used them every morning. The verdict: just gone. There was never a law to repeal. The rule simply faded away, and the stone outlived it. The carvings are still there, over doors that everybody uses now.

Number twenty-two. Morning inspection. Hands flat on the desk. In many classrooms, the day didn't start with arithmetic. It started with the teacher walking down every row, looking at your fingernails. That same Massachusetts memoir describes it exactly. After the Pledge, the children spread their hands on their desks, smiled to show they'd brushed their teeth, and laid a clean handkerchief out where the teacher could see it. There's a famous photograph from 1917 by Lewis Hine, titled simply, Daily inspection of teeth and finger nails. If your nails were dirty, the whole class knew it. Some teachers kept a chart with gold stars for clean hands. The verdict: just gone. No law forbids it, but no teacher does it anymore. The school nurse still checks your eyes and your hearing. Your handkerchief, however, is your own business now.

Number twenty-one. No dungarees. Blue jeans were work clothes. And to a lot of school boards in the 1950s, they were something worse, the uniform of the juvenile delinquent. Boys were expected in slacks and a shirt with a collar. As late as 1967, a New Hampshire high school handbook still said sport shirts could be worn, but fully buttoned. Show up in denim with rolled cuffs like a movie rebel, and you were sent home to change. The verdict: just gone. Dress codes are still legal, and plenty of uniform schools still ban jeans. But walk into almost any American classroom today and count the dungarees. It's most of the room. Including the teacher.

Let's stop for a second and check your detention slip. If you broke the first five, the gum, the hallway, the wrong door, the dirty nails, and the dungarees, you'd have spent about a week after school, writing sentences on the blackboard. Hold on to that pink slip. It's about to get a lot longer.

Number twenty. Clean your plate. In the lunchroom, you took what the lunch lady put on your tray, and you ate every bite. The Clean Plate Club went back to the First World War, and in 1947, President Truman revived it to save food for hungry families in postwar Europe. Elementary schools formed clubs of their own. A lunch monitor might stand over you until the last forkful of creamed spinach was gone, while the rest of the class went out to recess. Nobody asked if you were full. Food was not to be wasted. Period. The verdict: just gone. And here, a law actually helped. In 1975, Congress changed the school lunch rules so high schoolers no longer had to take foods they didn't want. It's called offer versus serve. Today, the rule is almost the reverse. Take only what you'll eat.

Number nineteen. Practice your ovals. Every single day. Penmanship was a real subject, with its own grade on the report card. And in millions of classrooms, it was taught the Palmer way: rows and rows of looping ovals and push-pulls, the whole arm moving, not just the fingers. Some kids weren't allowed to touch ink until the fourth grade. Then came the inkwell, the dip pen, and the blots that could ruin a whole page. Sloppy letters meant doing the page over. The verdict: just gone, and then a twist. Cursive quietly dropped out of the national standards in 2010. But now it's coming back, by law. About two dozen states require cursive again. New Jersey signed its law in January of 2026. So if your grandkids are practicing ovals this year, you know exactly how they feel.

Number eighteen. When you see the flash, duck and cover. In January 1952, a cartoon turtle named Bert walked into American classrooms. The government film Duck and Cover taught children that if an atomic bomb ever flashed in the sky, they should drop under their desks and cover the backs of their necks. Schools ran the drill right alongside the fire drill. The teacher would call out in the middle of a lesson, and thirty kids would fold themselves under little wooden desks and wait in silence. The verdict: just gone. The civil defense program faded with the Cold War, and no school drills for atomic flashes today. But ask anyone who was in the third grade in 1955, and they can still show you exactly how to cover their neck.

Number seventeen. Write with your right hand. For generations, a left-handed child was treated as a problem to fix. Teachers moved the pencil into the right hand, and some even tied the left one down. By the 1950s, the practice was fading. Researchers at the University of Iowa had blamed forced switching for stuttering, and many schools had quietly given up. But if you were a lefty in a strict classroom in 1953, you might still spend the year learning to write with the wrong hand. Here's how much it mattered. In America, the share of people who write left-handed roughly tripled over the twentieth century, as teachers stopped fighting it. The verdict: just gone. No law was ever needed. Teachers simply stopped. And if you're a lefty who got switched, you probably still eat with one hand and write with the other.

Number sixteen. Girls wear dresses. Slacks are not school clothes. In March 1942, a sixteen-year-old in Brooklyn was sent home for wearing blue slacks to school, and it made the newspaper. That rule held for decades. A 1967 New Hampshire handbook still said it flat out: slacks and shorts are not acceptable as regular school wear. In winter, girls wore pants under their skirts for the walk to school and pulled them off in the cloakroom. Then they sat all day with cold knees. The verdict: illegal in five states, and a very risky rule everywhere else. In 2022, a federal appeals court ruled that a North Carolina charter school's skirts-only rule for girls was unconstitutional. In 2023, the Supreme Court let that ruling stand. No public school requires dresses today.

Number fifteen. Boys, keep your hair off the collar. In the fifties, the worry was the greaser's ducktail. By the sixties, it was the Beatles. In September 1966, three boys in Dallas, members of a garage band called Sounds Unlimited, showed up to enroll at W. W. Samuell High School with Beatle haircuts. The principal refused to let them in until they got a trim. They sued. And they lost. In 1968, a federal appeals court sided with the school. For years, principals across the country checked hair against collars and sent boys straight to the barber. The verdict: just gone. And here's the surprise. It was never made illegal. The Supreme Court never settled it, and the lower courts split. Hair rules simply went out of style, right along with the crew cut.

Number fourteen. Stand in the corner, and face the wall. Talk out of turn, and you'd be sent to stand in the corner, nose toward the plaster, while the lesson went on behind you. In some older classrooms, there was even a tall paper cone waiting on a stool. The dunce cap. By the fifties, the cap was mostly a relic, but the corner was very much alive. Everybody could see you. That was the point. You'd be standing in that corner by nine fifteen, counting the cracks in the paint and listening to everyone else do long division. The verdict: against school policy. No law bans a short time-out. But the teachers' code of ethics, adopted in 1975, says an educator shall not intentionally expose a student to embarrassment. Public shaming like that today would earn a teacher a very uncomfortable meeting.

Number thirteen. Say a bad word, and your mouth gets washed out with soap. This one mostly happened at home. Mothers reached for the bar of soap by the sink, and kids learned exactly what it tastes like. But sometimes it happened at school, too, with the bar from the washroom. The logic was simple. A dirty mouth needs cleaning. Nobody thought of it as anything but discipline. The verdict: against school policy, and today, it can be a crime. In May 2023, at an elementary school in New Mexico, a classroom aide was charged with child abuse after putting soap in a seven-year-old's mouth. So this is the one I promised you at the beginning. What was an ordinary punishment in 1956 can put a school employee in front of a judge today.

Time to check that detention slip again. If you broke all thirteen so far, you'd be looking at three weeks of detention, a note sent home, and a phone call your father had to take at work. And we haven't even gotten to the paddle.

Number twelve. Stand up and say the Pledge. Every pupil. No exceptions. Every morning, in nearly every classroom in America, the day began the same way. Chairs scraping back, hands over hearts, eyes on the flag in the corner. In June 1954, Congress added two words, under God, and millions of kids had to relearn it. At St. Petersburg High in 1950, the handbook said that during the flag ceremony, everyone stands at attention, facing the flag nearest him. Now here's the twist. Forcing a child to say it was already unconstitutional before the 1950s even began. On Flag Day, 1943, the Supreme Court sided with two young sisters from West Virginia who had been expelled for refusing to salute. The verdict: illegal, since 1943. Schools can lead the Pledge. They cannot make you say it. Most kids in 1956 had no idea.

Number eleven. Boys take shop. Girls take home economics. No switching. In junior high, the hallway split in two. Boys went to the wood shop to make birdhouses, bookends, and lopsided lamps. Girls went to the home ec room to sew aprons and bake biscuits. If a boy wanted to learn to cook, or a girl wanted to run the lathe, the answer was no, and nobody thought twice. It wasn't about talent. It was about which door you walked through. The verdict: illegal. Title IX, passed in 1972, and the rules that followed in 1975 say a school cannot require or refuse a class, including industrial arts and home economics, because you're a boy or a girl. Today, a boy can take cooking and a girl can take welding, and nobody blinks.

Number ten. No buttons, no armbands, no protests. In 1956, you had no free speech rights at school. If the principal didn't like the pin on your sweater, it came off. That changed because of a thirteen-year-old in Des Moines, Iowa. In December 1965, Mary Beth Tinker, her brother John, and their friend Christopher Eckhardt wore black armbands to school. The principals had banned armbands just two days before. The students were suspended. Four years later, in 1969, the Supreme Court ruled in their favor, with a line every law student learns: students don't shed their rights at the schoolhouse gate. The verdict: illegal, since 1969. Schools can still stop speech that seriously disrupts class. But a quiet armband, a button, a message on a shirt? That's protected now.

Number nine. The principal can go through your pockets whenever he likes. In the fifties, the school stood in for your parents. If a teacher wanted to see what was in your desk, your lunch pail, or your coat pockets, you emptied them. No reason needed. Comic books, marbles, folded-up notes, all confiscated into a desk drawer, some of them never seen again. The verdict: illegal, since 1985, at least without a reason. That year, in a case that started with a fourteen-year-old caught smoking in a New Jersey restroom, the Supreme Court ruled that the Fourth Amendment protects students at school, too. And here's the twist. The Court actually allowed the search of her purse, because the vice principal had a reasonable suspicion. So a school can still search you today. It just needs a real reason first.

Number eight. The principal can send you home. No explanation. No hearing. In 1956, the student handbook at Lincoln High School in Manitowoc, Wisconsin warned that conduct in or out of school could make a pupil subject to dismissal from school. Who decided what counted? The principal. There was no appeal, and no chance to tell your side. The verdict: illegal, since 1975. In early 1971, a student named Dwight Lopez was one of about seventy-five students suspended in Columbus, Ohio, after a lunchroom disturbance. He said he was only a bystander, and nobody ever asked him. The Supreme Court ruled in Goss versus Lopez that before a suspension, a student is owed notice and a chance to explain. Suspensions are still legal. The silence isn't.

Number seven. Talk back, and the ruler comes down on your knuckles. Nearly everyone who went to school in the fifties has a story about the ruler. The wooden twelve-inch one, with the thin metal edge. It lived on the teacher's desk, and it wasn't just for measuring. Hold out your hand, palm down. The stories are most famous from parochial schools, but public school teachers used it too. It stung for an hour, and nobody told their parents, because at home you'd get it again. The verdict: banned in thirty states. A ruler across the knuckles is corporal punishment, and thirty states plus Washington, D.C. now ban it in public schools. But here's the part people don't know. In private schools, only five states ban it. New Jersey did it back in 1867. New York and Maryland didn't get around to it until 2023.

Number six. Get married, and you're off the team. It sounds strange now, but plenty of teenagers in the fifties married young, and schools had rules for them. In Garland, Texas, in the spring of 1959, a sixteen-year-old football letterman named Jerry Kissick married his fifteen-year-old sweetheart. The school board had already ruled that married students would be restricted wholly to classroom work. No football, no clubs, no student council. His family sued, and a Texas court upheld the ban. Up in Mesick, Michigan, two boys were cut from the football team after summer weddings. The verdict: illegal. In 1972, federal courts began striking these rules down, and the Title IX rules of 1975 put limits on how schools could treat married students. Today, a married senior plays on Friday night like everyone else.

Number five. The paddle. Remember the principal at the beginning, waiting at the door? This is what he was holding. A board of solid wood, sometimes with holes drilled through it, and in some offices, a nickname painted on the handle. Get sent to the office, and you'd bend over a chair and take your licks. Three, five, sometimes more. And most parents said, good. On October sixth, 1970, at Drew Junior High in Miami, a fourteen-year-old named James Ingraham got twenty licks in the principal's office. His case went all the way to the Supreme Court, and in 1977, the Court ruled that paddling in school did not violate the Constitution. The verdict: banned in thirty states. Thirty states and Washington, D.C. have banned it. But in seventeen states, there is still no state law against it. In the 2021 school year, schools reported about thirty-two thousand cases. Right now. In America.

Number four. Every morning, the class prays together. In many classrooms, after the Pledge came a prayer. In New York, the state Board of Regents wrote one in 1951, twenty-two words long, and recommended it for every classroom. It began, Almighty God, we acknowledge our dependence upon Thee. In 1958, the schools of New Hyde Park, New York adopted it. Five families sued. On June twenty-fifth, 1962, in Engel versus Vitale, the Supreme Court ruled six to one that a prayer written by the government and led by the school did not belong in a public classroom. The verdict: illegal, since 1962. Here's what often gets lost. Students can still pray on their own at school, any time they like. What ended was the school leading it. For a lot of families in 1962, that ruling was a genuine shock.

Number three. Ten verses of the Bible, every morning, required by law. This wasn't just custom. In Pennsylvania, the law said at least ten verses from the Holy Bible shall be read at the opening of each school day. A survey around 1960 found Bible reading in about four in ten school systems across the country. In November 1956, at Abington Senior High near Philadelphia, a sixteen-year-old named Ellery Schempp decided to protest. During the required reading, he quietly read from a different holy book, and he refused to stand for the Lord's Prayer. He was sent to the principal's office. His family's case went all the way to the Supreme Court. The verdict: illegal, since 1963. In an eight to one decision, the Court struck down required Bible reading in public schools. Teaching about the Bible, as history or literature, is still allowed.

Number two. A girl who becomes pregnant is removed from school. Immediately. For most of the twentieth century, this was simply how it worked. A girl disappeared from class one day. Sometimes she was sent to stay with an aunt in another town. Often she never came back, and nobody explained anything to the rest of the class. At North Middlesex Regional High School in Townsend, Massachusetts, the rule even had a number. Rule 821. It said her membership in the school shall be immediately terminated. In 1971, a senior named Fay Ordway told her principal she was expecting, and she was told to leave. She went to federal court, and in March 1971, a judge ordered her back into class. The verdict: illegal. Title IX, passed in 1972, and its rules forbid shutting a student out because of pregnancy. Today, she walks across the stage with her class.

And number one. The rule that shut the schoolhouse door. In the 1950s, a child with a disability could simply be refused. Many states had laws that let schools turn away children who were deaf, or blind, or judged unable to benefit from the classroom. Parents were told to keep them home, or send them to an institution. In September 1950, in Minneapolis, parents from across the country met because of exactly that, and founded the group that became The Arc. They fought for twenty-five years. By 1970, American schools were educating only one in five children with disabilities, and Congress found that one million children were shut out of public school entirely. The verdict: illegal, since 1975. On November twenty-ninth, 1975, the Education for All Handicapped Children Act became law. Today it's called IDEA. Every child, whatever the disability, is owed a free public education. Of all twenty-five rules, this is the one that changed the most lives.

That's the list. Twenty-five rules, from a stick of gum to the schoolhouse door. Some of them, we're better off without. A few of them, honestly, taught us something. To stand in line, to look a grown-up in the eye, to clean up after ourselves. And one of them, the paddle, never fully left. So now it's your turn. Which rule would you bring back?
