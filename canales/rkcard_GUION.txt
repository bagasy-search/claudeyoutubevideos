But first you need to see what is actually holding your door shut. Because it is probably not what you think.

Go to your front door. Open it, and look at the edge, the part that faces the frame. If you only have a doorknob, you will see one piece of metal sticking out. It is angled on one side. It looks like a little ramp. That is called a spring latch.

That ramp is there on purpose. It is the reason you can pull a door shut behind you without turning the knob. The door swings closed, the ramp hits the metal plate on the frame, the plate pushes the ramp in, and a spring snaps it back out into the hole. Click. Door closed.

That is a wonderful piece of engineering for keeping a door closed. It was never meant to keep a door locked.

Think about what that ramp does. It is designed to get out of the way when something pushes on its angled face. And the plate on the frame is not the only thing in the world that can push on an angled face. Anything thin and stiff that reaches that face can do the same thing. A card. A strip of plastic. Old-timers in my trade called it loiding, from celluloid, what those strips used to be made of. The trick is older than most of us.

So when you lock a knob, what are you actually locking? You are locking the knob. The outside knob will not turn anymore. But the latch, the ramp, can still be pushed. Locking the knob does nothing to that little ramp.

That is the whole secret. A knob lock stops a hand. It was never designed to stop a card.

Now, to be fair, this does not work on every door. It depends on which way the door swings, on the gap, on the trim, and on the lock itself. On some doors it is nearly impossible. And on a lot of them it comes down to that one little piece of metal I mentioned.

They did not leave it open. They left it latched. And latched is not locked.

So let us do the ten second test. Stand inside your house with the front door closed. Look at the edge of the door where it meets the frame, on the side with the knob.

First question. How many pieces of metal go from the door into the frame? If the answer is one, and it is the one that moves when you turn the knob, then that door is held shut by a spring latch and nothing else. That is the first thing to know.

Second question. Look for a small second bolt next to the latch. It is usually a thin little plunger, sometimes flat, sometimes round, sitting right beside the angled ramp. Most keyed entry knobs have one. That is the piece that was built to stop this.

It is called a deadlatch, or a guard bolt, and the idea is very simple. When the door is closed properly, that little plunger gets pressed in by the plate on the frame. And while it is pressed in, it locks the ramp in place. The ramp cannot be pushed back. The card has nothing to work against.

But it only works if the plunger stays pressed in. And for it to stay pressed in, the door has to close tight enough, and in the right place, that the plunger lands on the flat face of the plate and not in the hole.

Here is what happens in real life. Houses settle. Doors sag. The top hinge screws work loose. Somebody adds weatherstripping. Somebody paints the frame four times. And over the years the door drifts a little, an eighth of an inch, a quarter of an inch.

And now, when you close that door, the little plunger does not land on the plate anymore. It slips into the hole right next to the latch. It pops back out. And the lock that was supposed to protect you is just sitting there, doing nothing, looking exactly the same as it did the day it was installed.

So here is the ten second part. With the door closed and latched, look at the gap between the door and the frame. If you can see daylight around that latch, or you can slide the edge of a sheet of paper in there and move it up and down freely, the door has more play than it should.

Then open the door and watch the little plunger as you close it slowly. It should get pushed in by the plate and stay in. If it slides into the hole with the latch, the deadlatch is not doing its job. That is it. That is the test. Ten seconds, and nothing to buy.

Fix number two costs about three dollars. Tighten the door.

Remember the sag we talked about. The most common cause is the top hinge. The screws that hold it came from the factory about three quarters of an inch long, and they only go into the thin door frame, not into the wall framing behind it.

Fix number three is also cheap, and it is the plate on the frame. That plate is called the strike.
