So let's start with the postcard. Greenland's is spectacular.

Icebergs the size of cathedrals floating past colourful wooden houses. Red, blue, yellow, green, scattered on bare rock by the sea. Dog sleds. The northern lights. Whales in the fjord.

All real. And mostly what's on the postcard is ice. About four fifths of Greenland is covered by an ice sheet, in places more than three kilometres thick. People live on the thin rocky edge around it, almost all of them by the sea.

There are no roads between the towns. None. You travel by boat, by plane, by helicopter, or in winter in the north, by dog sled and snowmobile.

The capital, Nuuk, has about twenty thousand people. About a third of the whole country, in one town. Most Greenlanders are Inuit, somewhere between eight and nine in every ten. Their language, Greenlandic, is the official language.

So who are these people? Not explorers. Not extras in a geopolitics documentary. Families who've lived in the Arctic for centuries, who fish, who hunt seals, who work in shops and schools and the hospital, who worry about rent and their kids and the price of food flown in from far away.

Let's go back a little. Because to understand why so many people want Greenland, you need to know who's had it.

Denmark and Norway's kings established colonial rule in Greenland in the eighteenth century, with a missionary in seventeen twenty-one. For more than two hundred years, Greenland was a colony, run from Copenhagen.

And then, in eighteen sixty-seven, right after buying Alaska from Russia, the American government started looking at Greenland too. An official report studied the idea of buying it. Nothing came of it. But the idea never really went away.

During the Second World War, when Denmark was occupied by Germany, the United States took over the defence of Greenland and built bases there. And when the war ended, it didn't really want to leave.

In nineteen forty-six, the United States made a formal, secret offer to Denmark. One hundred million dollars, in gold, for Greenland. We only know about it because the documents were later found in the American national archives.

Denmark said no. But the Americans stayed anyway, with an agreement, and with bases.

Nobody, in that whole negotiation, asked a single Greenlander.

So, a quick recap. A colony for two centuries. An island quietly offered for gold. And a people who weren't part of the conversation. Now let me show you what that looked like on the ground.

In the far north, there was a small Inughuit village called Uummannaq. The Inughuit are the northernmost people in Greenland, some of the northernmost people on Earth. They hunted walrus and seal, on land their families had used for generations.

Right next to it, the Americans had built a big air base. Thule. And in nineteen fifty-three, the base needed to expand.

So on the twenty-fifth of May, nineteen fifty-three, the families were told at a meeting that they had to move. Twenty-seven families. One hundred and sixteen people. The plans had been made since at least nineteen fifty-one. The people themselves were not part of the discussions.

They moved north, more than a hundred kilometres. Many carried their belongings by dog sled. And in their new place, which would become the town of Qaanaaq, they lived in tents from May until November, while houses were being built.

It took fifty years for that to reach a courtroom. In two thousand and three, the case reached Denmark's Supreme Court, and the Danish state acknowledged that the move had been a serious and unlawful act against the people who lived there. An official apology was made. Compensation was paid. Half a century late.

That's one story. There's another one that's even harder to tell.

In nineteen fifty-one, Danish authorities chose twenty-two Greenlandic children, most around six or seven years old, and sent them to Denmark. The idea was to turn them into a Danish-speaking elite who would come back and modernise Greenland.

Six of them were adopted by Danish families and never came home. The sixteen who came back didn't return to their families. They grew up in a children's home in Nuuk, speaking Danish, many of them unable to talk properly with their own relatives.

When the official investigation came out, it described what the children were left with in simple words. Loneliness. A feeling of being different and rootless. A lost, divided identity.

In twenty twenty, Denmark formally apologised. In twenty twenty-two, the six who were still alive got an apology face to face, and compensation.

And then there's the hardest one. From the nineteen sixties onward, thousands of Greenlandic women and girls, some of them still children, had contraceptive coils fitted by doctors. Many of them say no one asked for their permission, or explained what was being done. Some only found out years later, when they couldn't get pregnant.

An independent investigation was set up by both the Danish and Greenlandic governments. In twenty twenty-five, they apologised together, and Denmark agreed to compensate the women.

I tell you these stories not to point a finger at anyone alive today. Both governments have said sorry, in public, and that matters. I tell you because when people in Greenland hear foreigners talking about who should own their island, these are the memories in the room.

Being moved. Being chosen. Being decided for.

Let's recap. A village moved for a base. Children sent away for an experiment. Women decided for by doctors. Every time, the same pattern. Important people far away, deciding what's good for Greenland. Now let's look at what Greenlanders built for themselves.

In nineteen seventy-nine, Greenland got home rule. Its own parliament, its own government, for most domestic matters.

Then in nineteen eighty-five, Greenland did something remarkable. It voted to leave the European Economic Community, the club that would become the European Union. Mostly because of fish. Greenlanders wanted to control their own fishing waters. A country of about fifty thousand people walked out of Europe's biggest club, on its own terms.

And in two thousand and nine, a new self-government law gave Greenland even more control, and, crucially, it recognised that the people of Greenland have the right to independence if they choose it, by their own decision.

So legally, today, the door is there. If Greenlanders want to leave, they can.

So why haven't they? Here's where the money comes in. Because this is the part the headlines usually skip.

Greenland's economy runs on two things. Fish, and a cheque from Denmark.

Fish and shrimp make up the vast majority of what Greenland sells to the world. Estimates put fisheries at around ninety percent of exports.

And every year, Denmark sends a fixed sum called the block grant. In twenty twenty-five, it was four billion, four hundred and fifty million Danish kroner. That's just over half of everything the Greenlandic government takes in. Around a fifth of the whole economy.

So imagine you're a Greenlander. You want independence. You also want the hospital to stay open, the school in your village to keep its teacher, the plane to keep flying to your town. And half the government budget comes from the country you'd be leaving.

That's not a simple choice. And Greenlanders know it better than anyone.

Now, you might be thinking, what about all the minerals? The rare earths, the uranium, the stuff everyone says is under the ice? Doesn't that make Greenland rich?

Maybe one day. But here's something important. Greenlanders have their own opinions about mining too.

There was a huge project in the south, near a town called Narsaq, for rare earths that came with uranium. Many people there worried about radioactive dust, about their sheep farms, about their water and the fish. In twenty twenty-one, Greenland's own parliament voted to ban uranium mining above a certain level, which blocked that project. The vote was close, and the debate has gone back and forth since.

But think about what that means. While foreign powers were talking about Greenland as a treasure chest, the people of Greenland were voting on whether they even wanted to open it.

And there's a third thing everybody talks about from far away. The ice.

Between two thousand and two and twenty twenty-three, according to NASA, Greenland lost an average of two hundred and seventy billion tonnes of ice every single year. That's the weight of the water in about a hundred million Olympic swimming pools. Every year. It all ends up in the sea, and it raises the sea level everywhere, including on the beaches where I grew up in Spain.

For scientists, Greenland's ice is a climate thermometer. For investors, melting ice means new shipping routes and easier access to rock that used to be buried. But for the people who live there, it's something much more ordinary, and much more personal.

In the far north, hunters have always travelled on the frozen sea, by dog sled, to reach seals and walrus. When the sea ice forms later and breaks up earlier, the hunting season gets shorter and the trips get more dangerous. Hunters there have been describing this to researchers and reporters for years. The thing the rest of the world calls a headline, they call their road to work.

So when you hear that melting ice makes Greenland more valuable, remember who's standing on that ice.

Here's something else that tells you a lot about where Greenland wants to go. For most of its history, if you wanted to fly to Greenland from Europe or North America, you had to land somewhere else first, at an old military airfield inland, and change to a small plane. The capital's runway was too short for big jets.

In November twenty twenty-four, Nuuk opened a new international airport, with a runway more than twice as long as the old one, two thousand two hundred metres. For the first time, big planes could fly straight into the capital from Europe and North America.

That's not a small thing. It's a country deciding to open its own front door, rather than waiting for someone else's.

And life there is not cheap. Almost everything that isn't fish or meat from the hunt has to come in by ship or plane, across thousands of kilometres of ocean. Fresh fruit, milk, building materials. People in small towns live with that every day, with a supply ship that can be delayed for weeks by the ice.

So: home rule, a walk-out from Europe, a legal right to independence, an economy built on fish and Danish money, and a parliament that's willing to say no to a mine. Now, what do the people actually want?

In January twenty twenty-five, a polling company surveyed a representative sample of Greenlanders for two newspapers, one Danish and one Greenlandic. One expert called it the first poll that truly asked a representative cross-section of the population.

The first question. Do you want Greenland to leave Denmark and become part of the United States? Eighty-five percent said no. Six percent said yes. Nine percent didn't know.

Second question. If there were a referendum on independence today, how would you vote? Fifty-six percent said yes. Twenty-eight percent no. The rest didn't know.

And third, the really interesting one. Would you still want independence if it made your standard of living worse? Forty-five percent said no. Not if it makes life poorer.

So there it is. Most Greenlanders don't want to be bought. Most would like to be independent, one day. And almost half aren't willing to pay for it with a worse life.

Honestly, that sounds less like a geopolitical prize and more like any family I know. We want to be free. We also want to pay the rent.

There's one more number that stayed with me. In twenty twenty-five, only six hundred and eighty-four babies were born in all of Greenland. The lowest since the Second World War.

The people that everyone is fighting over are a very small, very fragile nation. Every family matters. Every child matters. Every person who moves away to Denmark for work or study is a real loss.

When you see Greenland on a map, it's huge. When you count the people, it's a small town. And that small town is being asked, again and again, to carry the weight of the world's arguments about the Arctic, about bases, about minerals, about melting ice.
