Let's start with the animal itself, because the bison has a story worth hearing.

The European bison is a cousin of our American buffalo. A little taller, a little leaner, more of a forest animal. A big bull can weigh around as much as a small car.

And it came close to being gone for good. After the First World War, soldiers and poachers shot the last wild ones in the Białowieża Forest, on the border of Poland and today's Belarus. The very last wild one in the Caucasus Mountains was shot in nineteen twenty-seven.

What saved them was a handful of animals in zoos and private parks. Every European bison alive today comes from about a dozen founders. Twelve animals. That's a family reunion, not a species.

Poland put them back into the wild in the nineteen fifties. Today there are thousands across Europe. That part is a real comeback, and I tip my hat to the people who did it.

Now here's where England comes in. A wildlife charity in Kent, together with a wildlife park, wanted to bring bison into an old woodland called the Blean, just outside Canterbury.

The idea goes like this. A bison rubs against trees, strips the bark, and knocks some of them down. That opens up the forest floor to light. More light, more plants, more insects, more birds. The bison are like bulldozers that work for free and don't need diesel.

Sounds lovely. And honestly, the science behind it isn't crazy. Big grazing animals did shape European forests for a very long time.

But here's the first thing the headlines skipped. Nobody's actually sure the European bison ever lived in Britain. There was an older kind of bison, the steppe bison, that was here a very long time ago. Whether this species ever was, scientists still argue about. So when they said bison were coming back, some experts would say they were coming for the first time.

The money came from a lottery charity. In twenty twenty, they announced an award of one million, one hundred and twenty-five thousand pounds. For a herd of four animals.

So let's recap. A rare animal with a good comeback story. A charity with a big grant. A wood near a city full of footpaths. Now watch what happens when the plan meets the law.

In Britain there's a law from nineteen seventy-six called the Dangerous Wild Animals Act. It covers lions, tigers, wolves, crocodiles. And bison.

That law says the bison can't share space with the public. So they couldn't just open the gate and let them wander. They built a double fence around the trial area, much stronger than any cattle fence.

Then there was another problem. The Blean is crossed by public footpaths, and in England those footpaths are a big deal. People have walked them for centuries.

The charity didn't want to close them or move them. So the plan became bridges. Bridges for the bison to cross over the paths, with ramps for people to walk up and look at them. Canterbury's council approved the plans with conditions, and work was set to start in September of twenty twenty-four.

Think about that for a minute. Wild animals that need a dangerous animal license, a double fence, and their own bridges. That's not a wild animal anymore, with all respect. That's a very well-run park. Which is fine. Just call it what it is.

And the bull? There was supposed to be a bull from Germany. He got stuck in paperwork after Brexit. He finally arrived on the twenty-third of December, about five months late. Even the bison had trouble with the border.

And people want to see them, of course. Over in the Netherlands, where bison were put out on a big nature reserve, a walking trail through their area was closed in twenty nineteen. Too many visitors were leaving the path and going off looking for the herd. You put a famous animal in the woods, people will go find it. Whether the animal likes it or not.

And the good news came before the bull did. That first September, one of the cows, who had arrived already pregnant, gave birth. The first calf born to a free-roaming herd in Britain in a very long time. People cried on the news. I'd have smiled too.

A year later, the charity reported what it was seeing. Dung beetles moving into the bison droppings. Paths opening up in the undergrowth. Trees stripped of bark and dying back, the way they planned. They even said the old cow's parasite load had dropped, and wondered out loud whether the bison were finding plants that worked like medicine.

By the end of twenty twenty-four, the herd had about fifty hectares of woodland to roam, with another hundred and fifty set aside for later. A hectare is about the size of a soccer field. So picture fifty soccer fields of forest, with a strong fence around them.

Now, I want to be fair to the bison project. It's fenced. It's licensed. It's on land the charity owns. As far as I can find, those bison haven't trampled anybody's barley. So why are farmers nervous about it?

Because the bison was never the whole plan. The bison was the poster. Behind the poster is a movement called rewilding, and the farmers have already met it. Up north. In Scotland. And the first meeting went badly.

So let me take you up to Tayside, in the east of Scotland. Good farm country. Rivers, barley, potatoes, and flood banks that farmers have kept up for generations.

In the early two thousands, people started seeing beavers in the rivers there. Nobody had released them legally. Some escaped from private collections. Some, most people believe, were let go on purpose by folks who thought they knew better. Nobody was ever punished for it.

And the beavers did what beavers do. They dug burrows into the riverbanks. Into the same flood banks that keep the water off the fields. When a bank full of beaver tunnels gives way in a flood, the river goes right into the farm. Farmers there reported serious losses from exactly that.

Now think about what a flood bank is. It's not a wall somebody built last year. It's an earth bank along the river, sometimes a hundred years old or more, kept up by the farm, patched every time it slumps. When it fails, it's the farmer who pays to fix it. Not the people who let the beavers go.

By twenty sixteen, the Scottish government decided the beavers could stay. In twenty nineteen they became a protected species. Which meant a farmer who had a problem now needed a government license to deal with it.

And here's how that went. In the first months under protection, in twenty nineteen, licenses were issued to kill eighty-seven beavers in Tayside. In twenty twenty, farmers and landowners shot a hundred and fifteen more under license. In twenty twenty-one, another eighty-seven.

Think about the people on both sides of that. A conservation win that has to be managed with a rifle every year. Nature lovers furious at farmers. Farmers furious that they're the ones holding the rifle for a mess they didn't make.

And the beavers kept spreading. The government's own survey found the population had more than doubled in three years, to over a thousand.

Now. Some of the beavers were trapped and moved instead of shot. And plenty of people in Scotland love having them back. Beaver dams can hold water in a drought and slow down floods further down the river. That's real too. But the farmer next door got the bill, and nobody asked him first.

So that's the first thing the farmers of Britain learned. If an animal shows up illegally, and enough people love it, it stays. And somebody local ends up paying.

Now let's talk about the lynx. A wild cat, about the size of a big dog, with tufted ears. It vanished from Britain a very long time ago.

In twenty eighteen, a group called the Lynx UK Trust asked the government for permission to release six lynx in Kielder Forest, in the north of England, right up near the Scottish border. Sheep country.

The sheep farmers there pushed back hard. And this time, the system listened. The environment secretary at the time turned it down in December twenty eighteen, on the advice of Natural England, the government's nature agency.

The reasons are worth hearing. The plan didn't meet international guidelines. It leaned on volunteers, without clear funding. And there was, in the government's words, a lack of local buy-in. In plain words: they didn't bring the neighbors along.

The sheep farmers called it a great relief. And you might think that would be the end of it. It wasn't.

In January of twenty twenty-five, in the middle of a Scottish winter, four lynx turned up near a town called Kingussie, in the Cairngorms National Park. Nobody had a license. Somebody had simply let them out.

They were rounded up by a zoo team over the next few days. One of them died. The others were taken to Edinburgh Zoo.
