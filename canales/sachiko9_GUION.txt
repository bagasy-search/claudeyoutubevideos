[HOOK] Look at this jar. It holds the cloudy rice water from my rice, and I am pouring it onto a big green plant. You wash the rice, you pour the cloudy water down the sink, and you think nothing of it. Then you look at your plant on the windowsill and the leaves are pale, the bottom ones are yellow, and the soil has a little crust on top. You bought the plant food. You watered more. Nothing changed. I want you to know something first. It was never you. You did not forget how to take care of a plant. You were just throwing away the thing that could have helped.
[HOOK2] The jar in my hand is the cloudy water from my rice. My mother called it togi-jiru, rice wash water, and in our house it never went down the drain if there was a plant that could drink it. Today I will show you seven uses my mother swore by. The first one, for your plants, is the one that changed my garden. I will show you how to dilute it, how often to pour it, and the smell that tells you it is ready or it is spoiled. Number two is for the greasy pot you think is ruined forever. Number three is an old Japanese trick for bamboo shoots. Numbers five and six are the two I am careful about, the hair and the face, and I will give you the honest answer on both. And number seven is for the wooden cutting board you use every day, the one most people never think about. Please stay with me to the end.
[INTRO] You know me, I'm Sachiko. I am seventy eight years old, and I still stand at the same white enamel sink every morning with my sleeves pushed up. Come, sit with me. Look at the indigo curtain in the doorway. My mother hung one just like it in Osaka, in a little kitchen where the wooden floor was always a bit damp in the rainy season, and the air smelled of dashi, rice, and the soy sauce she kept in an old brown bottle by the window. That is the kitchen I want you to picture while I talk.
[STORY] I was born in Osaka in 1948, three years after the war ended. I did not live through the war. My mother did. She was a young woman in those years, and the stories she told me at this same kind of table were always about food. How little there was. How she mixed everything she could find into a pot. How nothing, nothing at all, was thrown away. When I was small and I left a grain of rice on my plate, she picked it up with her chopsticks, looked at me, and said mottainai. It means, don't waste. It is one of the few words in Japanese that has a whole feeling inside it, a little sadness and a little pride.
[STORY2] The years after the war were lean. We were not starving in my time, but we were careful. The summers in Osaka were hot, and the rainy season, the tsuyu, came in June and lasted for weeks. Everything was wet. The laundry did not dry. The bread went green in two days. In a house with six people and one small stove, you learned to see value in what other people threw out. My mother saw value in the water from the rice.
[STORY3] Let me tell you where the rice water came from in our house. Every morning, before the sun was strong, she made the rice for breakfast. Before she cooked it, she washed it. You know this part, you have done it yourself. You put the rice in a bowl, you pour water over it, you swirl with your fingers, and the water turns milky white. You pour it off. You do it again. The first water is the cloudiest, the second is lighter, and the third is almost clear. My mother did not pour any of that water away. She kept it in an old glass jar on the windowsill, and she gave it to the plants.
[STORY4] In nineteen seventy I came to America with my husband. We rented a tiny apartment in a building with thin walls and a kitchen the size of a closet. I was twenty two years old, and I did not speak much English. I was homesick every day. I had no garden, only a narrow balcony with a rail. But I had my mother's habit, and so the first thing I did was buy a small pot of morning glories from a market stall. I did not know if they would grow in the American sun. I wrote to my mother in Osaka and told her about the balcony. She wrote back one page. In her careful handwriting, she told me to wash the rice and save the water for the flowers, like always.
[STORY5] Now I want you to understand something about that letter, because it matters for everything I am going to tell you today. My mother was not a scientist. She never read a book about plant food. She did not know the word nitrogen. What she knew was what worked. She watched her plants for years, and she knew which ones grew strong and which ones turned pale. Her knowledge came from her hands and her eyes, the same way your grandmother's knowledge came to her. Today we have a little more science, and I will tell you what the science says, honestly, even where it says less than people hope. But the rice water itself is the same as it was in that small kitchen in Osaka.
[STORY6] So this is where the trick comes from. A washing habit, a jar on the windowsill, and a mother who did not waste anything. In Japan, many home gardeners still do this with the first rinse of the rice. I have heard people in the market say it, and I have read it in old gardening magazines. In America, almost nobody I know does it. They wash the rice, they throw the water down the drain, they go to the store, and they pay for a bottle of plant food that costs much more than a bag of rice. So let us start.
[STORY7] Before I begin, one word of warning, and I will say it again later. Rice water is not a miracle. It will not turn a dead plant green. It will not fix a plant that has root rot, or a plant that lives in a dark corner with no light. What it does is gentle and slow, and it works best when you use it the way my mother did, with patience, with a clean jar, and with a little common sense. If you want the magic, you will be disappointed. If you want a kind habit that saves water and gives your plants a little extra, you will be happy with it.
[CH1] Now, the main method. Number one, for your plants. Take your time with this part, because most of the mistakes happen here.
[STEP0] First, the rice. Use plain white rice, any kind you already have. You do not need a special rice for this. Put the rice in a bowl, and cover it with cool tap water. Swirl gently with your fingers, for about ten seconds. Then pour the cloudy water into a clean glass jar. That is your first rinse, the strongest one, and it is the one I use most for the plants in pots on my balcony.
[STEP1] Now, do you need the second rinse? Yes, if you want a lighter water. Add fresh water to the same rice, swirl again, and pour that into a second jar. This second water is much clearer, almost like tea. For a seedling, or a plant that is still young, I always use the second rinse. For a big plant that is already strong, the first rinse works well. You decide, by looking at your plant. A pale plant can handle a little more. A tender plant needs less.
[WATER] Here is a rule I never break. The water must be cool. Room temperature, or cooler, never hot, never warm from the stove. Hot water scalds the roots. And never use the water from cooking the rice, the one you pour off after it boils, because that water has salt in it if you added salt, and it is much thicker. Only the wash water. Only the cool, cloudy water from the bowl.
[DILUTE] Now the dilution. This is the part everybody gets wrong, so listen carefully. For a plant in a pot, I use one part rice water and one part plain water. One cup of rice wash water, one cup of plain water. Mix them in your watering can. That is the way I always do it. It is a habit from my kitchen, not a number from a laboratory. Do not pour the thick water straight onto the soil. The starch sits on top, it forms a crust, and the crust keeps the air and the water from getting down to the roots. That crust is the thing that makes a plant look sad even though you watered it.
[HOWOFTEN] How often? Once every one to two weeks. Not every day. Not every time you water. Most of the time, for your plants, you still use plain water, just like before. The rice water is the extra, a gentle drink once in a while. My morning glories get it about every ten days in the warm months. My pothos gets it every other week. My peace lily, the big one in the corner that you can see in the thumbnail, gets it about once a month, because its leaves tell me when it has had enough.
[HOWPOUR] How to pour it. Pour slowly, around the edge of the pot, right at the soil, not on the leaves. Let it soak in. If the soil is very dry and the water runs straight through the bottom, pour a little, wait a minute, then pour a little more. Never let the pot sit in a puddle. Every pot needs a hole at the bottom, and if the saucer fills up, pour the extra water out after twenty minutes. Roots that sit in water all day will rot, no matter how good the water is.
[FRESH] Now the smell. There are two ways to use rice water. The first is fresh. You pour it, you dilute it, and you use it the same day. Fresh rice water smells like nothing much, like a little bit of wet rice, very clean. That is the best way, and if you can, do it this way every time.
[FERMENT] The second way is what my mother did. She let the water sit on the windowsill, covered loosely with a cloth, for one or two days. Not in the sun. In the shade, at room temperature. After a day it begins to change. It gets a little sour, like a glass of plain yogurt that has been left out, a light, clean sour. It may look a little more clear on the top. My mother said that was when the plants liked it best. I agree, but I want to be careful with you. Sour is normal. Rotten is not.
[ROTTEN] How do you know the difference? A sour batch smells like a sour pickle, faint, and it is still clear and runny. A rotten batch smells like something that died in a drain. It turns slimy or sticky. It has a thick film or any mold, the fuzzy kind, green, white, grey, or black. When you see those things, throw it out, all of it, into the outside trash, and wash the jar with hot soapy water before you make new water. Never try to save a batch with mold by scooping off the top. The mold grows in the liquid too, and you cannot see all of it.
[FRIDGE] And where do you keep it, if you do not use it right away? In the refrigerator, in a clean closed jar, and you use it within three days. That is my own rule. Some guides say longer, but I do not take the risk with the jars, and I do not want you to take it either. If you make a big batch, pour only what you need into the watering can, and put the rest in the cold. Label the jar with the day. Your memory is not as good as you think it is.
[PLANTS] Which plants like it? From my own garden, I can tell you what I have seen. My morning glories love it. They are thirsty climbing plants with big leaves, and they grow fast when they are fed lightly. My pothos, the trailing plant with the heart shaped leaves, also seems happy with it. My peace lily, the one in the thumbnail, is dark green and glossy after the rice water, and it stays that way when I do not overdo it. My spider plant and my small herbs on the sill take it well too. For leafy green plants in the garden, I use it on the lettuce in a pot, and it seems to help the color. I am telling you what I see. This is not a laboratory.
[PLANTS2] Which plants do not like it? Cactus. Succulents. Any plant with thick leaves that stores water, like a jade plant, because they want to be left alone and they rot easily. Do not give rice water to a seedling in the first week, when the roots are still small. Do not give it to a plant that sits in a saucer of water. And do not give it to a plant that is sick with root rot, the roots are black and soft and smell bad. Those plants need dry soil and light, not food. Fix the root first.
[WHATSCI] Now the honest part, because I promised you. Rice wash water is not a fertilizer like the bag from the store. It contains small amounts of nutrients that came from the grain: starch, a little nitrogen, a little phosphorus, a little potassium, and some natural material from the grain itself. The amounts are small. In 2023, a field study in the journal Heliyon tested fermented rice wash water against a mineral fertilizer over three planting cycles, and its authors called the water a potential plant fertilizer. That is a real study, and I think you should know it.
[WHATSCI] But it is not a guarantee for your balcony. In 2021, a study published in the journal Agronomy said that most research on washed rice water so far is not rigorous enough. Its authors tested how hard you wash the rice, how many days the water ferments, and how much water you use, because those things change what is in the jar. So what does that mean for you? It means you use it as a gentle drink, a little at a time, and you keep watching your plant. That is all my mother ever did.
[CHECK] How do you know it is working? Watch the new leaves. If they come out a little bigger and a little greener over three or four weeks, that is good. If nothing changes, that is also fine. Rice water will not turn a weak plant into a strong one. The light, the pot, the drainage and the water you use every day matter much more. The rice water is only the little extra, the way my mother gave a spoon of something good to the children at dinner.
[CHECK2] One more thing, and it is a small one. Damp soil attracts small flies. If you pour rice water too often, you may see tiny black flies around the soil, the kind that come up from a wet pot. They are called fungus gnats. They do not bite you, but they are a sign the top of the soil stays wet. So let the top inch of the soil dry out between waterings. Use a small fork to loosen the top, once a month. The flies go away when the soil is dry on top.
[USES] Now the other uses, numbers two to seven. These are the things my mother did in the kitchen, and the things I still do, with the same jars. Some of them I can tell you with confidence. Some of them are only what my hands have learned. I will tell you which is which.
[USE2] Number two. The greasy pot. You know the one. The pan you fried the fish in, the pot with the brown film on the bottom that will not come off. My mother used rice bran for those pots, and she scrubbed with a little cloth. I use the rice water first. Pour cool rice water into the pot and let it sit for twenty or thirty minutes. Then wash it with dish soap, as you always do. The water loosens the film a little, and the soap does the real work. Be careful with this one. Let the pan cool down first. Never pour cool water into a hot pan from the stove. It can crack the pan, and the splash can burn you.
[USE3] Number three. Bamboo shoots. In Japan, in spring, fresh bamboo shoots are everywhere, and they have a bitter, harsh taste when they are just picked. The old cooks remove that harshness by boiling them with rice bran, or with the rice wash water. A Japanese agriculture ministry recipe for another spring vegetable, the butterbur sprout, says that boiling it in rice bran or rice washing water makes the fibers soft and takes out the bitterness. The same old method is used for bamboo shoots. One Kyoto cook I have read boils the shoots with about the same weight of rice bran for about ninety minutes, then leaves the pot to rest for two hours before peeling. Cook them the same day you buy them. If you cannot, keep them covered in cool water in the refrigerator and change the water every day.
[USE4] Number four. Starching cloth. In Japan, for hundreds of years, families stiffened cotton with a thin paste made from rice, to make a tablecloth or a collar hold its shape. At home I do a light version with rice water. I dip a clean cotton tablecloth or a handkerchief in cool rice water, squeeze it gently, and hang it to dry flat, not in the wind. Then I iron it while it is still a little damp, on the cotton setting, with a cloth between the iron and the fabric. The result is a light stiffness, nice to the touch. It is not as strong as the spray starch in the store. It is much cheaper, and it smells of nothing at all.
[USE5] Number five. The hair rinse. This is one I am careful about, and I want to be honest. After I wash my hair with shampoo, I pour cool rice water over my hair, massage it for about one minute, and rinse well with plain water. It feels soft to me afterward, and that is all I can tell you, from my own head. I cannot tell you that it makes hair grow, and I cannot tell you that it repairs damage. Nobody can promise that with a kitchen water. If your scalp itches, burns, or breaks out, stop, rinse with plain water, and see your doctor. And rinse well, because rice water left in the hair can smell sour in a warm room.
[USE6] Number six. The face. My mother washed her face with it, and to me it feels soft on the skin. But here is the careful part. Before you use it on your face, do a patch test. Put a little cool rice water on the inside of your wrist, on a cotton pad, and leave it for a day. If the skin turns red, itches, or stings, do not use it on your face. Never use it on broken skin, and keep it away from your eyes. I cannot tell you it removes spots or wrinkles. That is the kind of promise a salesman makes, and you will hear it from many people on the internet. I will not make it. Rice water is a gentle rinse, nothing more.
[USE7] Number seven. The wooden cutting board. This is the one I want you to pay the most attention to, because it is the one you use every day. A cloth dipped in cool rice water is nice for wiping the dust and the little smell from a wooden board after you have cleaned it. But rice water is not a sanitizer. It does not kill germs on raw meat. After you cut raw chicken, raw beef, or fish, wash the board with hot soapy water and dry it. The United States Department of Agriculture food safety experts say that washing with hot soapy water after each use is the right way to keep a board clean.
[USE7] If you want more, they say you can sanitize a wooden or plastic board with one tablespoon of plain, unscented liquid bleach in one gallon of water, flood the surface, let it stand for several minutes, then rinse and dry. Use one board for raw meat and another board for vegetables and bread. When a board gets deep grooves that you cannot clean, it is time for a new one.
[USE7B] After all of that, here is how I finish the board. Hot soapy water. A rinse. A dry towel. Then I stand the board up on its edge in the rack, so the air can move all around it. A wooden board that stays wet on the counter will crack and it will smell. I learned that habit from my own hands, not from a book, and it is the best one I know for keeping wood from warping.
[MISTAKES] Now the mistakes. I promised you cases, so here they are, one by one, from my own kitchen and from the women who come to my sink to ask me questions. Every one of them is common. Every one of them has a simple fix.
[CASE1] Case one. Your plant turns yellow at the bottom, a few weeks after you started, and the soil stays wet for days. If the leaves look like that, it was not the rice water. It was the amount. You poured it too strong, or you poured it too often, and the roots cannot breathe. Stop the rice water completely. Let the soil dry out to the top inch. Then go back to plain water, and only use the diluted water once in two weeks after that. If you see mushy black roots when you take the plant out of the pot, cut those away with clean scissors and repot in dry soil. It is not you. It is the soil that stayed too wet.
[CASE2] Case two. A white crust forms on the top of the soil, like salt or like dried glue. This is the starch, and it is common when you pour the thick water straight from the jar. Take a small fork or a spoon and scrape off the top layer, about half an inch, gently. Throw it away. Then water with plain water, slowly, until it runs out the bottom. From now on, dilute one to one, and pour only around the edge of the pot. The crust will not come back if you do this.
[CASE3] Case three. The water smells rotten. I know you want to save it. Do not. The smell is the message. A sour smell is normal. A rotten smell, or a slimy, sticky, or moldy jar, means bad bacteria have taken over. Pour it out, and not on your plants. Wash the jar with hot soapy water, let it dry, and start over with fresh rice water. Anyone who says a moldy jar is fine is not taking care of your plant. Your plant does not need that risk.
[CASE4] Case four. Little black flies circle the pot all day. As I told you, these are fungus gnats. They come from a soil that stays wet on top, and damp soil makes them come faster. Let the top of the soil dry out, use the small fork once a month to loosen it, and keep the jar of rice water covered with a lid so the smell does not call them. A yellow sticky card near the pot catches the adults. It is nothing dangerous, but it can be annoying for a month.
[CASE5] Case five. You tried it on a cactus, or a fat succulent, and now the stem is soft at the bottom. This is not a rice water problem alone. It is a water problem. Those plants store water in their bodies, and they rot in wet soil. Take it out of the pot, cut away any soft part with a clean knife, let the cut dry for a few days in the shade, and repot in dry gritty soil. Next time, leave those plants alone, and water them only when the soil is dry all the way down.
[CASE6] Case six. You washed the hair, you used the rice water, and the next day the hair smells sour. The water was left in the hair. Rinse much longer with plain water. Use it cool, and use it the same day you mix it. And never keep a jar of rice water in the bathroom, because the warm steam makes it go bad faster. Keep it in the refrigerator, and bring out only what you need.
[SCIENCE] Now, the simple science. You asked me why it works, and you have a right to know. Plants are made of water, light, and some food from the soil. The most important food for leaves is nitrogen. The next is phosphorus, which helps the roots grow. And the third is potassium, which helps the plant handle stress, water, and disease. Those three letters, N, P, and K, are on every bag of fertilizer in the store. The extension services of the universities of Maryland, Georgia and Oregon teach the same thing to home gardeners. They also say plants do not need much more than the soil already gives them, and that too much food can hurt the plant.
[SCIENCE2] So where does rice water fit? The rice grain has small amounts of starch and minerals, and when you wash it, some of those small things go into the water. Some of the starch can feed the tiny living things in the soil. That is a general process, and I cannot tell you how much rice water does it in your pot. The amounts are small, which is why I tell you to use it as a drink, not as a meal. A bag of real fertilizer is stronger, and you should use it with care when the plant needs it. A soil test from a garden store or your local extension office tells you what your soil really lacks. Rice water does not replace that. It is a gentle habit, not a cure.
[SCIENCE3] And here is one more thing to keep in mind, because I think it is important. Starch and water are food for many things. For mold. For flies. For bacteria. That is why the same jar can be a help one day and a problem the next. Keep it cold, keep it clean, and keep it short, and it stays on the good side.
[MONEY] Now let us talk about money, because you deserve to know what this costs. A bag of plain rice is a few dollars, and one bag gives you many weeks of rice water for your whole balcony. You already wash the rice, so the water costs you nothing extra. You were going to pour it down the drain anyway. Now compare that with the store. A bottle of liquid plant food can cost several times more than a bag of rice, and you may use it up in one season. Here is my simple weekly plan, which is how I do it in my own kitchen. Monday, wash the rice for dinner, and pour the first rinse into the clean jar. Wednesday, if the plant is thirsty, use plain water. Friday, use the diluted rice water once, every other week, on the plants that like it. Sunday, look at the leaves, the soil, and the drainage holes, and then decide. That is the whole plan. There is nothing to buy.
[MONEY2] One more money thing. If your rice water goes sour and you do not use it, you have lost nothing but a little time. Throw the jar out, and make a new one tomorrow when you wash the rice again. Do not spend money on a special container. A clean glass jar from your kitchen, the kind that once held pickles or jam, is the best container I know.
[MEMORY] Now I want to tell you about my mother, and the watering can. This part is quiet, so please lean in a little.
[MEMORY2] Every morning, when I was a girl in Osaka, my mother went out to the narrow balcony before the sun came over the roofs. The balcony was no wider than a bench. She had a few clay pots along the rail, and in the summer the morning glories climbed up strings she tied herself, a little higher every morning. The flowers opened blue and purple in the early light, and then they closed by noon. She poured the cloudy rice water into an old green watering can, the one with a crack near the spout that she would never throw away. She poured it slowly. She always said the flowers drink in the morning, so you give them the morning.
[MEMORY3] I remember her hands. They were small and quick, and they were always a little wet. I remember the smell of the wet earth, and the sound of the water going into the clay. She did not talk much when she was on the balcony. She just poured, and watched, and nodded at the flowers, as if they were family.
[MEMORY4] My mother died many years ago, in Osaka. I was in America, far away, and I could not be at the funeral. When I went back to Japan, someone from the family gave me a box of her things, and inside it was the old green watering can, with the crack by the spout, still a little stained from the rice water. I did not cry in front of anyone that week. I took the can home, and the next morning, before the sun came over the roofs of my building, I washed the rice, poured the cloudy water into a jar, and went out on my little balcony with the can.
[MEMORY5] I do the same thing every morning now. I pour the water slowly, at the base of each pot, the way she did. I do not talk to the plants much. But when the morning glories open, I stop for a moment, and I think of her hands, and I feel that she is standing next to me on the balcony. That is all. It is a small thing, and it is the thing I still do for her.
[RECAP] So, let us remember everything. Number one, for your plants. Wash the rice in cool water, and keep the cloudy first rinse. Dilute it one part rice water to one part plain water. Pour it slowly around the edge of the pot, once every one or two weeks, and never on a plant with wet roots or on a cactus. Use it fresh, or let it turn a little sour for a day or two in the shade, but throw it out if it smells rotten or grows mold, and keep the jar cold if you are not using it. Number two, the greasy pot. Let it soak, then wash with soap, and let the pan cool first.
[RECAP] Number three, bamboo shoots, boiled with rice bran or rice wash water to take away the bitterness. Number four, starch for a tablecloth, dried flat and ironed while it is still damp. Number five, the hair rinse, light and smooth, with no promises. Number six, the face, after a patch test on the wrist, and never on broken skin. And number seven, the wooden cutting board, washed with hot soapy water after raw meat, and never trusted to rice water alone.

Look at the bottom leaves, the top of the soil, and the drainage hole. Write down what you see in a notebook, or on the back of an envelope, just like I write in mine. Then wash the rice, save the first rinse in a clean jar, and dilute it one to one. Pour it on one plant only, the one you love most. Two weeks from now, come back and look again. You do not need a scientist to see a change in a leaf. You only need to look.
