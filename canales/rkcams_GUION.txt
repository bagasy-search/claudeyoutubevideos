Number one. The side door.

Most people think the front door is the door. You lock the front, you check it twice, you feel safe. And the front door is usually the one with the best lock, because it is the one the builder thought about. The side door is the one that goes to the garage, or the laundry room, or the yard where the trash cans are. It has the cheapest hardware in the house, and it is the one you use forty times a day without really looking at it.

Here is what I actually found the morning after. A split jamb. Wood that had been cracked along the grain, right where the strike plate sits, and the screws in it were the short ones that came in the box with the door. Three quarters of an inch long, going into a frame that needed three inches of bite. The whole door looked fine from the outside. The paint was perfect. But the jamb had nothing to hold on to.

Now, the mechanism, in simple terms. A deadbolt is only as strong as the screws that hold the strike plate to the frame. The lock can be excellent and the door can still give way if the wood around it gives way. That is what a split jamb is. It is not a lock problem. It is a wood problem with a lock on it.

How do you check yours tonight? Stand inside, close the side door, and look at the gap between the door and the frame along the latch side. Then look at the strike plate. Are the screws the same length as the screw next to them? Are any of them loose, or sitting proud of the metal? Put your thumb on the frame and push. If it gives, or you see a crack that runs up from the plate, you have found it.

The fix. For zero dollars, go out tonight and tighten every screw in that plate, then check that they are all seated flush. For about twenty-five dollars, buy long screws, the three inch kind that go through the frame and into the stud behind it, and replace the short ones. Only the screws. Do not overthink it. That one swap is worth more than any camera on the market.

Here is the line I want you to remember. A short screw is a three-dollar problem that looks like a security system.

Number three. The sliding patio door.

Every sliding door I ever worked on had the same story. A nice big glass door, a wonderful view of the yard, and a little metal bar lying in the bottom track. The bar is supposed to stop the door from sliding open even if somebody lifts it out of the track. In half the houses I visited, there was no bar. In the other half, there was a bar, but it was an old broom handle, or a piece of two-by-four that was too short to reach the frame.

Here is what I actually found. A patio door in a ranch house in a quiet subdivision, sliding on a track worn down by thirty years of sand. The owner had a lock on the handle that looked good. But the track had so much play that the door lifted almost half an inch. You could see it with your eyes. You could feel it with your hand. And the lock was doing nothing, because the door was not sitting in the frame the way it was built to.

The mechanism is simple. A sliding door has two jobs: to keep the glass from moving sideways, and to keep it from lifting out of the track. The handle lock only does the first job. The second job is the bar and the rollers and the track itself.

How do you check yours tonight? Put your hands on the door near the handle and lift gently. Do you feel movement, or a clunk? Look at the bottom track. Is there sand, grit, or a little metal lip that has broken off? Is the bar in place, and is it long enough to actually reach the frame when the door is closed?

For zero dollars, clean the track with a stiff brush and a vacuum. Put the bar in place and cut it to length if it is too long. For about twenty-five dollars, buy a proper security bar or a track insert that fills the gap at the bottom. For about sixty, if the rollers are worn, replace them. A worn roller is a slow problem that becomes a fast one.
