In 1935, a New South Wales entomologist named Walter Froggatt lobbied the government against bringing cane toads into Queensland. He warned that a toad that eats almost anything could become as great a pest as the rabbit.

They treated him like a crank. The sugar growers wanted the toads, the newspapers took their side, and the scientists who backed the plan called Froggatt pessimistic and biased.

So a sugar researcher brought one hundred and two cane toads from Hawaii, and in August 1935 their children, about two thousand four hundred of them, were released in the cane fields to eat the beetles.

In December 1935, the government banned any further releases, because of the warnings. Nine months later the ban was lifted. The growers had won.

Today there are an estimated two hundred million of them, still moving west, up to sixty kilometres a year. Froggatt was right. So why did nobody stop them? Today I'm opening the whole file: who did it, who warned them, and who is still paying.

You know me. I'm Walt. Forty years in the field, most of it looking at animals that were exactly where they belonged, and I've seen this kind of mistake twice. This one has the biggest bill.

I'm sitting in my field-station office right now. There's a coffee mug next to me that I should have washed by now.

Every file on this channel is a true story. The sources are in the description, all of them.

Let's start with the problem they had, because it's easy to forget how bad it was.

Northern Queensland grows sugar cane. In the 1930s, cane was the money crop up there, and the growers were losing a lot of it to beetles.

The farmers called the young beetles cane grubs. They live underground and eat the crop from below, where you don't see the damage until it's already done.

Imagine you've put your life into a field, and something you can't even see is eating it. Now imagine the mill needs your cane next season, and the bank wants its money.

To be fair to the people involved, they had real reasons. The beetles were real. The grubs were real. The crops were being lost year after year. That's the part people skip, and it's exactly why the mistake was so easy to make.

Somebody came up with a predator: a toad that eats beetles. Biologists have a name for this, biological control. You bring an animal in from somewhere else to fight a pest. On paper it looks tidy.

There were reports from Hawaii and Puerto Rico that cane toads there were eating the beetle pests in sugar fields. For the people in Queensland, that was enough. Nobody had to prove it would work in their own fields. Somebody else had said it worked, and that was the whole argument.

In June 1935, Reginald Mungomery, an entomologist working for the Queensland Bureau of Sugar Experiment Stations, went to Hawaii to get some. The Bureau's job was to help the sugar industry grow. That's a government office with a strong interest in the answer being yes.

He caught 102 cane toads. According to one account, that was fifty-one females and fifty-one males. Keep that number in your head, because it's the number in the title.

They were packed into two cases for the trip. The cases left Honolulu on the third of June and reached Gordonvale, in north Queensland, on the twenty-second, almost three weeks later. One of the males had died on the way. So there were one hundred and one of them alive when they arrived.

They were kept at a government experiment farm near Gordonvale, and they bred. That's what cane toads are good at.

Think about what the Bureau had that winter: about a hundred toads, a government farm, and a sugar harvest that wouldn't wait. The plan needed the toads to work quickly. The record doesn't show anybody asking the one question that mattered: what happens when the toad stops eating beetles and starts eating everything else?

Let me be exact, because this is where the story usually gets garbled. The one hundred and two were the breeders. The animals that went into the field were their offspring.

On the nineteenth of August, 1935, about two thousand four hundred of those offspring were released into sites around Gordonvale. Mungomery and his junior colleague, James Buzacott, put a large batch into the Little Mulgrave River.

Two thousand four hundred is more than twenty times the number they started with. The title's number is the breeder count. I'm saying it plainly so nobody can say I hid it.

The warning next.

There was one man who spoke up. His name was Walter Froggatt, an entomologist from New South Wales, the state next to Queensland.

Froggatt looked at the plan and called it a mistake before it happened. In 1935, he went to the government and argued against it. He was a lone voice, and that's a lonely place to stand. Being right early is a lonely business. The people who are wrong usually have the microphones.

It wasn't a popular position. The cane growers were for it. So were the politicians, the leading scientists, the premier of Queensland, and in the end the prime minister. Froggatt was up against all of them.

But he got through to someone. He lobbied the Commonwealth Department of Health, and in December 1935 the Director-General of Health banned any further release of toads.

The timing matters. The ban came in December. The big release was in August. The horse was already out of the barn.

A year later, in 1936, Froggatt put his warning in print, in The Australian Naturalist. "This great toad, immune from enemies, omnivorous in its habits, and breeding all the year round, may become as great a pest as the rabbit or cactus."

I want to be careful about one thing. Froggatt's printed words came out in 1936, not 1935. His 1935 work was the lobbying, and that's what got the ban. Both things are true, and I'm telling you so you can check it yourself.

Immune from enemies. Three words, and that's the whole problem. Nothing in Australia was going to eat this thing.

Look at what that sentence was doing. He wasn't calling the toad a nuisance. He was saying there was nothing left in the country to stop it, and he said so in print a year after the release had already happened.

The sugar side didn't let it go. The Bureau and the cane growers pushed the Queensland government, and the Queensland government took the case to the Prime Minister, Joseph Lyons.

In September 1936, Lyons rescinded the ban. The growers had won. The toads were allowed to go wherever they could walk.

Froggatt paid for his position. Some newspapers called his views decidedly pessimistic, and some scientists accused him of an incurable bias. He had said a plan was wrong, and the people who liked the plan were not in a forgiving mood.

Count them: one hundred and two imported, about two thousand four hundred released, a clear warning on the record, and a ban that lasted about nine months. That's the first act. Here's where it goes wrong.

Did the toad at least do the job? The whole point of the toad was to eat beetles.

According to the scientific record, there is no evidence that the toads affected the number of cane beetles they were brought in to eat. Read that again. The toad didn't make a dent in the bugs.

The record doesn't show anybody testing whether the toads even ate the beetles before they were released. A plan that looks clean on paper doesn't have toads in it. Paper has never been hungry.

You put your reputation behind a plan. You spend the money and a few years. Then the thing you bet on doesn't do what you promised, and the thing nobody predicted shows up everywhere.

Because a cane toad is not a picky eater. It eats insects, lizards, small mammals, and whatever else fits in its mouth.

Its real weapon is chemical. Adult toads have large glands on their shoulders, and when they feel threatened they secrete a toxin. That toxin often kills the native animals that try to eat them.

Nobody on the government side seemed to plan for that. The animals of this continent never had to learn what to do about a poisonous toad.

Plainly, if a native animal eats a toad, it gets poisoned. If it has never met a toad before, it has no idea it shouldn't. Evolution doesn't move that fast, and the toads never gave it time.

Put the first act together. One hundred and two toads were the parents. About two thousand four hundred of their children went into the field. The government said stop, and then it said go. And the grubs, the whole reason for the exercise, were still eating the cane. A 1936 account already called the toad an unsuccessful control for the grubs that kept damaging the sugar crops.

Let's go year by year, because the numbers tell it better than I can. The toads never stopped at the edge of the cane. Each year they got further from the office that had decided they were useful.

In 1994, the frog expert Michael Tyler, in his book on Australian frogs, put the western advance at about forty kilometres a year.

In 2001, the invasion front reached the wetlands of Kakadu National Park, in the Northern Territory. Wetlands are exactly where cane toads breed, because their eggs go into fresh water. A national park full of water was an easy place for them to take hold.

In 2011, toads had become established in the irrigation country around Kununurra, in the north of Western Australia. That same year, the Sydney Morning Herald reported that the national population was estimated at more than two hundred million.

Two hundred million is not a number you can count. You estimate it, and the estimate is the best anyone has. I'd rather tell you that than invent a precise figure.

In 2014, researchers estimated the western front had sped up to sixty kilometres a year. That's about thirty-seven miles. Every year. In one direction.

The arithmetic that keeps me up is simple. Ten years at that pace is six hundred kilometres. Every generation of toads starts from wherever the last one ended, and none of them need a permit.

By 2022, cane toads were turning up in the suburbs of Sydney. That's a long way from the cane fields. A modelling study that year said toads were already in the Kimberley and advancing toward the Pilbara, another large part of Western Australia.

The walking isn't finished. The official line from a national museum is that the toad is continuing to spread west very rapidly. I don't need a museum to tell me that. I need only look at the map on my wall. Plans like that come with a ribbon and a photograph. Nobody photographs the animals they cost.

Who is paying is the next question, and the animals pay first.

The yellow-spotted monitor is a big, handsome lizard. In some local populations, the numbers dropped by up to ninety percent after the toads arrived. That's not a dip. That's a collapse.

The northern quoll is a small spotted marsupial that lives in the north. Researchers have recorded precipitous declines in its populations where the toads moved in.

Two species of crocodile and around seventy kinds of freshwater turtle were also found to be at risk. Each one is a whole family of animals that had no say in the matter.

Snakes are more complicated. Some species have been reported with smaller jaws. And in some places, the red-bellied black snake built up resistance to the toxin in under seventy-five years.

Seventy-five years is an eye-blink for evolution, and the snake paid for it. Nature can adapt. It just doesn't do it on the schedule a politician wants.

Some animals learned a trick. Native water rats, called rakali, have learned to eat the toad's hearts and livers, and skip the skin and the glands. Nobody asked the rats to be clever. They got there on their own.

Ask a monitor what it thinks of the plan. It can't tell you, and that's the point. Nobody let the monitors vote.

But for every animal that learns a trick, others don't. And the toads aren't waiting around for anyone to catch up.

Cane toads breed all year round. Their eggs are laid as very long strings in nearly all fresh water. A pond, a drain, a puddle after the rain. If there's water, there's a nursery.

The spread wasn't only walking. A study by researchers at Macquarie University found that most toads moved long distances on commercial trucks, carrying landscaping and building materials. At least fifty toads a year were arriving in Sydney that way.

The toad got a ride. Somebody put it in the truck, and somebody else didn't check the load.

Notice the words "at least" in that study. That's researchers being careful, and it's the only honest way to count a toad that's riding in the back of a truck.

Who paid? The sugar growers were supposed to be the ones who benefited. Instead they got a new plague, and the beetle problem the toad was meant to solve is still there.

The native animals paid. The monitors, the quolls, the turtles, the crocodiles, the snakes. Every one of them paid for a decision that wasn't theirs.

The neighbours paid too, in ways that are harder to put a number on. Ponds that fill with toads. People who never asked for any of it.

Who got a say? The growers had a say. The premier had one, and so did the prime minister. The monitors didn't, the turtles didn't, and the rats didn't. That's the whole story in one sentence, and I don't need a second one.

Let me be fair about the scale of this. A toad is small. A toad population is a continent-wide problem, and it doesn't care whether your county is lucky. I'm not asking you to hate the people of 1935. I'm asking you to notice how they decided.

What's being done? People try. In 2011, the Sydney Morning Herald reported thousands of toads killed in a single event called a Toad Day Out. In Townsville, a 2015 toad catch weighed 143.6 kilograms, about 317 pounds.

Those are real numbers, and they're real work. But a single event in a single town is a drop in the pond. Killing toads by hand doesn't stop a species that is spreading across a country about as big as the contiguous United States, and that breeds all year long.

Some projects protect particular threatened animals in small areas. Those are worth doing. They're just small.

The plain problem with every fix I know of is scale. Each one works on a few square kilometres. The toads work on a continent. You can win a small fight every week and still lose the war.

Researchers have also tried taste-aversion baits, to teach quolls to avoid toads. In 2023, field research in the Kimberley found those baits were not effective in reducing the toad-linked quoll decline.

In late 2025, a US biotech company said a toad-resistant quoll could be born in two to four years. That's a plan, not a result. A plan like that needs labs, money, and years of field work before anyone knows if it works in the wild. I'll tell you when I see a result. I've seen plans before.

I'll believe the quoll is safe when a quoll tells me so. Until then I'll keep reading the field reports, and I'll read them slowly.

What doesn't work is pretending one decision can be taken back. You can't un-release two thousand four hundred animals with a phone call. You can only manage what's already there.

A clever plan beats a boring warning every time. The warning is a list of things that might go wrong. The plan is a picture of everything going right. Only one of those two things gets a budget.

Here's what forty years and this file taught me. The people in 1935 weren't stupid. They had a problem, a plan that looked clean on paper, and one warning from one man, which they overruled.

The warning was right where it counted. The toad was immune from enemies. It bred all year round. And it became the kind of pest Froggatt compared to the rabbit and the cactus.

The lesson fits in one sentence. Nature doesn't read your business plan.

People hate that sentence. They think I'm saying nature is clever, or cruel. I'm not. Nature is indifferent to what looked good in the meeting, and that's worse.

Before you let something loose to fix a problem, ask what else it will eat, what will eat it, and whether anyone checked. Somebody always has a good reason. Nature never has one at all.
