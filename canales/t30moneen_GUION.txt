Almost four thousand years ago, in the richest city in the world, two friends sat on a low wall watching the king's caravan go by. One of them built chariots. The other played the lyre. Both of them worked from sunup to sundown. Both of them were good at what they did. And both of them had empty purses.

And one of them asked a question you may have asked yourself at some point: "Why is it that I work so hard, and there's never anything left over?"

That question is the opening of a little book written about a hundred years ago, a collection of stories set in ancient Babylon. It's called The Richest Man in Babylon. George Clason wrote it, and banks used to hand it out as free pamphlets. Today it's one of the most widely read money books in history. And the amazing thing is that what it teaches hasn't gone out of style. Not even a little.

In this video I'm going to walk you through the seven rules the book calls "the seven cures for a lean purse." I'll tell them through the story, with its characters, so they actually stick. And at the end I'll tell you about the mistake the richest man in Babylon made when he was young, because it's the same mistake almost all of us make with the first money we ever manage to save.

Let's start with the story.

The chariot builder was named Bansir. The musician was named Kobbi. And that morning, watching the caravan, they remembered an old childhood friend. A boy who'd played with them in the same streets, gone to the same school, who was no smarter and no stronger than they were. His name was Arkad. And now he was the richest man in all of Babylon.

"How can that be?" they asked each other. "We all started out the same. What does he know that we don't?"

So they did something a lot of people never do. They went and asked him.

Arkad welcomed them in. And he told them his story.

When he was young, Arkad worked carving words into clay tablets. He earned very little. One day, an old and very wealthy moneylender named Algamish ordered an urgent job from him. Arkad said he'd work all night on it, if in exchange Algamish would teach him the secret of his fortune. He worked until dawn. And when he handed over the tablet, the old man gave him a single sentence.

"I found the road to wealth when I decided that a part of all I earn is mine to keep."

Arkad laughed. He thought it was a joke. What do you mean, a part of what I earn is mine? Everything I earn is mine! But the old man explained. "No. You pay the baker, the tailor, the man who sells you your sandals. You pay everybody, except yourself. You work for everyone, and nothing is left for you."

That's the first rule in the book. And it's the foundation of everything else.

Rule number one: start your purse to fattening. For every ten coins that come in, keep one. One out of every ten. Every time. Before you spend on anything else.

It sounds small. And that's exactly why it works. Arkad explained it to his friends like this: if you put ten coins in your purse and only take out nine, the purse starts to grow. Slowly, but it grows. And the strange thing, Arkad said, is that you don't even feel the difference. You live on nine almost the same as you lived on ten. But the purse, which was always empty before, starts to get heavy.

The key is the order. You don't save what's left over, because nothing is ever left over. You save first. And you live on what remains.

Rule number two: control your expenses.

Whenever Arkad taught this to the people who came to learn from him, someone always pushed back. "But I can't save one coin out of ten. Everything I earn goes to the necessities."

And Arkad answered with something that's as true today as it was then. What we call "necessary expenses" always grow until they match what we earn. If you get a raise, a few months later you're short again. Not because prices went up that much. But because, without noticing, we start confusing what we need with what we want.

The book's advice is simple. Make a list of everything you spend. And look at each item honestly. Is this a need, or is it a want? Wants never end. There's always one more. But you can choose which ones to satisfy, and you pay for them out of the nine coins. Never out of the tenth.

Think about your own month for a second. The coffee you buy on the way somewhere, the subscription you forgot you still pay for, the thing you ordered late at night because it was on sale. None of those is a crime. Arkad never says you should live like a monk. What he says is that you should be the one deciding. Write it down, look at it, and pick on purpose. Most people who try this for the first time are surprised. Not because they find one huge expense, but because they find a dozen little ones they never actually chose. They just happened. And every one of those little leaks is a coin that could have gone into the purse.

Arkad's point is that a budget isn't a cage. It's a map. It shows you where your coins are going, so you can send them where you actually want them to go.

Rule number three: make your gold multiply.

This is the part most people skip. Saving is great. But a pile of coins sitting in a drawer doesn't grow. It just sits there.

Arkad explained that money can be like an army of workers. Every coin you put to work brings other coins back to you. And those coins can be put to work too. And they bring back more. He called it the children of your gold, and the children of the children. Today we call it compound interest. But the idea is exactly the same, and it's four thousand years old.

Arkad said his first profit was small. But instead of spending it, he put it back to work. And he did that year after year. Until one day, what his coins brought him was more than he earned with his own two hands.

Rule number four: guard your treasure from loss.

And here's the mistake I promised you.

When Arkad saved up his first real money, after a whole year of keeping one coin out of every ten, he trusted it to someone he knew. A man named Azmur, who made bricks. Azmur was traveling far away, to the land of the Phoenicians, and he told Arkad that over there he could buy precious jewels at a good price, and when he came back they'd sell them in Babylon and split a big profit.

What happened? The Phoenicians sold Azmur pieces of colored glass. And Arkad lost everything he'd saved in a year.

When he went to tell old Algamish, the moneylender didn't feel sorry for him. He said, "Whoever heard of asking a brickmaker about jewels? If you want to know about bread, ask the baker. If you want to know about jewels, ask the jeweler."

That's the lesson. The first money you save is the hardest money you'll ever save. And it's exactly the money that's easiest to lose, because you're anxious to make it grow fast. The book says: before you trust your money to anything, ask someone who truly knows that business. Choose what's safe, even if it pays less, over what's promising but could cost you everything. Promises of huge, fast profits almost always hide a brickmaker buying glass.

The book drives this home with another story, one of my favorites. It's about a spear maker named Rodan. One day, Rodan does a great job for the king, and the king rewards him with fifty pieces of gold. More money than Rodan has ever had in his life. And suddenly, everybody wants a piece of it. His sister wants him to lend it to her husband so he can open a shop. Friends show up with "can't miss" opportunities. Everybody has a plan for Rodan's gold.

Rodan doesn't know what to do, so he goes to see an old gold lender named Mathon. And Mathon, instead of giving him a lecture, opens a box. Inside that box are little tokens, one for every loan he's ever made. And next to each token, Mathon tells the story of what happened.

Here's one, he says, from a farmer who borrowed to buy cattle, and paid me back with interest, because he knew exactly what he was doing. Here's another, from a young man who borrowed to chase a dream he knew nothing about, and never paid back a single coin. Here's one from a woman whose husband borrowed against her jewelry. Some tokens brought money back. Others brought nothing but regret.

And the lesson Mathon gives Rodan is simple. Before you lend or invest a single coin, ask yourself: will this money come back to me? Does the person really know this business? Is there something solid behind the promise? If you can't answer those questions clearly, keep your gold where it's safe.

Mathon puts it something like this: it's better to be a little too careful than to end up with a great deal of regret. And Rodan, in the end, decides to protect his gold instead of handing it to the first person with a good story. It's the same lesson Arkad learned the hard way with Azmur and the colored glass. Only Rodan had the sense to ask before he lost it.

And notice something. Neither Arkad nor Mathon tells you to hide your money under the mattress and never use it. Remember rule three. Money should work. The point is that it should work somewhere you understand, with people who know what they're doing, where it can actually come back to you. Growth and safety, together. Not one without the other.

Rule number five: make your home a profitable investment.

In the Babylon of the book, a lot of people lived in rented houses, paying a lot, in places where their wives didn't have a single corner to plant some flowers and their children had nowhere to play. Arkad told them that when you own your own place, part of what used to go to rent starts staying with you. And on top of that, the family lives happier.

Today every country and every person has their own situation, and it isn't always possible. But the underlying idea still stands: what you pay to live somewhere, if you can manage it, should be building something that's yours.

Rule number six: insure a future income.

This one is for those of us who've got some years on us. Arkad said we're all going to grow old, if we're lucky. And that a wise man prepares, while he's young, the purse he's going to need when he can't work anymore. And also the purse his family will need if he's no longer around.

It doesn't say you should live in fear. It says something simpler: the day you can't work with your hands anymore, your coins have to keep working for you. And that day gets prepared for today, not on that day.

Rule number seven: increase your ability to earn.

And this is the last one, and for a lot of people, the most important. Arkad told his students that the desire to earn more has to come with something concrete. It's not enough to want to be rich. You have to get better at what you do. Learn more. Do your work with more care than everyone else. That way you're worth more, and you get paid more.

And he added something beautiful. Pay your debts as fast as you can. Take care of your family. Make a will, so your affairs are in order. And treat the people who've had less luck than you with respect and kindness. Wealth, Arkad said, isn't just gold. It's also being a man people can trust.

So, to make it stick, the seven cures for a lean purse. One: keep one coin out of every ten, before anything else. Two: control your spending and separate needs from wants. Three: put your money to work, so it has children, and the children have children. Four: protect it, and ask someone who knows before you risk it. Five: if you can, make your home an investment and not just an expense. Six: start building your future income today. And seven: learn, improve, and become more valuable at what you do.

And what happened to Bansir and Kobbi, the two friends on the wall?

They left Arkad's house deep in thought. And I always picture them walking home, with Bansir saying it all sounded too simple, and Kobbi answering that Arkad hadn't given them any secret at all. He'd given them something harder: a rule you have to follow every single day, even when nobody's watching.

Because that's what makes this book so special. It doesn't promise to make you rich in a month. It doesn't sell you a trick. It tells you that wealth gets built one coin out of ten, over years, with patience, without getting fooled. It's boring. And that's exactly why it works.
