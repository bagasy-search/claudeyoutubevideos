In 1890, one man let about sixty European starlings loose in Central Park, to honor Shakespeare, or so the story goes. Today North America holds roughly a hundred and forty million of their descendants. Farmers pay for them every year.

From sixty birds to a hundred and forty million is a multiplication of more than two million times. Nobody who opened that cage was planning for that.

The starling eats grain and feed meant for animals. It also takes the nest holes that native birds need, and it doesn't wait politely for them to leave. So the bill lands in two places: on the farmer's grain, and on the bluebird that lost its box.

You know me, I'm Walt. I've spent forty years in the field, and I've seen this kind of mistake twice. Right now I'm at my desk, and the coffee mug in front of me hasn't been washed since spring. Every file on this channel is a true story. The sources are in the description.

Today I'm opening the whole file. Who started it, what happened year by year, who is still paying, and at the end, what is working now.

Let's go back to 1890.

The man was Eugene Schieffelin. He was a New Yorker who belonged to the American Acclimatization Society. That society existed to move plants and animals from one part of the world to another and see what would take hold. Its members called it progress. The birds were the cargo.

The Society had tried starlings before. Its members released them once in the 1870s, and that attempt didn't take. So the idea was not new. It was waiting for another try, and the 1890 release was that try.

The birds came over from England, and they arrived dazed after long weeks on a ship. When they were let out, they barely moved. The Central Park birds were not an impressive start.

In 1891, Schieffelin released about forty more, the next spring. About sixty, then about forty. That's about a hundred birds in all, by the Park Service's own count. That is the whole event that started the bird we are living with today: two releases, from one man, in one city.

So here's where we are. One society, one bird, and a few dozen starlings let out in one city. Not a disaster yet. Just a decision that looked small.

Now, the story everybody repeats.

The popular version goes like this. A man who loved Shakespeare wanted every bird named in the plays to live in America. The starling was one of them, so he brought it over. It's a neat story, and it sounds like something an enthusiast would do.

Shakespeare does mention a starling, one time. It's in Henry the Fourth, Part One, a single line. That is the entire literary connection anybody can find in the plays.

The idea that Schieffelin was trying to bring every Shakespeare bird to America does not show up in the record at the time. It appears in the late 1940s. Edwin Way Teale presented it as fact in a 1947 essay, and he repeated it in a book of essays called Days Without Time, in 1948. He gave no evidence for it.

Sixty years is a long time for a story to wait for proof. A story that waited that long deserves a hard look before anybody repeats it at dinner.

In 2021, two researchers, Lauren Fugate and John Miller, went looking. They did not find evidence that Schieffelin was the Shakespeare fan the story describes. Miller called the popular story essentially fiction. That is a strong word, and it's his word, not mine. The record that can be found starts in the 1940s, and no recorded words from Schieffelin support the tale.

Think about why a story like that sticks. It has a romantic villain, a famous writer, and a reason that sounds almost noble. It explains the bird in one sentence. Facts are harder to carry around. A starling was released, it multiplied, and nobody wrote down a good reason for the second release, so the people who came later filled the gap with a better one.

That's what I'd call a case file with the middle missing. You can tell the ending. You can't prove the beginning.

Put the two versions next to each other. The story says a poet's wish drove the whole thing. The record says a society's hobby and one man's two releases. The first version makes a better movie. The second one is the one that happened.

So what do we actually know? A society wanted the bird here. A man released the birds in a park. The birds lived, and then they spread.

Now, the question I want you to answer for yourself. Why would a group of educated people think letting in a new animal was harmless? Because they thought nature was a shelf. You take a thing off one shelf and put it on another, and it stays where you put it.

Nature doesn't work like a shelf.

Every animal in a place fits into a web of other animals. Something eats it, something competes with it, something lives in the holes it doesn't use. Put a new animal in the middle of that web and everything around it moves. Sometimes the change is small. Sometimes it's the start of a long fight.

Now the spread, year by year, because that's where the bill starts to grow.

By 1928, according to a USDA review of starling biology, the birds had reached the Mississippi River. Thirty-eight years after the Central Park release, they had crossed half the country, and they were still moving.

In 1929, the National Park Service says starlings had reached southeastern Minnesota. That's the middle of the continent, a long way from a park in New York. The NPS wording is simple, and it's worth remembering: a few dozen birds, one city, and then the middle of the country.

In 1942, the same USDA review says starlings were observed on the West Coast. That's the far edge of the continent, reached in about half a century.

The detail that should bother you most is this. A genetic study cited in that USDA review concludes that all the starlings in North America descend from the New York City colony. Every one of them traces back to the birds in Central Park. The park was not a footnote. It was the whole family.

Put the numbers side by side so they stay in your head. Sixty birds in 1890. The Mississippi by 1928. Minnesota in 1929. The West Coast by 1942. Each one is a few years of the same bird, going where nobody had asked it to go. And here's the checked math: from the Central Park release in 1890 to the West Coast in 1942 is fifty-two years. That's a long time for a person and a short time for a plan.

Now the honest part. The estimate for North America at the peak is about two hundred million birds, according to the National Park Service, which gives no year for it. The USDA review puts the peak at about two hundred million as well, and says the population has since declined to about a hundred and forty million. Either way, the number is in the millions, and nobody planned for that many.

This is the checked arithmetic on those two numbers. Two hundred million down to a hundred and forty million is a drop of sixty million, which is three in ten of the peak. A drop of three in ten birds does not mean a three-in-ten smaller bill. The bill was never only about the head count. It was about where the birds ended up, and what they ate when they got there.

Starlings also do some good, and I won't pretend they don't. The University of Illinois Extension says they eat many kinds of invertebrates, including insects, snails and worms. It also says starlings could be important for controlling some crop pests, though that has not been quantified. Walk a field in the right season and you'll see them working. That's the part of the story nobody puts on a poster.

But the same extension page lists fruits, berries and grains in their diet, and it says crop damage, including fruit and grain, totals millions of dollars every year. The grain stored for the animals that feed us is the part a farmer feels first. A pest is a pest when the bill comes in, and the bill is where this story gets expensive.

Picture what that looks like on the ground. Starlings move in flocks, and they like the same things farmers store: grain, cattle feed, barns with open doors. Nobody on those farms was asked whether they wanted company.

Take one farm, a plain one with a grain bin and a row of barns. Nobody on that farm invited the birds, and nobody there ever voted on the idea. The farmer finds the grain gone, the feed scattered, the bin lid chewed. He can't send a letter to the man who released the birds in 1890. He can only buy more feed and get up earlier the next morning.

The money behind that farmer is a simple formula. The most-quoted figure is about eight hundred million dollars a year in damage to American agriculture. A USDA-National Wildlife Research Center review from 2007 states that Pimentel and colleagues estimated the damage at $800 million a year, based on $5 per hectare. Five dollars a hectare is about two dollars an acre. Multiply that across the crop land that starlings reach, and you get the big number. I could not confirm the starling breakdown in the 2000 paper itself, so treat the figure as the best one we have, not a receipt.

Eight hundred million a year is a big number. Spread over a whole country, it's a bill that comes in every year and never gets paid off.

The second bill is quieter. Starlings nest in holes. They take the holes that woodpeckers dig and that bluebirds need. In a few places, the starling has pushed native birds out of nest sites.

Bluebird lovers will tell you the starling is a bully, and in one way they're right. A Texas bird atlas describes a starling competing for cavities even with woodpeckers that have only just dug a hole, and it describes starlings taking over the nests of native birds. That's the behavior. It's real, and you can watch it happen in a yard.

The research on that is more mixed than the anger about it. A 2003 study looked at twenty-seven native species and found only sapsuckers clearly losing out. Most species seemed to hold their own. So the displacement is real, but the population damage is smaller than the behavior suggests.

I don't like to overstate things, and I'm not going to. Starlings did not wipe out bluebirds. They made life harder for some of them, in some places, and that's enough to matter.

Who is paying today? The farmer, first, because a starling doesn't need much. It eats a little from a lot of places, so it can show up wherever people grow or store food, and when a flock finds your grain, it doesn't leave a note. Then the homeowner with starlings in the attic. Then the native bird that loses its nest hole, because the starling got there first and didn't leave.

The homeowner pays too, and it's usually a small bill that comes at a bad time. A nest in the attic, a mess in the eaves, a repair crew on a Saturday. Nobody writes a headline about that. It just adds up, house by house.

Nobody pays the man who released the birds. He's been dead for a long time. That's how this kind of story usually works.

Here's the arithmetic I'd give any farmer. If the eight-hundred-million figure was even roughly right in 2000, and it stayed in that range, then the same bill over the twenty-six years since adds up to more than twenty billion dollars. That's a rough multiplication, not a finding. Take it as a warning, not a receipt.

Every committee loves a plan that ends on the day it's approved.

The people who write these plans are not stupid. Most of them are smart, friendly, and sure of themselves. They are also far away from the consequences, and that distance is the problem. The meeting was very polite. The bill was not.

A plan with a budget for the birds and nothing for the cleanup isn't a plan. It's a wish with a letterhead.

Look around this office. Specimen jars on the shelves. A wall map with pins in it, and handwritten notes I can't always read anymore. A pile of old reports I should have thrown out years ago, and a paperweight shaped like a bald eagle that nobody has ever explained to me. Every pin is a place somebody decided was fine. Most of them weren't.

I keep those reports because the people who wrote them were usually right, and the people who read them were usually in a hurry. Power loves a ribbon-cutting. The cleanup comes later, and nobody invites the cleaners.

Now, what is working? Not much at the continent scale. Nobody has removed starlings from North America, and nobody is going to.

What works is local. Farms that keep feed covered and barn doors closed have fewer starling problems. Homeowners who block the holes in the attic have fewer nests in the eaves. Some places use nets, noise, and scare tactics on particular crops, for a while. None of it is a cure, but it's the stuff that actually lowers the bill.

Think of it like a barn with a hole in the roof. You can patch the hole. You can move the hay. You can put a tarp over the feed bin. Each one helps, and none of them brings back the grain you lost. That's what local work looks like. It's small, it's steady, and it counts.

What doesn't work is pretending the bird is going to leave by itself. It isn't. It's been here for well over a century and covers most of the continent. Anybody who tells you the problem will fix itself has not been out in a field in February with a clipboard.

Here is the other part, which people don't like hearing. The solution is boring. It's maintenance, like a leaky roof. You don't fix it once and walk away.

Here is the timeline in plain form. In 1890, about sixty birds went into a park in New York. In 1891, about forty more. By 1928 they had reached the Mississippi. By 1929, Minnesota. By 1942, the West Coast. And for the whole time, the bill has come due every year.

If you're keeping score, that's more than a hundred and thirty years of the same argument, with the same birds, and the same farmers on the losing side.

Back to the question I asked at the start. How did a few smart men with good intentions do this? My answer, after forty years, is not very flattering to smart men. Good intentions don't count for much when you have no way to pull the thing back.

And why did nobody stop them? Because nobody could see the bill yet. The damage came later, in small pieces, spread across farms that were not in the park.

Think about who was in the room. Men who liked birds, men who liked the idea of a grand collection, men who wanted their country to look like the one they had left. Nobody in that room had to drive out to a feedlot in a snowstorm and count what was left of the grain. The people who would pay were not invited to the meeting.

That's not a conspiracy. It's worse than that. It's ordinary. Good people can make a bad decision when the cost falls on somebody they never meet.

I've seen the same pattern with many animals and many plants. The person who makes the choice goes home to a quiet evening. The person who inherits the choice goes out in the morning and starts the work of cleaning it up.

The bird spread across the continent. It ate some insects and a lot of grain. It pushed out some native nesters. The bill came every year, and the person who started it was long gone.

Now here's why this matters to you today, not only to a farmer in some other century. Every year somebody somewhere proposes to move a species, a plant or a fish, because it looks useful, pretty, or profitable. The proposal usually comes with a good story attached. Good stories are cheap. The cleanup is expensive, and it lands on people who were never in the meeting.

Before you let any new animal, plant or fish loose somewhere, ask three questions. Does it have anything that eats it where it's going? Does it have anything it competes with? And who pays when it works too well?

If nobody can answer the third question, don't do it.
