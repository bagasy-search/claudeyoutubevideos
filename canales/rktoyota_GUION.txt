If you drive a Toyota with a push button start, your key is talking to the street all night long. Right now, sitting on the kitchen counter, it's sending out a little radio signal every few seconds, waiting for the car to answer. And the car is sitting in the driveway, listening.

That's how the car knows you're close when you walk up and grab the handle. It's a nice feature. But anybody standing near your house with the right equipment can hear that conversation too. And there's a way to tell your key to stop talking. It's two buttons, in a certain order, and it takes about five seconds.

Toyota built it into a lot of their smart keys, and most owners have never heard of it. Tonight I'm going to show you exactly how to do it, how to check that it worked, which keys have it, what to do if yours doesn't, and three more things every Toyota owner should know before bed.

First, I want to say this clearly. This is not a Toyota problem. Every car with a keyless entry system works this way, from every brand. Toyota just happens to make some of the most popular cars in America, so a lot of you are driving one. And Toyota is one of the brands that gave owners a simple way to put the key to sleep. That's a good thing. They just didn't tell many people about it.

Let me explain what's going on, because once you understand it, the fix makes sense.

Your smart key doesn't just wait for you to press a button. It's always listening for the car. When you walk up to the car, the car sends out a short-range signal, and your key answers back with a code. The car checks the code, and if it matches, it lets you open the door and start the engine. You never take the key out of your pocket.

The problem is that this conversation can be stretched. Thieves use a pair of radio devices, usually called a relay. One person stands near your house, as close to where the key is as they can get. The other stands next to your car. The device by the house picks up your key's answer and passes it along to the device by the car. The car thinks the key is right there, and it opens up. Sometimes it starts, too.

I'm not going to go into how those devices work, and you shouldn't need to. What matters is that they need your key to answer. If your key is asleep, there's nothing to pass along. That's the whole trick.

So here's how you put a Toyota smart key to sleep. Toyota calls it battery-saving mode. It was made to save the key's battery when you're not using the car for a while, but it does something else too. It turns off the part of the key that answers the car.

Hold the key in your hand. Press and hold the lock button. While you're holding the lock button down, press the unlock button twice. Then let go.

If it worked, the little light on the key will blink four times. That's the key telling you it's going to sleep.

Now, while it's asleep, the key won't answer the car. If you walk up and grab the door handle, nothing happens. That's what you want overnight. In the morning, just press any button on the key, and it wakes right up. Lock, unlock, any one. Then it works like normal again.

So the routine is simple. You come home, you walk in the door, you put the key to sleep. Lock, hold, unlock twice, four blinks. In the morning, press any button. That's it.

Now, how do you know if your key has this? Here's the honest answer. A lot of Toyota and Lexus smart keys from roughly the last ten to fifteen years have battery-saving mode. Not all of them. It depends on the model and the year, and Toyota has changed key designs over time.

The easiest way to find out is to just try it. Lock, hold, unlock twice. If the light blinks four times, you've got it. If it doesn't, try once more, slowly, and make sure you're holding the lock button the whole time. If it still doesn't blink, your key may not have the feature, or it may work a little differently.

The second way is your owner's manual. Look in the index under smart key, or battery-saving mode, or electronic key. If your key has it, the manual will show you exactly how. If you don't have the paper manual anymore, Toyota keeps the owner's manuals for most models on their website, for free. Look up your model and year.

And the third way is to call your dealer's service department and ask. They hear this question a lot now. It's a two-minute phone call.

Now let me show you how to test that it really worked, because I don't want you to trust the blinking light alone.

Put the key to sleep. Walk out to the car with the key in your pocket. Grab the driver's door handle like you normally would. If the door doesn't unlock, good, the key is asleep. Now press any button on the key, wait a second, and grab the handle again. It should open. Do that test once, and you'll know it works on your key.

Here's something important to remember. When the key is asleep, the push button start won't work either. That's on purpose. So if you put it to sleep and then forget, and you get in the car in the morning and it says key not detected, don't panic. Just press any button on the key and try again.

So what if your key doesn't have battery-saving mode? Then you still have good options, and they're cheap.

The first is a signal-blocking pouch or box. They're sometimes called Faraday bags. You put the key inside, close it, and no signal gets in or out. You can buy a pouch for ten or fifteen dollars, or a little metal box with a lid for about the same. Keep it by the door where you drop your keys.

But here's the thing. Some of them don't work. I've tested a few that let the signal right through. So test yours. Put the key in the pouch, close it all the way, walk out to the car, and grab the handle. If the door unlocks, your pouch is not doing its job. Get a different one.

And here's a free trick you probably already have in the kitchen. An old cookie tin, the metal kind with a tight lid. Put the key inside, close it, and do the same test. A lot of times, that works just as well as anything you'd buy. Same goes for the microwave, but please don't keep your keys in the microwave. Somebody will turn it on.

The second option is free too. Keep the key as far from the outside walls and the driveway as you can. Not in a bowl by the front door. Not on a hook by the garage. Put it in a drawer in the middle of the house, or upstairs. Distance makes a relay attack much harder. It doesn't make it impossible, but it helps.

Now, three more things every Toyota owner should know, and they apply to most other cars too.

Number one. A steering wheel lock. I know, it looks old-fashioned. But a thief doing a relay attack is counting on being in and out in under a minute, quietly. When he looks through the window and sees a big bright steering wheel lock, a lot of the time he just moves on to the next car. They cost thirty to fifty dollars. I'd put one on any car that sits outside overnight.

Number two. Park smart. If you have a garage, use it, even if it means cleaning it out on a Saturday. A car in a closed garage is a lot harder to steal than a car in the driveway. And if you park in the driveway, try to park nose in, close to the house, with the light on. Thieves don't like light.

Number three. Turn on any extra security your car already has. A lot of newer Toyotas have a connected app that can send you an alert if the car is moved, or let you see where it is. Some have an option for a security PIN or extra confirmation before starting. Look at your app, or ask your dealer what your car has. Most people never set any of it up.

And one more, a locksmith habit. Your spare key. Where is it? If it's in a drawer by the front door, it's talking to the car too. Put the spare to sleep as well, or keep it in a metal box. A spare key in a bowl by the door is just as good to a thief as the one in your pocket.
