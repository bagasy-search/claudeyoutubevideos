Those tiny bright red dots on your chest, the ones that look like somebody flicked drops of red paint on your skin, are not a disease, they are not a sign that your liver is failing, and they are almost never cancer. But there is one kind of red dot that looks a lot like them and means you should call your doctor today, not next month.

I'm Dr. Emmett Rowe, and I get asked about these dots more than almost anything else. Somebody unbuttons their shirt in my office, points at their chest or their belly, and says, "Doctor, these keep popping up. Every year there are more of them. What is wrong with my blood?" Some people have three or four. Some have fifty. They can be as small as a pinhead or as big as a pencil eraser. And a lot of people have been counting them in the bathroom mirror for years without ever asking anyone, because it feels embarrassing to bring up.

So today I want to take that worry away, show you exactly what these spots are and why they appear, and then give you the few signs that really matter, so you know the difference between a harmless red dot and one that needs a doctor.

Let's start with the name. These little red bumps are called cherry angiomas. You may also hear them called senile angiomas, or Campbell de Morgan spots, after the doctor who described them more than a hundred and fifty years ago. The word angioma sounds scary, but all it means is a small growth made of blood vessels. And cherry just describes the color.

Here is what is actually happening under your skin. Your skin is full of tiny blood vessels called capillaries, thinner than a hair. With time, in some spots, a little cluster of those capillaries grows and widens and bunches together, right under the surface. Because it is packed with blood, you see it as a bright red dot. That's all it is. A little knot of normal blood vessels in the wrong place. It is not a blood clot. It is not a broken vessel. And it is not blood leaking out.

They are incredibly common. Most people start getting a few after the age of thirty, and by the time we reach our seventies, the majority of us have at least some. So if you have them, you are in very good company.

Now let me describe what they usually look like, so you can compare with your own.

A cherry angioma is round or slightly oval. The color is bright red, cherry red, sometimes a deeper red or even purple as it gets older. When it is new, it is usually flat and tiny, like a pinpoint. With the years, many of them become a little dome, smooth and shiny, like a tiny drop of red wax sitting on the skin. They show up most on the chest, the belly, the back and the upper arms, the trunk of the body. And they usually don't hurt and don't itch.

Here is one little test I show my patients. Take a clear drinking glass and press it gently against the spot. A lot of cherry angiomas get a bit paler under the pressure, because you are squeezing the blood out of those little vessels for a moment. Keep that glass in mind, because we are going to come back to it, and it becomes very important in a few minutes.

So why do they appear? The honest answer is that the biggest reason is simply age. The years change the way those tiny vessels grow. The second reason is your family. If your mother or your father had a lot of red dots, there is a good chance you will too. Hormones can play a part, which is why some women notice new ones during pregnancy. And a few medicines and chemical exposures have been linked to them, but for most people, it is just time and genes.

And I want to clear up three myths, because I hear them all the time.

Myth number one. Red dots mean your liver is bad. This one scares a lot of people, and here's where it comes from. There is a different spot, called a spider angioma, that can be connected to liver problems. A spider angioma has a red center with thin red lines coming out of it, like the legs of a spider. That is not the same thing as a round cherry dot. Cherry angiomas by themselves are not a sign of liver disease.

Myth number two. They are caused by something you ate, or by drinking too much, or by not being clean. No. They are not about your diet, they are not about hygiene, and they are not contagious. You cannot catch them and you cannot give them to your grandchildren.

Myth number three. They turn into cancer. Cherry angiomas are benign. Benign means not cancer. They do not turn into skin cancer over time. They may grow a little bit, they may get darker, and new ones may keep appearing, but that is their normal behavior.

People also ask me if they can prevent them. Honestly, not really. There is no cream, no vitamin and no special diet that has been shown to stop cherry angiomas from appearing. Sunscreen is still a great habit for your skin in general, and it protects you from the spots that really are dangerous, but it won't stop these little red dots. So please don't spend your money on a product that promises to make them vanish. If someone is selling you a bottle that dissolves red spots overnight, keep your wallet in your pocket.

So if they're harmless, why am I making a whole video about them? Because of the lookalikes. There are a few red and purple spots that can look similar at a quick glance in the mirror, and one of them in particular should never be ignored.

The first one, and the most important one, is called petechiae. Petechiae are tiny red or purple dots, flat, like pinpoints, that appear because a little blood has leaked out under the skin. And here's the key difference. Cherry angiomas show up one by one, slowly, over months and years. Petechiae usually show up all at once, in a cluster, sometimes over a few hours or a couple of days, often on the legs and ankles, or on the chest.

Now remember the glass. When you press a clear glass on petechiae, they do not fade at all. They stay exactly the same color. Because the blood is not inside a vessel anymore, you can't squeeze it away.

Petechiae can come from something simple, like hard coughing or straining. But they can also be a sign that your blood is not clotting the way it should. That can happen with low platelets, with some infections, and sometimes with blood thinners and other medicines that many people over sixty take every day.

So my rule is simple. If a crop of tiny flat red or purple dots appears suddenly, and they don't fade under a glass, call your doctor that same day. And if those dots come with a fever, with feeling very sick, with new bruises you can't explain, with bleeding gums or nosebleeds, or with blood in your urine, don't wait for an appointment. Get seen right away. That's not a cherry angioma. That is your body asking for help.

Let me paint you a picture, because this happens more often than you'd think. A patient of mine, let's call her Ruth, seventy-four years old, a retired school librarian. For years she had dozens of little red dots on her chest, and they never bothered her. One Sunday evening, getting ready for bed, she noticed new tiny dots on both shins, flat, like someone had sprinkled red pepper on her skin. She also had a bruise on her arm she didn't remember getting. Her first thought was, "Oh, it's just more of my red spots."

But she remembered her spots were round, raised, and had come one at a time over twenty years. These were different. So she pressed a glass on her leg, and the dots didn't fade. She called the after-hours line that night. It turned out one of her medications had dropped her platelets. They changed it, and she was fine. If she had waited until her yearly checkup, it could have been a very different story.

The difference was not that Ruth knew medicine. The difference was that Ruth knew her own skin.

Now let's talk about the other lookalikes, quickly.

The spider angioma I already mentioned. Red center, little legs. One or two can be normal, especially in pregnancy. But if you notice several new ones on your face, neck or chest, mention it to your doctor, because he or she may want to check your liver.

Then there is a spot called a pyogenic granuloma. The name is confusing, because it is not an infection. It is a red bump that grows fast, over a few weeks, looks raw and wet, and bleeds with the slightest touch. It is not dangerous in itself, but it should be looked at and usually removed.

And there is a rare one I don't want to skip. Some skin cancers, including a type of melanoma, can be red or pink instead of brown. They are uncommon, but they exist. What gives them away is behavior. They grow, they change, they bleed on their own, or they look different from every other spot you have.

So here are the warning signs. Show a red spot to a doctor if it grows quickly over weeks. If it changes shape, or the edge becomes irregular. If it turns black or blue. If it bleeds again and again without being scratched. If it becomes an open sore that doesn't heal. Or if it is the one spot that looks different from all the others. Dermatologists call that the ugly duckling sign, and it is one of the most useful rules you can learn.

And one more. If dozens of new cherry angiomas appear suddenly, over just a few weeks or months, mention it at your next visit. Most of the time it means nothing, but a sudden eruption is worth a conversation and a basic checkup.

Now, what about bleeding? Because this is the part that frightens people. A cherry angioma is made of blood vessels, so if you catch one with a razor, a fingernail, a bra strap or a towel, it can bleed a surprising amount. It looks dramatic, but it is usually easy to stop. Take a clean cloth or gauze, press firmly on the spot, and hold the pressure for ten full minutes without peeking. If you take blood thinners, it may take longer. If it keeps bleeding after twenty minutes of steady pressure, then it's time to get help.

That brings me to the thing I really want to warn you about. What people do at home.

I have seen patients who tried to burn these spots off with a hot needle. I have seen people cut them with nail clippers, tie a thread around them, put apple cider vinegar or garlic on them, or buy one of those online remover pens that burns the skin. Please don't. You are cutting into a little ball of blood vessels, so it bleeds. You can get an infection, and you can leave a white scar that looks worse than the red dot ever did. And the worst part? If that spot was one of the rare lookalikes, you just destroyed the evidence a doctor needed to see.

So what if they bother you? Maybe one catches on your clothes, maybe it bleeds every time you shave, or maybe you just don't like how it looks. That's a perfectly good reason to ask. A doctor or a dermatologist can remove them safely in the office, usually in a few minutes. The common options are a tiny electric needle that seals the vessels, a laser that targets the red color, freezing, or a small shave removal. Most insurance plans consider it cosmetic, so ask about the cost before you book. And if there is any doubt about a spot, the doctor can send it to the lab.

And what happens when you do go in to have a spot checked? It's much simpler than people imagine. The doctor will look at it under good light, often with a small handheld magnifier with a light built in, called a dermatoscope. Under that lens, a cherry angioma looks like a little cluster of round red or purple pockets, almost like a bunch of tiny grapes. That picture is so typical that most of the time the doctor knows what it is in a few seconds. No needles, no blood test, no drama. And if you show up with your monthly photos, you just made that doctor's job a lot easier.

So let's put this into a simple routine.

Once a month, after a shower, stand in good light in front of a mirror. Look at your chest, your belly, your arms, and ask someone you trust to look at your back. You're not counting the red dots. You're looking for anything new, anything growing fast, anything bleeding, anything that is the ugly duckling.

And here's the trick that makes this work. Take photos. Take one picture of your chest and one of your back, and a close-up of any spot you're unsure about, with a coin next to it for size. Next month, take the same pictures and compare. Change is very hard to see from memory, and very easy to see in a photo.

Keep a clear glass in the bathroom cabinet. If you ever notice a sudden crop of tiny flat red dots, press the glass on them. If they don't fade, you know what to do.

Let me leave you with the big picture. Those tiny red dots on your chest are, for almost everyone, just a sign that your skin has been around for a while. They're not your liver, they're not something you ate, they're not contagious, and they don't turn into cancer. But they share the neighborhood with a few spots that are serious. So know your skin, take your photos, respect the glass test, and never cut, burn or pick at a spot a doctor hasn't looked at.
