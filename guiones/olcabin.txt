Well, now. Come sit down, friend. This old tin box on my shelf holds twenty-five dishes that used to fill every cabin kitchen up here in the north woods, and hardly anybody makes them now. One of them has been through a bath of lye. And number one is a bread I can ruin in exactly one way, and I'll show you that way before we're done. Pour yourself a cup of coffee, and let's start at the bottom of the box.

Number twenty-five. Egg coffee. Yes, an egg. In the coffee. The Scandinavians who settled Minnesota and the Dakotas mixed a raw egg into the grounds with a splash of water, dropped the whole mess into boiling water, and let it boil until the foam settled. Then a half cup of cold water, and every ground sinks to the bottom like a stone. The egg grabs the bitterness and hauls it down with the grounds. It was church basement coffee, smooth as a creek stone, and you never taste the egg. Not one bit.

Number twenty-four. Fattigmann. That's Norwegian for poor man, and I'll tell you, the name is a joke. The dough is egg yolks, cream, sugar and a splash of brandy, and none of that was cheap. You roll it thin, cut long diamonds, slit the middle and pull one end through, then drop them in hot fat at about three-fifty for a minute and a half. Cardamom, powdered sugar, and a coffee beside it. Poor man's cookie, rich man's ingredients. Go figure.

Quick word, friend, while I've got you. Everything I cook in this kitchen, I've been writing down: every recipe, every measure, and the reason under every step. It's a cookbook now, fifty old camp recipes, and a few of the dishes in this countdown are in it. If you're on the TV, point your phone at this code. Number twenty-three. Pickled herring. I know, I know. Hear me out. Vinegar, sugar and water, brought to a boil with allspice and peppercorns, poured over herring fillets layered with red onion and lemon in a jar. Give it a couple of days in the cold. Then you set a piece on a cracker with a dab of sour cream. Some Swedish-American families call the first time a new in-law tries it a trial by herring. Finish the cracker, and you're family.

Number twenty-two. Fruit soup. Prunes, dried apricots, raisins and dried apples, simmered with tapioca, cinnamon, a little sugar and a squeeze of lemon, until the whole pot goes glossy as stained glass. You eat it in a bowl, warm or cold, at Christmas, and it's a dessert, not a soup, so don't argue with me. The Norwegians will tell you the Swedes make theirs with the pale fruit and the Norwegians use the dark, and that argument is older than my stove.

Now, some of you are writing to say you still make these. Good! Then you're the reason they're not gone. Tell me in the comments what your family did different, because I promise your grandmother's version was better than mine. And hold on to your hat, because number seven has been through a lye bath.

Number twenty-one. Krumkake. The word means curved cake. A thin batter goes onto a patterned iron, and the second it comes off you roll it around a wooden cone while it's still hot, and it sets up crisp and lacy. Fill it with whipped cream and fruit and eat it before it shatters. Those old irons were made to sit over the fire, before there was such a thing as a modern stove. If you find one at an estate sale, buy it.

Number twenty. Leipäjuusto. Finnish bread cheese. Up in Michigan's Upper Peninsula they call it juustoa, and it squeaks against your teeth. It's a fresh cheese pressed flat and then baked or broiled until it gets brown freckles all over the top. The old way used the first milk of a cow that had just calved. You eat it warm with coffee and a spoon of jam. The story goes a mother would serve coffee and cheese to a suitor, and if he liked the cheese, well, you can guess how that ended.

Number nineteen. Nisu, the Finnish cardamom bread. Over in the Upper Peninsula they still use the old word, nisu, which just meant wheat back in the old country. It's a soft, sweet dough scented with crushed cardamom seed, braided in three strands, brushed with egg and sprinkled with sugar. Slice it thick, butter it, and set it next to a hot cup of coffee. That's a whole afternoon right there.

Number eighteen. Rice porridge. The Norwegians say risgrøt, the Swedes say risgrynsgröt, and on Christmas Eve a family hides one blanched almond in the pot. Whoever finds it in their bowl wins a little marzipan pig, and gets to open a present first. Mine's the plain version. Half a cup of long-grain rice, four cups of whole milk, a third of a cup of sugar, and time. Bare simmer, stir every five minutes, forty to fifty minutes, and pull it while it still looks a little loose, because it keeps thickening as it cools. Butter, vanilla and cinnamon at the very end. This is a page from the book, page sixty. Rice, milk, time. Stir it and it turns to cream.

Number seventeen. Dried-apple pie. Think about a logging camp in February. No fresh apples for months, but the cook has a sack of dried ones. The old records list dried apples, tea and butter as luxuries in the early camps, so that pie meant something. Simmer eight ounces of dried apples in two cups of cider with brown sugar and cinnamon for fifteen minutes, thicken it with a spoon of cornstarch, and bake it between two lard crusts in a cast-iron skillet at four hundred for thirty-five to forty minutes. That one's in the book too, page fifty-nine. And wait till you see number four, a porridge that pours its own butter back on top.

Number sixteen. Potato sausage. Ground pork and beef, boiled potatoes and onion, salt, pepper, allspice, stuffed into casings and simmered gently, never fried hard. It came to the Midwest with the Swedes in the eighteen hundreds, and the potato let a family stretch the meat further. You serve it next to meatballs and lingonberries, with a little mustard on the side. There's an old Scandinavian shop on East Lake Street in Minneapolis where folks still line up for it at Christmas. That tells you something.

Number fifteen. Bannock. Not Scandinavian either, it's straight north-country trail bread. Flour, baking powder, salt, a little fat, and water, patted into a skillet. The Ojibwe call it ba'wezhiganag, and Scottish fur traders brought a version of it into the north country, and it fed the voyageurs on the long paddle. Mine's two cups of flour, a tablespoon of baking powder, half a teaspoon of salt, two spoons of lard, and three-quarters of a cup of water. Medium-low heat, six or seven minutes a side, and cover the pan for the first side, so the outside doesn't burn before the middle bakes. Page twenty-three.

Number fourteen. Kringle. Danish bakers brought it to Racine, Wisconsin, and Racine bakeries were selling it by nineteen twenty-two. It's a big flat oval of butter pastry, folded into paper-thin layers around a filling of pecan or almond or apple, and iced on top. Wisconsin made it the state pastry in twenty thirteen, which you'd think would keep it from being forgotten. Now go ask a young person to make one at home.

Now look at the box, friend, before we go on. Lefse, fattigmann, krumkake, rømmegrøt and lutefisk crossed the ocean from Norway. The herring, the meatballs, the limpa and the potato sausage came out of Sweden. Nisu, pannukakku, mojakka, leipäjuusto and that rutabaga casserole are Finnish. The kringle is Danish, the booyah is Belgian, and the pasty is Cornish. Every one of them ended up in the same north woods, at the same long table. That's the real recipe. Nobody up here ever cooked just one people's food. And number one is still waiting on that one mistake.

Number thirteen. Sausage and sauerkraut. Every people that settled the north woods had some way to carry cabbage through a hard winter, and this is the skillet where it landed. Brown a pound and a half of smoked sausage six to eight minutes. Cook a sliced onion in the same pan, add two sliced apples, a spoon of brown sugar and a teaspoon of caraway, and stir in a pound of lightly drained kraut with half a cup of cider. Nestle the sausage in, cover, and simmer low for twenty minutes. Boiled potatoes and plenty of mustard. This is a page from the book, page forty-three. The apple is the trick. It softens the sour without hiding it.

Number twelve. Mojakka. That's the Finnish-American stew from Minnesota, Wisconsin and Michigan, beef and potatoes, or fish and potatoes, and up on the Iron Range they'll fight you over which one is right. Beef is lihamojakka, fish is kalamojakka. One story says the word comes from lumber camp workers asking the cook for more Jacob's stew, more Jacob, mo-Jacob. I can't prove it. I like it anyway. Potatoes, onion, a hot bowl, and a cold day.

Number eleven. Wild rice hotdish. Wild rice is manoomin to the Ojibwe, who harvested it for centuries, and in nineteen seventy-seven Minnesota made it the state grain. Hotdish itself was first printed in a nineteen thirty cookbook from the Grace Lutheran Ladies Aid in Mankato. The recipe called for hamburger, macaroni and canned peas. Nowadays plenty of folks make it with wild rice. Brown some meat, fold in cooked wild rice, a can of soup, celery and onion, and bake it until the top goes crusty. It tastes like a Sunday after church.

Number ten. Swedish meatballs. Now, in twenty eighteen Sweden's own official account said the recipe came from King Charles the Twelfth, who brought it home from Turkey in the early seventeen hundreds. Plenty of folks argue about that. I don't care. I know the pan. Ground beef and pork, a little onion, allspice, breadcrumbs soaked in milk, browned slow in butter, then a cream gravy scraped up from the bottom of that same skillet. Serve them with potatoes and lingonberries. And hang on, because number three is a whole dinner that miners carried down a shaft.

Number nine. Lanttulaatikko. That's Finnish for rutabaga casserole. Boil the rutabaga soft and mash it with a little cream, breadcrumbs, an egg, butter and a pinch of nutmeg, then bake it about an hour until the top goes lightly brown. Now most of you are making a face. I get it. A rutabaga isn't a pretty vegetable. But that sweet, earthy mash is what the Finns put on the Christmas table. Try it once before you decide.

Number eight. Pannukakku. A thick, creamy, baked custard pancake, the star of a Finnish breakfast in the Upper Peninsula. Melt butter in a cast-iron skillet in a hot oven, pour in a batter of eggs, milk, flour, sugar and a little cardamom, and watch it puff up at the edges and go golden. It sinks in the middle when it comes out. That's how you know it's right. Cut it in squares, put a berry on the top, and nobody talks for a full minute.

Now why did all this fade? That's my opinion, not a statistic. These dishes took a whole day, or a special iron, or a kettle and a crew. Supper turned into something one person does alone in twenty minutes. The ones that lived, lived because a church kitchen made them once a year. So if you take one thing from my box, take a Saturday and a helper.

Number seven. Lutefisk. Here we go. Dried cod, soaked in a lye solution, then soaked and rinsed in fresh cold water until the lye is gone. Some folks say the Vikings invented it after a fire and a rainstorm. I say somebody was very hungry. It comes out white and jiggly as a jelly, and you bake or steam it and pour melted butter all over. Now, I'll be honest, friend, it's an acquired taste. But look at this. Lutheran churches across Minnesota still put on a lutefisk dinner every winter, and the halls fill up. Somebody loves it. The rest of us eat the potatoes and lefse.

Number six. Limpa. A soft Swedish rye bread, sweetened with brown sugar and molasses, and spiced with anise, caraway and fennel, and a little orange peel. It's baked mostly at Christmas, and the smell in a cabin kitchen when that loaf comes out is the whole reason people put up with the winter. Slice it thin. Butter it thick. Top it with cheese or a piece of that pickled herring from number twenty-three, if you're brave.

Number five. Salt-pork fish chowder. Not Scandinavian, friend, pure logging-camp northwoods, and it's in the book. Every cabin on a cold lake had a pot for it. Render four ounces of diced salt pork eight minutes. Cook a chopped onion in the fat, add a pound and a half of cubed potatoes and two cups of water, and simmer until they're nearly tender. Nestle a pound and a half of firm white fish on top, six to eight minutes, then pour in three cups of milk and a cup of cream, and heat it until it steams. Do not boil it. That one's in the book, page thirty-four.

Number four. Rømmegrøt. Sour cream porridge. Rømme is sour cream, grøt is porridge, and it was too rich to eat any old Tuesday. It was for weddings, and baptisms, and the harvest. Here's how it's done. Two cups of full-fat sour cream in a heavy pot, simmered about five minutes, until the fat starts to separate. Sift in three-quarters of a cup of flour, whisking hard. Keep cooking, and the butterfat rises to the top. Skim it off and save it. Now whisk in two cups of hot milk, a half teaspoon of salt, and cook until it's thick and glossy. Pour it in a bowl, make a well in the middle, pour the butter back in, and dust it with cinnamon sugar. The porridge crowns itself with its own cream. Now that's a bowl worth waiting for. My whole cabin used to go quiet over this one, and I never did figure out if it was the cinnamon or the butter. I say it's the butter.

Number three. The pasty. Cornish miners brought it to the Iron Range, and many of them had worked first in the copper and iron mines of Michigan. It was hearty, and portable, and cheap, a whole dinner in a crust you could carry down a shaft. Then the Finns showed up and put rutabaga in it, and that's the version I love. The filling is beef in small chunks, diced potato, diced rutabaga, and onion, with salt and pepper and nothing else. Cornwall's rule says skirt steak. Up here plenty of folks grind the beef, and I won't tell if you do. For the crust, two and a half cups of flour, a teaspoon of salt, a cup of cold lard and six to eight tablespoons of ice water. Roll it into rounds the size of a dinner plate, pile the filling on half, fold it over, and crimp the edge along the side in a D shape. Cut a slit. Bake it in a hot oven at four hundred for fifteen minutes, then down to three-fifty for about forty-five. Eat it with your hands. Ketchup, or gravy, depending on which side of the Range you were raised.

Number two. Booyah. It's a thick stew that started with the Belgian immigrants who settled around Green Bay and Door County, and the name is probably how somebody spelled the French word for broth. Chicken, beef and pork, sometimes all three, and carrots, potatoes, celery, onions, peas and rutabaga, with the spices tied up in a cheesecloth bag. In a church parking lot they cook it in a kettle of fifty gallons or more, and it can take two days and a crew of cooks. At your house, do it like this. A whole stewing chicken and two pounds of beef shank, covered with cold water, simmered slow for about three hours. Pull the meat, chop it, and put it back with the vegetables and the spice bag for the last hour. Nobody eats one bowl. Season it at the end, taste it, and let it sit off the heat a while. It's better the next day, the way beans are. Booyah is the reason a whole town shows up on a Saturday, and it's also the reason there are leftovers to send home with everybody.

And now, number one. Lefse. Norwegian potato flatbread, soft as a dish towel, and cooked on a big flat griddle. It goes back to medieval Norway, and wheat wouldn't grow well in the north, so cooks made do with what they had. When the potato arrived in the seventeen hundreds, the recipe changed, and the immigrants carried it to the north woods in the eighteen hundreds. Here's how it goes. Boil about one and three-quarter pounds of russet potatoes and push them through a ricer while they're still hot. Stir in five tablespoons of butter, a quarter cup of heavy cream, two teaspoons of sugar and a teaspoon and a half of salt. Now here's the one way to ruin it. Roll it before it's cold, and the dough sticks and tears. So chill it overnight, cold all the way through. The next day, work in a cup and a half of flour, and make it quick. Roll each ball paper thin with a grooved lefse pin, slide the lefse stick under it, and lay it on a hot dry griddle. Little brown freckles, about a minute a side on a griddle that's good and hot, flip with the stick, and stack it between clean towels so it stays soft. Butter and sugar, roll it up, and eat it right there. Two weekends before Christmas, in a church basement, a whole row of women and, lately, some men roll lefse side by side. The bread itself is just potato and flour. The rest is everyone standing at the table together.
