So the first thing I do every fall is ask one question about my coop. Is it dry?
Here's where the wet comes from. Every hen breathes out water all night long. Twelve hens breathing in a closed-up coop make a lot of moisture. Their droppings are wet too. And if your waterer is inside, that's more. All that wet air goes up, and if it can't get out, it settles on the walls and the roof, and it freezes there, and it freezes on their combs.
That's what frostbite is, mostly. Wet air and cold combs.
So the coop has to breathe. My coop has vents up high, under the roof, on both ends, covered with hardware cloth. They stay open all winter. Even at fifteen below. The wet air rises and goes right out the top, and the roost sits lower, below those vents, so there's no wind blowing across the girls while they sleep.
That's the difference between ventilation and a draft. Ventilation is up high, above their heads. A draft is down at roost level, blowing on them. You want the first one, and you don't want the second one.
Here's how I check for a draft. I go out on a windy evening and hold a strip of ribbon, or a lit candle in a jar, right where the hens sit on the roost. If the ribbon flutters or the flame leans, there's a draft. Then I find the crack and close it. But I never close the vents up high.
And the smell test I've told you about before. If you walk in on a cold morning and you can smell ammonia, or the inside of the windows is covered in frost, the coop isn't breathing enough. [emphatically] Open it up more, not less.
