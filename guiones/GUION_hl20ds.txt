I spent forty winters up a pole in a storm, fixing lines so folks could flip a switch and get warm. Now I’m retired, and I’ve learned that keeping your house warm when the grid goes down doesn’t take a generator or a pile of expensive gear.

You can do it with about twenty dollars of simple supplies, stuff you can grab at any hardware store or dollar shop. I’m talking about keeping one room livable for you and your family when the power’s been out for days. Not the whole house. One room. That’s the secret right there.

Pick your room before the storm hits. You want the smallest room you can comfortably sleep in. A bedroom, a den, maybe a walk-in closet if you’re really tight on space. The smaller the space, the easier it is to hold heat. Stay away from rooms with big picture windows or sliding glass doors. If you’ve got a room with one window and a door that closes tight, that’s your winner. The room should be on an interior wall if possible, not a corner room that gets slapped by wind on two sides. If your only option has two outside walls, don’t worry. We’ll deal with that.

Once you’ve got your room, you seal it. I mean seal it like you’re trying to keep smoke out. Roll up a bath towel or an old blanket and shove it tight against the bottom of the door. That cold air seeps in like water through a crack in a boat. If the door leads to a hallway, you can hang a heavy blanket over the doorframe using thumbtacks or a tension rod. Make a curtain. Every layer stops heat from sneaking out.

Now the window. Even closed and locked, a window leaks. You need a window insulation kit. This is a few bucks at any hardware store. It’s a sheet of clear plastic you cut to size and tape around the window frame, then you hit it with a hair dryer to shrink it tight. If the power’s already out and you can’t use a hair dryer, just tape the plastic as smooth as you can. Even a wrinkled sheet stops a draft. No kit? Use a heavy-duty clear garbage bag and some masking tape. It’s not pretty, but you’re not decorating a cake. You’re staying alive.

If the room has an unused electrical outlet on an outside wall, you’d be surprised how much cold air pushes through those little slots. Take a childproof outlet plug, the plastic kind you stick in to keep kids safe, and put it in every unused outlet. No plugs? Fold a piece of duct tape over the slots. Just don’t tape over an outlet you need to use.

Next, the floor. Heat sinks right down through a bare floor, especially if you’re on a slab or over an unheated crawlspace. Lay down rugs, blankets, cardboard, even layers of newspaper. Cardboard is great. Break down some shipping boxes and tape them together to cover as much of the floor as you can. Put a blanket on top. Now your feet aren’t losing heat to the ground every time you stand up.

Now you’ve got a sealed box. But a sealed empty box is still cold. You need to trap your own heat. Your body puts out about as much warmth as a hundred-watt light bulb. You want to capture that and keep it close.

This is where the tent or blanket fort comes in. You can set up a small dome tent right on the floor of the room. A two-person backpacking tent is perfect. The mesh and fabric layers trap your body heat in a tiny bubble. If you don’t have a tent, you build a fort. Drape blankets over a table, over chairs pulled together, over a rope tied from one wall to another. Make a low ceiling. You want just enough space to lie down and sit up. The smaller the air pocket, the faster your body warms it. Climb inside with your sleeping bag or blankets. If you’re with another person or a kid, even better. Share that space. Shared body heat is free and it works.

Keep your head inside the tent or fort. Wear a beanie, a knit cap, something that covers your ears. You lose a huge amount of heat off your head. I’ve worked in bucket trucks with a wind chill of thirty below, and a dry wool cap on my head made the difference between finishing the job and tapping out.

Now let’s talk about hot water bottles. This is an old trick that works like a charm. You need a sturdy water bottle, like a Nalgene or a metal canteen. Nothing thin that will melt or split. Heat water on a camp stove outside, or on a grill, or however you’re safely heating water. Never, and I mean never, bring a camp stove inside to heat water. We’ll get to that. Once the water is hot but not boiling, fill the bottle, screw the cap on tight, and wrap it in a towel or a sock. Put that bundle inside your sleeping bag or under your blankets near your core. Your chest, your belly, between your thighs where the big arteries run. That bottle will radiate heat for hours. You can also use a hot water bottle the old-fashioned way, the rubber kind with a stopper. Same idea. Wrap it so you don’t burn your skin.

If you’re awake and moving around the room, fill a couple of bottles and stick them in your jacket pockets, or hold one against your lower back. It’s amazing how a little heat in the right spot can change your whole outlook.

Dress in layers. Not one thick sweater. Layers. Start with a snug base layer that wicks moisture. Cotton is rotten for this. If you sweat even a little, cotton gets damp and then you’re cold. Use synthetic long underwear or wool if you have it. Then a looser middle layer, like a fleece or a flannel shirt. Then an outer layer that blocks drafts. Same for your legs. Long johns, then loose pants. Two pairs of socks, but make sure your boots or shoes aren’t tight. Tight footwear cuts off circulation and makes your feet colder.

Eat something before you go to sleep. Your body needs fuel to generate heat. A handful of nuts, a granola bar, something with fat and protein. Digestion warms you up. Drink warm liquids if you can. Even a cup of hot water with a tea bag in it will raise your core temperature and help you relax.

Now I’m going to say the hard stuff, and you need to listen. This is where people die. Every winter, every single winter, I read about families who tried to heat their homes with a charcoal grill or a generator in the garage or a gas oven with the door open. They don’t wake up. Carbon monoxide is odorless and colorless. It puts you to sleep and you never come back. Do not bring a charcoal grill inside your house. Not in the room, not in the living room, not in the attached garage with the door open. Charcoal makes carbon monoxide for hours after the coals look dead. Do not use a propane heater that isn’t rated for indoor use. Even the ones that say indoor safe need ventilation. Read the label. If it says outdoor use only, that label was written because someone died. Do not run a generator inside your house, inside your garage, on your porch right next to an open window. A generator needs to be at least twenty feet from any door or window, and the exhaust pointed away. Do not use your gas oven or stovetop as a heater. That’s not what it’s for. The combustion byproducts will fill your house.

Candles. A candle gives a little light and a tiny bit of heat, but it’s an open flame. If you light a candle inside a blanket fort, you’re asking for a fire. If you light a candle and fall asleep, it can tip over. If you have pets or kids, they knock things over. A candle inside a tent is a death trap. If you must use one for light, put it in a sturdy jar on a non-flammable surface, far from anything that can fall on it, and blow it out before you sleep. Better yet, use battery-powered LED lights. A pack of LED tea lights costs a couple bucks and lasts for days on one set of batteries. No fire risk.

If you have a fireplace or a wood stove that’s properly installed and you know the chimney is clean, use it. But even then, don’t stuff it full of paper and garbage. Burn seasoned wood. Keep a window cracked an inch to let in combustion air so smoke doesn’t backdraft into the room. Have a carbon monoxide detector with fresh batteries in the room where you’re sleeping. If you don’t own one, buy one today. They cost less than a pizza and they save your life. I’ve wired houses where the only reason the family woke up was that little plastic box screaming on the wall.

Back to staying warm. If you wake up in the night cold, don’t just lie there shivering. Get up. Move your arms and legs. Do some squats, swing your arms, get the blood moving. Then climb back into your warm nest. Shivering is your body’s way of telling you it’s losing the battle. Help it out.

In the morning, if the sun is out and it’s warmer outside than inside, open the curtains on that one sealed window and let the sun hit the room. Even weak winter sun adds heat. As soon as the sun goes down, cover the window back up with your blanket or plastic.

Keep the door to your warm room closed at all times. Every time you open it, you dump your heat into the rest of the cold house. If you have to go to the bathroom or get supplies, be quick. Treat that door like the hatch on a submarine.

If you have a dog or a cat, bring them into the room with you. A pet is a living space heater. A medium-sized dog puts out a surprising amount of warmth. Just keep them inside the blanket fort with you. They’ll appreciate it too.

Now let’s talk about the twenty-dollar setup. Here’s your list. A window insulation kit, a roll of duct tape, a couple of heavy-duty clear garbage bags if you skip the kit. A pack of childproof outlet plugs. A cheap foam sleeping pad or a yoga mat to insulate you from the floor. If you don’t have a tent, a few spring clamps or binder clips to hold blankets to chair backs and table edges. A sturdy hot water bottle, or a Nalgene-type bottle you already own. A roll of masking tape. That’s it. You probably have half of this already. The point is you don’t need a thousand-dollar generator and ten gallons of gas to survive a cold snap. You need a small room, some plastic, some blankets, and a clear head.

I want you to try this setup before you need it. On a cold evening, pick your room, seal it up, build your fort, and sleep in it. See what works and what needs tweaking. You don’t want to be figuring this out in the dark with frozen fingers while the wind is howling. Practice now. It’s like putting on your climbing hooks before you’re at the top of the pole.

Remember, this is about one room. Don’t try to heat the whole house. Don’t try to keep the pipes from freezing with a candle in the basement. That’s a different problem. This is about keeping you and your family warm through a multi-day outage. Focus on the small bubble of heat you can control.
