I spent forty years climbing poles in Ohio winters, and I can tell you the power grid does not owe you a thing when the ice comes down. When the lights go out for three days in January, your furnace is just a cold metal box, but twenty dollars of simple supplies will keep one room of your house warm enough to keep you alive.

I am not talking about magic. I am not promising you free heat. Heat takes energy, and when the wires are down on the snow, you are the energy source. You have to trap what little warmth you make. That is the whole job. You do not try to heat your whole house. A two thousand square foot house with no furnace will suck the life out of you in hours. You pick one small room. An interior room is best. A bathroom or a small bedroom that does not share an outside wall if you have one. If all your rooms have outside walls, pick the smallest one facing south where the winter sun hits the glass during the day. Close the door. You are abandoning the rest of the house until the trucks roll and we get the lines back up.

Before you even think about getting comfortable, you need to seal that room. This is where your twenty dollars goes to work. Go to the hardware store today while the power is still on. Buy a few rolls of cheap painter tape and a couple sheets of thin plastic drop cloth. When the outage hits, tape that plastic right over your windows. Do it tight. Tape all four edges to the frame. You are creating a dead air space between the freezing glass and your room. Dead air is insulation. It stops the cold from radiating off that window pane and eating your body heat. Take your extra towels, your old rags, whatever you have, and shove them under the door gap. If there is a heating vent in the floor, block it. When the furnace is off, those ducts are just tunnels bringing freezing basement air up into your room. Stuff a rag in there. Block it tight.

Now you have a sealed box. But a big box still loses heat fast. So you make the box smaller. You pitch a tent inside that room. I am serious. A basic three season dome tent works perfectly. If you do not own a tent, you build a blanket fort like you did when you were seven years old. Drape heavy comforters over chairs, over a table, pin them to the walls. Make a small enclosed space just big enough for you and whoever is with you to sit or lie down. Why do this? Because your body puts out about as much heat as a hundred watt incandescent light bulb. In a big room, that heat floats up to the ceiling and disappears. Inside a tent or a blanket fort, that same body heat warms the air around you. The temperature inside a pitched tent in a cold room can be fifteen to twenty degrees warmer than the room itself. It works. I have seen it work.

Layering is next, and most people do it wrong. They put on their heaviest coat right away. Do not do that. You start with a base layer against your skin. Cotton kills. I learned that working storms in the Appalachian foothills. Cotton holds sweat, and wet skin freezes. Wear wool or synthetic long underwear. Tight to the skin. Then add a loose middle layer, like a fleece or a thick wool sweater. That traps warm air. Your outer layer should block wind, though inside your house wind is less of an issue. The real trick is your extremities. Put on thick wool socks. Wear a knit hat to bed. You lose a massive amount of heat through your bare head. Keep a pair of gloves handy. If your hands and feet are warm, your brain thinks your whole body is warm.

Here is a lineman trick for you. Hot water bottles. Not the fancy electric ones. Just thick rubber hot water bottles you buy at the pharmacy. Before the power drops, or if you have a safe way to boil water outside, fill them up. Boil water on a camp stove outside, bring it in, pour it carefully into the bottle. Screw the cap on tight. Wrap that bottle in a t shirt so it does not burn your skin, and shove it in your sleeping bag or under your blankets near your groin or your armpits. Those spots have major blood vessels close to the surface. Warming the blood there warms your whole body fast. One good hot water bottle will radiate heat for six to eight hours. It is the best twenty dollar investment you will ever make when the grid fails.

Sleeping warm is its own problem. The floor is your enemy. Cold sinks. If you lay your sleeping bag directly on the carpet, the floor will pull the heat right out of your back. You need a barrier. Fold up a mattress, stack couch cushions, lay down thick moving blankets. Get yourself at least four inches off the actual floor. Then lay your sleeping bag on top of that. If you have two sleeping bags, zip them together or put one inside the other. Sleep in your base layers and your hat. Do not bury your face inside the bag. You breathe out moisture all night. If that moisture gets trapped inside your bag, the insulation gets damp, and damp insulation is useless. Breathe outside the collar.

Eat before you sleep. Digestion creates heat. Eat something with fat in it. Peanut butter, cheese, nuts. Your body has to work to break down fats, and that work generates internal warmth. Drink warm water if you have it. Avoid alcohol. People think whiskey warms you up. It does not. It dilates your blood vessels, pushes warm blood to your skin, makes you feel warm for ten minutes, and then dumps your core heat into the room. Alcohol in a winter outage is a fast way to get hypothermia. Stick to water or warm broth.

Now we need to talk about how people die. I have worked storm restoration for four decades. I have seen the crews pull bodies out of houses after the ice storms. And they were not killed by the cold. They were killed by trying to beat the cold the wrong way. Listen to me very carefully. Carbon monoxide is a silent killer. It has no smell. It has no color. It just puts you to sleep and you do not wake up. Every single winter, folks drag portable generators into their garages, or onto their covered porches, thinking they are safe because the garage door is cracked open. They are not safe. A generator produces deadly exhaust instantly. Never run a generator inside your house. Never run it in an attached garage. Never run it near an open window where the wind can blow the exhaust right back inside. It needs to be outside, far away from the structure, with the exhaust pointing away from the house. Period. No exceptions.

And do not bring your charcoal grill inside to cook or stay warm. Charcoal produces massive amounts of carbon monoxide. Burning charcoal in a closed room will kill you in hours. Same goes for propane camping stoves. Those little green bottles you screw the burner onto. They are for outside. Using them indoors consumes your oxygen and fills the room with carbon monoxide. People think because the flame is blue and clean, it is safe. It is not. Ventless indoor propane heaters exist, but if you do not know exactly how to use them, if you do not have a working battery powered carbon monoxide detector in that room, do not risk it.

Then there are candles. People buy fifty tea light candles and think they are going to heat a room. A candle gives off almost no usable heat. What it does give off is an open flame. I have seen houses burn to the foundation during outages because someone knocked over a candle in the dark, or fell asleep with one burning near a blanket fort. If you use a candle for light, put it in a deep glass jar. Keep it away from anything that burns. Never leave it alone. Never go to sleep with it lit. Better yet, buy battery powered lanterns. Use the candles only if you absolutely have to, and treat them like the hazard they are.

You also need a battery powered carbon monoxide detector. If you have any combustion happening anywhere near your house, a generator running outside, a neighbor running equipment, you need that alarm. Batteries die. Check them now. Do not wait until the sky turns grey and the sleet starts falling sideways.

This setup I just described is not luxury. It is survival. You will be cold. You will be uncomfortable. You will miss your television and your microwave. But you will be breathing, and your core temperature will hold. Pick the room. Seal the windows with plastic and tape. Block the vents. Pitch the tent. Layer your clothes right, wool first. Use hot water bottles on your pulse points. Insulate yourself from the floor. Eat fats. And never, ever bring combustion inside your living space.

I climbed poles in minus ten degree weather with wind chill dropping it to thirty below. I know what cold does to a man. It makes you tired. It makes you stupid. It makes you want to take shortcuts with fire and exhaust. Do not take the shortcuts. Follow the rules. Trust the physics of trapped body heat. It is slow, but it works.
