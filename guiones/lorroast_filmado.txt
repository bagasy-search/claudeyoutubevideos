[INTRO | she laughs and waves her hand] But my mother's oven was a gas oven from nineteen forty eight, and nobody knew what temperature it really was. Some Sundays it was perfect. Some Sundays Daddy chewed one bite for a whole minute and didn't say a word. He was a kind man.
[INTRO | she counts on her fingers while Danny holds the phone toward her] Danny read me some more of your questions, and I heard three things over and over. What cut of meat do I buy? Do I really have to brown it first? And can I put the roast in frozen? I will answer every one of those, and a few more.
[WHY | an oven door open, a roasting pan inside, the roast drying at the edges] Now first, why does the oven dry it out? Because an oven heats the air, honey, and hot dry air pulls the water right out of the meat. Every time you open the door to look, the heat runs out, and then it comes roaring back. And most people cook it too hot, so the meat squeezes up and pushes all its juice out into the pan.
[WHY | the slow cooker on the counter, the lid on, a little steam at the edge] A slow cooker is the opposite. The heat is low and gentle, the lid holds the steam in, and the meat sits there for eight hours in its own juice. It can't dry out, because there's nowhere for the water to go. That is why Bernice's roasts were always better than mine.
[CUT | two pieces of raw beef side by side on butcher paper, one marbled chuck roast and one lean round roast] Now, the meat. This is the most important thing I will tell you today. Buy a chuck roast. That's the shoulder. It might say chuck roast, or shoulder roast, or seven bone roast. Three or four pounds. And look for these little white lines of fat running all through it. That is what melts in the pot and makes it tender.
[CUT | she taps the lean roast with one finger and shakes her head] Don't buy the lean roasts. The round, the rump, the eye of round. They look nice and they cost more, but they have no fat inside, and in a slow cooker they come out dry and grainy. The cheap cut is the right cut, honey. The butchers always knew that.
[CUT | an old photo of a small-town butcher counter with a butcher in a white apron] Mr. Petersen raised cattle out on the county road, and he used to say, the shoulder works the hardest, so it has the most flavor. You just have to give it time. He was right about that, and he was right about most things.
[RECIPE | the floury table set with a chuck roast, onions, carrots, small potatoes, garlic, a cup of black coffee, a carton of beef broth, a bottle of Worcestershire and bay leaves] Here is what you need for one roast, enough for six or eight people. A three to four pound chuck roast. Salt and pepper. A couple spoons of flour. Two onions, cut in thick slices. A pound of carrots, cut in big chunks. A pound and a half of small potatoes, cut in half. Three cloves of garlic. One cup of strong black coffee. One cup of beef broth. A tablespoon of Worcestershire sauce, and two bay leaves.
[STEP | old hands patting the roast dry with paper towels, then salting it heavily] Step one. Take the roast out of the package and pat it dry with a paper towel, all over. A wet roast won't brown. Then salt it on every side. About two teaspoons of salt, and a good amount of pepper. More than you think. That is a big piece of meat, and the salt has to go all the way through it.
[STEP | she rolls the roast in a little flour on a plate] Then roll it in a little flour, just a dusting, and shake off the extra. That flour browns in the skillet and it helps thicken the gravy later. My mother did that, and her mother did that.
[STEP | a heavy black skillet with oil shimmering, the roast laid in and sizzling hard] Step two, and this is the one people skip. Brown it. Get a heavy skillet very hot with two tablespoons of oil, and lay the roast in. Don't touch it. Four or five minutes, until it is a deep dark brown, and then turn it and do the other side, and the edges too.
[STEP | she lifts the roast with a big fork showing a dark crust] Now somebody always asks me, Loretta, do I really have to brown it? Yes, honey. That brown crust is where all the flavor comes from. If you put a gray roast in the pot, you will get gray gravy. Ten minutes in the skillet is the difference between a good roast and a church supper roast.
[TRICK | old hands laying thick onion slices, carrot chunks and halved potatoes on the bottom of the slow cooker] Now here is the first church trick. The vegetables go on the bottom. Onions first, then the carrots and the potatoes. The meat goes on top of them. Most people do it backwards, and they wonder why the potatoes are hard and the meat is boiled.
[TRICK | the browned roast set down on top of the vegetables like on a rack] The bottom of the pot is the hottest, and vegetables take longer to cook than you think. So they go down there, close to the heat. And they make a little rack, so the roast sits up on top and cooks in the steam, instead of sitting in water. Cut the carrots and potatoes big, two inches. Little pieces turn to mush in eight hours.
[COFFEE | she pours a cup of black coffee into the hot skillet and steam bursts up] And now Bernice's trick. The coffee. Pour one cup of strong black coffee into that hot skillet you browned the meat in, and scrape up all the brown bits stuck to the bottom with a wooden spoon. That is all flavor, honey. Don't you wash that down the sink.
[COFFEE | an old photo of a big steel church coffee urn on a counter with cups lined up] Bernice got the idea from the church coffee urn. Every Sunday morning we made a big urn of coffee, and there was always some left after the service. One Sunday she poured the leftover coffee into her roast instead of water, and that was the best pot roast anybody had ever eaten in that basement.
[COFFEE | Loretta holds up a cup of coffee and smiles] Now it does not taste like coffee. I promise you. Danny didn't believe me either. It makes the gravy dark brown and rich, and it brings out the beef, the same way a little salt brings out the sweet in a pie. Nobody at church ever guessed it. They just asked Bernice for the recipe, and she said, it's a secret.
[LIQUID | the coffee, broth and Worcestershire poured around the roast, the liquid only halfway up the vegetables] So pour the coffee from the skillet over the roast, and then the cup of broth and the Worcestershire. Put in the garlic and the bay leaves. And here is the second trick. Don't drown it. The liquid should only come about halfway up the vegetables. Not over the meat.
[LIQUID | she shakes her head with a small smile] People think more water makes it juicier. It doesn't. The roast makes its own juice in there, a lot of it. If you cover it with water, you are boiling it, and you get a gray roast and a watery gravy. Two cups is plenty for a four pound roast.
[LOW | an old hand turning the dial of the slow cooker to low] And the third trick. Cook it on low. Not high. Low, for eight or nine hours. On high it gets done faster, but the meat tightens up and comes out stringy. Low and slow lets all that fat and the tough parts melt. It is the same as people, honey. You can't rush the tough ones.
[LOW | the slow cooker on the counter with the lid on and a kitchen timer beside it] And the fourth one, and this is the hardest one. Don't lift the lid. Every time you lift it to look, all the steam goes out, and it takes about twenty minutes to get the heat back. Bernice used to put a dish towel over the lid at church so nobody would peek. And they still peeked.
[DONE | a fork pushed into the roast and twisted, the meat pulling apart in big tender pieces] Now how do you know it's done? Not by the clock. Take a fork and push it into the roast, and give it a little twist. If it falls apart, it's done. If it's still tough, it is not ready yet. Put the lid back on and give it another hour. A tough pot roast just needs more time.
[DONE | Loretta at the table, holding up a finger] That surprises people. They think tough means it cooked too long. With a chuck roast, it is the other way around. It is tough until it gets to about two hundred degrees inside, and then all at once it goes soft. So if it is tough, wait.
[GRAVY | she lifts the roast and vegetables out onto a platter and covers them loosely with foil] When it's done, lift the meat and the vegetables out onto a platter, and cover them loosely with foil. Then pour all that juice into a saucepan, and let it sit a minute so the fat comes up to the top. Skim most of it off with a big spoon.
[GRAVY | she whisks a little cornstarch and cold water in a cup, then pours it into the simmering juice] Then mix two tablespoons of cornstarch in a quarter cup of cold water, stir it into the juice, and let it bubble for two minutes. It turns into the darkest, richest gravy you ever saw. Taste it for salt before it goes on the table. That's Bernice's coffee, right there.
