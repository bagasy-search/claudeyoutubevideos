Now, why does the pipe split somewhere other than where the ice is?

Picture the water line. On one end, the water main, pushing. On the other end, a faucet, closed. Now ice forms in one spot, in the cold part of the wall. That ice plug grows, and as water freezes, it gets bigger. It expands. That ice is now pushing water ahead of it, toward the closed faucet. And that water has nowhere to go. The pressure in that short piece of pipe, between the ice and the faucet, goes up, and up, until the pipe gives. Usually in a weak spot, a joint or a thin section, and often in a warmer part of the wall where you would never expect it.

And that is the whole reason for the drip.

A faucet that is open just a little gives that pressure a way out. It also keeps the water moving. So on the really cold nights, open the faucets on outside walls just a trickle. Not a full stream. A thin, steady drip. If it is a single handle faucet, set it in the middle so both the hot and the cold lines move a little. It uses some water. It costs a lot less than a ceiling.

Now let us do the cheap things. Most of these you can do in one afternoon, before the first hard freeze.

One. The outside faucets. Unhook every garden hose. A hose left on the spigot traps water right in the faucet, and that is one of the most common freezes there is. Then, inside the house, find the shut off valve for that outside faucet. It is usually in the basement, on the pipe that goes out through the wall to the spigot. Close it. Then go outside and open the spigot, and leave it open, so whatever water is in that short piece of pipe can drain out. If you hear it drain, good. That pipe is now empty, and an empty pipe cannot split.

Some newer houses have what is called a frost free faucet, with a long stem that shuts the water off inside the wall. Those still need the hose off. A hose left on a frost free faucet holds water in it, and it can split anyway.

Two. Insulate the pipes you found. Foam pipe sleeves, the gray tubes with a slit down the side, from any hardware store. Measure the size of the pipe, get the matching sleeve, snap it on, and tape the seams. Put them on every pipe along an outside wall in the basement, in the crawl space, and in the garage. It does not heat the pipe. It slows down how fast it loses heat. And on a cold night, slowing it down is often enough.

Three. Stop the cold air that hits the pipe. This is the one people miss. A lot of pipes freeze not because the wall is cold, but because there is a gap letting outside air blow right onto them. Look at the top of the basement wall, where the wood sits on the foundation. Look where pipes and wires go through the outside wall. If you can feel air moving, seal it. Caulk for small cracks, and the expanding foam in a can for bigger gaps. Be careful near anything hot, like the furnace flue. Keep the foam away from that.

Four. For the worst spots, the one pipe that freezes every year no matter what, there is heat cable. It is an electric cable that runs along the pipe and gives off a little heat. If you use it, buy one that is listed for pipes, with a built in thermostat, and follow the instructions to the letter. Do not wrap it over itself. Do not cover it with the wrong kind of insulation. And plug it into a proper outlet, not an extension cord. Installed wrong, heat cable starts fires. Installed right, it is a good tool for one problem pipe.
