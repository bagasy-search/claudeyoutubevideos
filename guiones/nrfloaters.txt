Okay. So what is a floater?

Your eye, the ball of your eye, isn't empty. It's filled with a clear gel. Doctors call it the vitreous. And it's a lot of your eye. Around eighty percent of the inside of your eyeball is that gel.

When you're young, that gel is firm and perfectly clear. Like... think of the white of a raw egg. Clear, a little thick, it holds its shape. Inside it there are very, very thin fibers, collagen fibers, spread out evenly like a net. You can't see them, because they're so fine and so evenly spread.

Now, here's what happens with the years. That gel slowly turns to liquid. Not all at once, a little bit here, a little bit there. And when parts of it become watery, those fine fibers don't have anything holding them apart anymore. So they clump together. They make little knots, strings, clumps.

And here's the part that nobody explained to you. You're not seeing the clumps. You're seeing their shadows.

Light comes in through the front of your eye, goes all the way through that gel, and lands on the retina at the back. The retina is like the film in an old camera. And when a clump of fibers is floating in the way, it casts a tiny shadow on that film. That shadow is your floater.

That's why they move when your eyes move. The gel sloshes, a little bit, like water in a glass when you turn it. And the clump drifts with it, a moment late. That's why it slides away when you try to look at it.

And it's also why they're so much worse against the sky, or a white wall, or a bright screen. A shadow needs light behind it. In a dim room you barely see them. Put a big bright even background behind them, and every single shadow shows up.

Remember that. Light behind the shadow. It's going to matter in a minute.
