I’ve been up on poles in Ohio winters for forty years, and I can tell you exactly what happens inside your house when the lights go out. Most folks don’t realize the trouble starts the minute they try to get warm.

Back in ’ninety-one, we had an ice storm that snapped lines for a solid week. I walked into a house on the south side where the family had every burner on the stove lit and the oven door wide open. The kitchen felt like a sauna, and the kids were huddled right there on the floor with blankets. Thing is, that gas range is not a furnace. It’s not vented for continuous heat, and it’s dumping carbon monoxide right into your living space. They were lucky the house was so drafty, or they wouldn’t have woken up. You open that oven door, you’re gambling with your family’s lives. If you have to use it for a few minutes to take the chill off, you crack a window, and you never leave it unattended. But the real fix is having a safe heat source planned before the storm hits.

Another time, maybe two thousand five, a fellow flagged me down on a side street. He had a generator running in his attached garage, door closed except for a little gap at the bottom. He was proud of it, said he ran an extension cord through the cat door so the exhaust wouldn’t get the patio wet. I pulled him outside and explained that carbon monoxide doesn’t care about your cat door. It seeps through the drywall, through the ceiling, right into the bedrooms above. His wife was upstairs with a newborn. I had him kill that generator on the spot. A generator needs to be twenty feet from any door, window, or vent, with the exhaust pointing away. Not ten feet. Not fifteen. Twenty. And you need a battery-operated carbon monoxide detector on every level of your house, with fresh batteries. Test it right now, while you’re thinking about it. I’ve found too many detectors with the batteries pulled out because the low-battery chirp annoyed someone.

Then there was the old couple out on Route Four. You could tell they’d been through it before. Power went out around nine at night, temperature dropping fast. I got to their place the next morning to check the service line, and they had it figured. They had a kerosene heater in the living room, sitting on a fireproof mat, with a window cracked one inch. They had a battery-powered CO detector on the mantel. They had jugs of water stored in the basement. They had a landline phone that worked without power. They’d closed off the upstairs and were living in one room, blankets over the doorways. They offered me coffee from a camp stove on the back porch. That’s how you do it. They didn’t panic, they just did what they’d practiced. You need to practice too. Walk your house today and figure out which room you’d seal off. Know where your water shutoff is if the pipes freeze.

Frozen pipes are a disaster people don’t think about until the water is running through the ceiling. I saw a house in ’ninety-eight where the power was out for three days. The family left to stay with relatives and didn’t drain the lines. Came back to a split pipe in the upstairs bathroom and a waterfall down the stairwell. The drywall, the insulation, the hardwood floors, all ruined. If you leave your house in a winter outage, shut the water off at the main and open the faucets to drain what’s in the pipes. Even a trickle of water moving through a line can prevent a freeze. When you’re staying put, let a faucet on an outside wall drip slowly. That movement makes a difference. And open the cabinet doors under sinks so whatever heat is in the room gets to the pipes.

Candles. I’ve seen more near-misses with candles than I can count. One house had a row of tea lights on the coffee table, right next to a stack of magazines. The kids were walking around with blankets trailing behind them. One wrong move, one blanket corner over a flame, and you’ve got a room on fire in seconds. Use flashlights or battery-powered lanterns. If you must use candles, put them in a sturdy holder on a metal tray, away from anything that can burn, and never leave them in a room with a sleeping person or a child. Blow them out when you leave the room. Every time.

Pets. People forget their animals are feeling the cold worse in some ways. I was in a house where the family had the dog’s bed right by the electric space heater. When the power came back on, that heater kicked in while they were asleep, and the dog’s blanket was touching the front grill. It smoldered for an hour before the smoke alarm woke them. Electric heaters need three feet of clear space in every direction. No curtains, no furniture, no pet beds. And plug them directly into the wall outlet, never into an extension cord or power strip. Those cords heat up under the load, and I’ve seen the melted plastic aftermath behind the couch nobody checks.

Neighbors. The best thing I saw in all my years was a neighborhood on Beech Street that treated an outage like a team effort. One guy had a generator and ran a heavy-duty cord to his neighbor’s fridge so their insulin stayed cold. Another brought over a camp stove and they made soup in the driveway. They checked on the elderly woman at the end of the street twice a day. They shared firewood. Nobody was trying to be a hero alone. That’s the practical side of this. Know your neighbors. Know who has medical equipment, who has small children, who has a working chainsaw if a tree comes down. Swap phone numbers now, not when the cell towers are overloaded and your battery is at ten percent.

I’ll tell you about the guy with the charcoal grill in the kitchen next.

He thought a charcoal grill in the kitchen was the same as a fireplace. It’s not. Not even close. I walked in and the air was thick, a blue haze hanging at the ceiling. He had the windows shut because he didn’t want to lose the heat. His wife was on the couch with a headache, and the kids were lethargic. That’s the carbon monoxide talking. Charcoal gives off massive amounts of CO, and it has no business inside a house, a garage, or even a screened porch. Not one single briquette. I don’t care how cold you are. I pulled them out onto the porch, opened every window, and called the paramedics. They all recovered, but that man sat on the curb shaking his head, realizing he nearly killed his family with a bag of Kingsford. You cook outside, period. If you’re cold, you put on more layers and get under blankets. You don’t bring the fire inside.

Another call that sticks with me was a duplex on the east side. Power out for two days, and the tenant downstairs had a propane camp heater running in the living room. The kind with the little green canisters. He had it on the carpet, right next to the sofa. Those heaters need ventilation, and they need a non-combustible surface. This one tipped over while he was in the bathroom. The carpet started to melt before the safety shutoff kicked in. The smell was horrible, and the floor was scorched down to the subfloor. He was lucky it didn’t ignite. Any portable heater with a flame needs a flat, hard surface and at least three feet of clearance all around. And you don’t leave it running when you leave the room. Not for a minute. I’ve seen the scorch marks on floors in a dozen houses. The ones that didn’t burn down were just lucky.

I remember a family that decided to sleep in their car with the engine running. They backed it out of the garage, which was smart, but they didn’t check the exhaust pipe. Snow had drifted up around the back of the car while they were inside packing blankets. The tailpipe was buried. Exhaust backed up under the chassis and seeped in through the floorboards. The mother woke up dizzy and nauseous, realized something was wrong, and got everyone out. If you’re using a vehicle for warmth, you clear the snow away from the exhaust pipe first. You check it every hour if it’s still snowing. And you crack a window, no matter how cold it is outside. Carbon monoxide can fill a car faster than you think.

Then there was the house with the fireplace they hadn’t used in ten years. Power went out, and the husband decided it was time to light a fire. He didn’t check the flue. A bird had built a nest in the chimney over the summer. Smoke poured into the living room, the alarms went off, and they had to open every door in a blizzard to clear it out. If you have a fireplace, you get the chimney inspected and cleaned every year. Before winter. Not after the first snow. A blocked flue will fill your house with smoke and carbon monoxide. And you need a screen or glass doors in front of that fire. An ember can pop out onto the rug and smolder for hours before it catches. I’ve seen the holes burned in hardwood floors where a single ember landed.

I got called to a house where the power had been back on for six hours, but the family was still sick. Headaches, vomiting, confusion. The fire department found high CO levels throughout the house. The culprit was a generator they’d shut off and dragged into the mudroom for the night. It was off, but it was still hot, still off-gassing fumes. Never store a generator, a gas can, or any fuel-burning equipment inside your house or attached garage. Not even in a closet. Not even for one night. The fumes linger and they seep. Store it in a detached shed or outside under a cover, away from the house.

I want to tell you about the woman who used her gas stove to heat bricks. She’d read about it somewhere, an old pioneer trick. She heated bricks in the oven, wrapped them in towels, and put them in the beds. That part’s not bad, actually. The problem was she left the oven on high and forgot about it when she went to bed. The kitchen got dangerously hot, and the cabinets above the stove started to warp. She was lucky they didn’t ignite. If you’re going to use any method like that, you set a timer. You don’t walk away from an open oven. You don’t leave a stove burner lit while you sleep. Ever.

One winter, a fellow tried to thaw his frozen pipes with a propane torch. He was in the crawlspace, holding a flame up to a copper pipe, and he set the insulation on fire. The flames spread into the wall cavity before he could even crawl out. By the time the fire department arrived, the whole kitchen wall was involved. Frozen pipes are a problem you solve with a hair dryer, a heating pad, or warm towels. Not an open flame. Never an open flame. And if you can’t see the pipe, you don’t start cutting into walls without knowing what’s behind them. You can hit a wire, you can hit a gas line. Call a plumber if you’re in over your head. Better to pay for the service call than to burn your house down.

I saw a house where the family had taped plastic sheeting over the windows to keep the cold out. That’s fine, it helps. But they taped it over the only window in the room where they were running a kerosene heater. They sealed themselves in with the fumes. You need that one inch of fresh air coming in. You need cross-ventilation if you’re burning any fuel. I don’t care how cold the draft feels. The draft is keeping you alive.

The quietest danger I ever saw was a family sleeping in a tent in the living room. They thought it would trap their body heat, make a little warm pocket. It does, but they zipped it all the way up, four people inside, no airflow. The carbon dioxide from their own breathing built up in that small space. Not monoxide, just CO2 from exhalation. They woke up with pounding headaches and a toddler who was groggy and unresponsive. They were lucky the tent wasn’t airtight. If you use a tent indoors, you leave the door unzipped at the top. You need air exchange, even from your own lungs.

I remember the young couple who bought a house with a wood stove and thought they were set. First outage, they loaded it up with scrap lumber from a renovation project. Pressure-treated wood. That stuff has chemicals in it, arsenic compounds, and when you burn it the smoke is toxic. They filled their house with fumes that burned their eyes and throats. You burn only seasoned, untreated firewood in a stove or fireplace. No painted wood, no plywood, no particle board, no trash. Those chemicals go right into your lungs.

Another one that gets people is the turkey fryer. A man set his up in the garage with the door open a couple feet, figured that was enough ventilation. It’s not. Those burners put out a massive flame and a massive amount of carbon monoxide. He was boiling water for coffee and heat, and within twenty minutes the CO detector in the kitchen was screaming. His garage had a door into the house that he’d left open so the heat would flow in. The CO flowed in faster. Outdoor cookers stay outdoors. No exceptions.

I pulled up to a house once where the family had strung extension cords all over the snow from a generator on the back deck. The cords were buried under fresh powder, and the kids were playing right on top of them. If a cord is damaged, if the insulation is nicked, that snow is water and water conducts electricity. You can energize the whole puddle. Run cords overhead if you can, or along a fence line where nobody walks. And use heavy-duty outdoor-rated cords, nothing you’d plug a lamp into. Check the amperage rating on the cord and make sure it matches your load. An undersized cord gets hot, melts the insulation, and starts a fire you won’t see until it’s too late.

I saw a basement full of water because the sump pump had no backup. Power out for a day, snow melting outside, and the groundwater came up through the floor. They lost the furnace, the water heater, boxes of family photos. If you have a sump pump, you need a battery backup or a water-powered backup. Test it now. Pour a bucket of water in the pit and make sure it kicks on. The time to find out it’s dead is not during a storm.

One house I went to, the family had a carbon monoxide detector that was twenty years old. They didn’t know those things expire. The sensor degrades, and after five to seven years it’s not reliable. Look at the back of your detector right now. There’s a manufacture date stamped on it. If it’s older than seven years, replace it today. Not tomorrow. Today. And write the replacement date on the new one with a marker so you don’t forget.

I remember a fellow who used his gas oven to heat the house and fell asleep on the couch. His dog woke him up, scratching at his face. The dog was in distress too, whimpering and unsteady. That dog saved both their lives. The CO level in the kitchen was over four hundred parts per million. At that level, you’re unconscious in two hours. He opened the doors, got the dog outside, and they both made it. But he told me later he’d ignored the headache for an hour, thought it was just stress. Don’t ignore a headache during an outage. Don’t ignore nausea or dizziness. Get outside, get fresh air, and figure out what’s burning inside. Your body is telling you something. Listen to it.

The last thing I want to tell you about is the aftermath. Power comes back on, and everyone relaxes. That’s when the surge damage hits. I’ve seen refrigerators, televisions, furnaces fried because they were still plugged in when the lines energized. When the power goes out, unplug your electronics and your major appliances. Leave one lamp on so you know when the power is back. Then plug things in one at a time, slowly, over a few minutes. The surge when the grid comes back up is real, and it’s hard on your equipment.

Food safety is another one people miss. Your fridge keeps food cold for about four hours if you keep the door shut. A full freezer, about forty-eight hours. A half-full freezer, maybe twenty-four. After that, you’re taking a risk. When in doubt, throw it out. I knew a family that ate chicken they’d cooked on the camp stove but stored in a fridge that had been off for twelve hours. All four of them got food poisoning in the middle of an ice storm. Now you’re dealing with dehydration and sickness when the roads are impassable. Not worth it.

I’ve told you a lot of hard stories tonight. They’re all true. I’ve seen the best and the worst of what people do when the lights go out. The difference between the two is planning. The old couple on Route Four didn’t do anything fancy. They just thought ahead. They respected the dangers. They didn’t assume they could wing it. You can be that family. You can be the one who helps your neighbors instead of the one who needs rescuing.

Start today. Check your detectors. Test your generator if you have one. Know where your water shutoff is. Put flashlights where you can find them in the dark. Write down emergency numbers on a piece of paper and stick it on the fridge. Walk through your house and imagine the power is out right now. What’s your heat source? What’s your light source? Where are your blankets? Where will your pets sleep? Do it before the storm, not during.

If you want more of what forty years on the poles taught me, subscribe to this channel and I’ll keep telling you what I know. Stay warm, stay smart, and look out for each other.
