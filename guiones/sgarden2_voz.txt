This bucket of grey ash and this bottle of vinegar. That is what my mother used instead of a landscaper. Last spring a man with a clipboard walked around my neighbor's yard for ten minutes and gave her a paper. Soil treatment, weed control for the path, four hundred dollars. I read the paper. Almost everything on it, my mother did with the ash from her bath fire and a bottle of rice vinegar. Come, sit with me in my yard. I will show you eleven of her tricks, and number six keeps the weeds out of your path for a whole year.
You know the feeling. You pull the weeds between the stones on Saturday, on your knees, and by the next Saturday they are back. Your tomatoes look tired. The lawn has yellow patches no matter what you buy. Slugs eat holes in the lettuce at night. And the store has a different bottle for every problem, each one with a picture of a happy family on it, and each one costs more than the last.
[whispering] I want to tell you something, and please listen. It is not because you are bad with plants. It was never you. Most yard problems are very old problems, and the old people had answers for them before there was a store. Some of those answers are better than the bottle. Some are cheaper. And a few are just ash and vinegar, which you can get for almost nothing.
So today, eleven tricks, one by one. How much to use, when, and where you must never use it, because ash and vinegar are strong, and I will tell you honestly where they hurt. The first trick is a little test you can do in one minute with a spoon of soil. And number six, the path one, is the reason my neighbor almost paid four hundred dollars. Stay with me until then.
You know me, I am Sachiko. On my kitchen channel I cook the old Japanese way. Here, on my garden channel, we go outside. When I was a girl in Osaka, our house had a wooden bath, an ofuro, heated by a little wood fire outside the wall. Every evening my father lit it, and every morning my mother took out the ash with a small iron shovel and carried it to the garden in a bucket. I can still hear that shovel scraping on the bricks.
Quick, while I have you. All eleven tricks are written in the description, with every measure, so you can keep them for the spring. And if you like the old ways, please subscribe to this garden channel. Now, the ash and the vinegar.
In Japan, every child knows a story called Hanasaka Jiisan. The old man who made the flowers bloom. A kind old man climbs a dead cherry tree with a basket of ash, throws the ash into the branches, and the whole tree bursts into flowers. My mother told me this story when I was small, sitting next to the bath fire. Then she would say, that is a fairy tale, Sachiko. [whispering] But the ash is not a fairy tale. And she would carry the bucket out to the garden.
My mother lived through the hungry years after the war, before I was born. Nothing was wasted. In Japanese we say mottainai, it means, do not waste what still has a use. The ash from the bath had a use. The vinegar that was a little too old for the kitchen had a use. When I came to America in nineteen seventy, I was surprised. People here put their fireplace ash in the trash, and then they went to the store to buy something to put on the garden. [laughs] My mother would not believe it.
Trick number one. The vinegar test. Before you put anything on your soil, you have to know if your soil is sour or sweet. Gardeners say acid or alkaline. Take a spoon of soil from the bed, put it in a little cup, and pour a little white vinegar on it. Listen and look. If it fizzes and bubbles, your soil is already sweet, alkaline, and you must not put ash on it. If nothing happens, it is not alkaline, and ash may help. My mother did this with a teacup. A soil test from the extension office tells you much more, but this tells you in one minute if you can use the bucket.
And here is the other half of the test. Take a new spoon of soil, wet it with a little water until it is like mud, and add a pinch of baking soda. If this one fizzes, your soil is sour, acid, and ash will help it. If neither one fizzes, your soil is in the middle, where most plants are happy, and you only need a very little ash, or none. My mother wrote the results on the wooden fence with a pencil, one line for each bed.
Trick number two. Ash for sour soil. Wood ash works like garden lime. It makes sour soil sweeter, and it gives the plants potassium and calcium, the things that make strong stems and good fruit. But only a little. The rule is about two cups of ash for a patch ten feet by ten feet, once a year, spread thin like flour on a table, and then mixed into the top of the soil with a rake. Do it in the fall or in early spring, not in the middle of summer. More is not better. Too much ash, and the soil gets too sweet, and the plants cannot eat.
And only clean ash. Ash from plain firewood, from your fireplace or your wood stove. Never the ash from charcoal briquettes from the grill, never from painted or treated wood, never from burned cardboard or trash. My mother only used the ash from the bath fire, which was dry branches and old wood from the house. And let it cool completely. I will tell you why in trick number eleven.
Which plants are happy with a little ash? The cabbage family, cabbage, broccoli, kale, they like soil that is not too sour. Lilacs and clematis by the fence. Beans and peas. And a lawn on sour soil, with moss in the shady corners, often looks greener the next spring after a thin dusting in the fall. My mother put her ash first where she grew cabbage and daikon, and they were the biggest on our street.
Trick number three. The slug ring. Slugs and snails come at night and eat holes in your lettuce and your hostas. My mother made a ring of dry ash around each young plant, a band about two fingers wide, a little away from the stem. A slug has a soft wet body, and it does not like to crawl across dry, rough ash. It turns around. But here is the honest part. When it rains, the ash gets wet and stops working. So you make the ring again after the rain. My mother checked her rings every morning when she took the bath ash out. Ten seconds per plant.
Trick number four. Ash in the compost. If you keep a compost pile, a little ash makes it better. But only a little, sprinkled thin between the layers, like salt on rice, not like snow. A handful every time you add a big bucket of kitchen scraps and leaves. Too much makes the pile too alkaline and it slows down. My mother kept her ash bucket next to the compost corner for this reason, and [laughs] she said, ash is a spice, not a meal.
Trick number five. Where you must never put ash. This one saves you a lot of tears. Never around blueberries, azaleas, rhododendrons, or camellias. They love sour soil, and ash makes them sick and yellow. Never in the potato bed, because sweeter soil gives potatoes rough, scabby skin. And here is a surprise. If you have a blue hydrangea, ash will slowly turn it pink. The color of a hydrangea depends on how sour the soil is. My neighbor's blue hydrangea turned pink the year her husband cleaned the fireplace into the flower bed.
Before number six, tell me in the comments. Do you have a fireplace or a wood stove? What do you do with the ash now? I read every comment, and I am curious how many of you are throwing away a garden in a bucket.
Trick number six. The path. This is the one that keeps the weeds out for a whole year. In front of my mother's house there was a little stone path to the door. Between the stones, weeds, every summer. And in Japan, salt has a special place at the door of a house. People put a small pile of salt by the entrance, for good luck. My mother used that salt for something more practical. Once a year, in the spring, she put salt and vinegar into the cracks of the path. Just the cracks.
Here is how I do it. First pull out the big weeds or cut them short. Then mix one quart of white vinegar with a quarter cup of table salt, stir until the salt melts, and add one small spoon of dish soap, so it sticks to the leaves. Pour it slowly into the cracks between the stones or the bricks, or the crack along the driveway, on a dry sunny morning with no wind. The vinegar burns the leaves in one or two days. The salt stays in the crack and keeps new seeds from growing. In my mother's path, one treatment in the spring and the cracks stayed bare until the next spring.
And now the honest part, the important part. Salt does not know where your path ends. Use this only in the cracks of a path, a driveway or a patio, where you never want anything to grow. Never next to the lawn, never near a flower bed or a vegetable bed, never near the roots of a tree or a hedge. Never on a slope where the rain carries it into the grass. Salt in good soil stays for a long time, and nothing grows there. That is exactly why it works in the cracks, and exactly why it must stay in the cracks. Wear glasses when you pour.
Trick number seven. Vinegar for the young weeds in the open. For the little weeds at the edge of the beds, plain vinegar without salt. The white vinegar from the kitchen, in a spray bottle, on a hot sunny day, sprayed right on the leaves. By evening they wilt, by the next day they are brown. It works on young weeds with soft leaves. On old weeds with deep roots, like dandelions, it only burns the top, and they come back from the root. For those my mother used a long thin knife and took out the root. [laughs] Vinegar for the babies, the knife for the grandparents.
Be careful with the strong vinegar some stores sell for weeds, the one many times stronger than kitchen vinegar. It can burn your skin and your eyes. If you use it, gloves and glasses, always. Kitchen vinegar is enough for the young weeds, and it will not hurt you. And never spray on a windy day, because vinegar does not choose between a weed and your tomato.
Trick number eight. Clay pots like new. Old terracotta pots get a white crust on the outside, from the minerals in the water and the fertilizer, and sometimes a green film. My mother soaked them. One part vinegar and four parts water in a big tub, the pots under the water for an hour, and then she scrubbed them with a stiff brush. The white crust comes right off. Then rinse them very well with plain water and let them dry in the sun before you plant in them again. A new pot at the store is a few dollars each. A soak is a few cents.
Trick number nine. Rusty tools. My mother had a small sickle, a kama, that she used for the grass along the fence, and a little hand hoe. When they got rusty, she put them in a jar of vinegar overnight. In the morning she scrubbed the rust off with a piece of steel wool, rinsed them, dried them very well, and wiped them with a rag with a little oil, so the rust does not come back. Do not leave them in the vinegar more than a day, because it starts to eat the good metal too. A tool that would go in the trash cuts like new.
Trick number ten. The bird bath and the feeder. A bird bath gets green and slimy in a week of summer. My mother did not use anything strong around the birds. She scrubbed the bath with a brush and a mix of one part vinegar to nine parts water, then rinsed it three times with clean water, until there was no smell of vinegar at all, and filled it fresh. The same for the hummingbird feeder here in America: take it apart, soak it in a little vinegar water, scrub, and rinse very, very well. Clean water brings more birds, and the birds eat the bugs in your garden.
Trick number eleven. Ash for the winter, and the one safety rule. When the steps are icy, a handful of ash gives your shoes something to grip. It does not melt the ice like salt, but you do not slip, and it does not hurt the concrete or the plants next to the steps. [laughs] Just take off your shoes at the door, because ash walks into the house. [whispering] And the safety rule, the most important thing I say today. Ash can hide live coals for days. Always keep your ash in a metal bucket with a metal lid, outside, on the ground, away from the house, the deck and the garage. Never in a paper bag, never in a plastic bin. Wait several days before you use it.
If something does not work, it is not you. If the weeds in the path come back in two months, the rain probably washed the salt away, or the crack was too wide and full of soil. Put it in again, a little more slowly. If your plants look yellow after the ash, you used too much, or the soil was already sweet. Do the vinegar test again and stop the ash for a year. If the slugs still come, it rained. Make the ring again.
Let me do the arithmetic, because this house does not do villains, it does arithmetic. The landscaper's paper, four hundred dollars, and the weed control again next year. My way. The ash, if you have a fireplace, costs nothing. A gallon of white vinegar, a few dollars. A box of salt, a dollar. A spray bottle, a brush, one old metal bucket with a lid. Less than twenty dollars for a whole year of the path, the beds, the pots, the tools and the birds.
I remember one spring morning in Osaka. I was eight. My mother was on her knees on the stone path in front of our house, pouring vinegar from an old brown bottle into the cracks, very slowly, like she was watering something precious. A neighbor walked by and laughed and said, you are watering the weeds. My mother did not look up. [whispering] She said, I am telling them goodbye. That summer our path was the only clean path on the street.
So, the eleven tricks one more time. One, the vinegar fizz test before anything. Two, two cups of clean ash for ten by ten feet, once a year. Three, the dry ash ring against slugs. Four, ash in the compost like salt on rice. Five, never ash near blueberries, azaleas, potatoes or your blue hydrangea. Six, salt and vinegar only in the cracks of the path, once in the spring. Seven, kitchen vinegar on young weeds in the sun. Eight, the vinegar soak for clay pots. Nine, the overnight soak for rusty tools. Ten, vinegar water for the bird bath, rinsed three times. Eleven, ash on icy steps, and ash always in a metal bucket with a lid.
