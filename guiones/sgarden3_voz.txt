This hole in my garden is going to feed my tomatoes next summer. Fish bones, eggshells, old tea leaves, a handful of rice bran and a bowl of peelings. Five kitchen scraps, buried this fall. My mother did this every autumn in Osaka, and in fifty years of gardening she never bought one bag of fertilizer. Not one. Come, sit with me in my garden. I will show you which five scraps, how deep, where, and what to plant on top of them in the spring.
You know the feeling. In the summer you water and water, and the tomatoes are small. The cabbage is pale. You go to the store and buy a bag of fertilizer, then a bottle, then another bottle, because the first one did not work. And every evening you scrape plates and peelings into the trash, a whole bag of good food for the soil, and it goes away in a truck.
[whispering] I want to tell you something, and please listen. It is not because you are bad with plants. It was never you. Most of the time the soil is just hungry. Not hungry for a bottle. Hungry for life. Worms, little creatures you cannot see, dark soft soil that holds water. The bag feeds the plant for a few weeks. The scraps feed the soil for years. And the best time to feed it is right now, in the fall.
So today I will show you all five scraps, one by one. What each one gives the soil, how to prepare it, and how deep it goes, because this is where most people make the mistake that brings the raccoons. I will show you my mother's trench, the one she dug every October. And at the end, what to plant on top in the spring so you get the most from it. Scrap number one is the one my neighbors think is crazy, and it is the one that works best.
You know me, I am Sachiko. On my kitchen channel I cook the old Japanese way, and nothing from my kitchen goes to waste. Here, on my garden channel, I show you where it goes. Behind our little wooden house in Osaka my mother had a garden the size of a bedroom. Daikon, cabbage, eggplant, cucumbers on bamboo poles, and a persimmon tree. And in the corner, under a wooden lid, a clay pot where she kept the scraps until she had enough to bury.
Quick, while I have you. The five scraps, with how deep and how much, are written in the description, so you can find them again next fall. And if you like the old ways, please subscribe to this garden channel. Now, the shovel.
My mother lived through the hungry years after the war, before I was born. In those years nobody in our neighborhood could buy fertilizer, and still the little gardens behind the houses had to feed families. So the women did what their grandmothers did. Everything that came from the soil went back to the soil. In Japanese we say mottainai. It means, do not waste what still has a use. To my mother, a fish bone in the trash was mottainai. A fish bone in the garden was next year's eggplant.
When I came to America in nineteen seventy, with my husband, we rented an apartment with no garden, only a balcony. I buried my peelings in a big pot on the balcony, and I grew shiso and green onions in it. The neighbors thought it was strange. Then we bought this house, with a real yard, and the first thing I did, before the furniture was in, was dig my mother's trench in the corner. That was almost fifty years ago, and that corner is still the darkest, softest soil in my garden.
Scrap number one. Fish scraps. The heads, the bones, the skin, what is left when you clean a fish. [laughs] This is the one my neighbors think is crazy. But fish is one of the oldest garden foods in the world. It is rich in the things plants need to grow leaves and fruit, and the bones are full of calcium and phosphorus, which go slowly into the soil over the winter. My mother buried the head and the bones of every fish she cleaned. Mackerel, sardines, the small fish from the market.
But here is the important part, the mistake that brings the raccoons. Fish must go deep. At least one foot down, twelve inches, with soil packed on top. If you bury it shallow, a raccoon, a dog, a cat or a skunk will smell it and dig it up in one night, and your garden looks like a battlefield in the morning. So dig deep, put the fish at the bottom, cover it with soil, step on it, and then put the other scraps on top. My mother also laid a flat stone or an old board over the spot for the first two weeks, until the smell was gone.
Keep the fish in the freezer, in a bag, until you are ready to dig, so it does not smell in your kitchen. One fish carcass for every two or three feet of trench is plenty. And only raw fish scraps, not fish cooked with sauce or oil. The oil and the salt are not good for the soil, and they smell more. My mother cleaned her fish at the kitchen sink, wrapped the bones in newspaper, and walked them out to the garden the same afternoon. [laughs] Fresh to the soil, she said, like to the table.
If you do not fish, ask at the fish counter of the grocery store or the Asian market. Many will give you the heads and the frames for very little, because they throw them away at the end of the day. I go on Saturday mornings. The man at the counter knows me now. [laughs] He says, for the garden again? And he wraps them for me in paper.
Scrap number two, eggshells. Most people throw them in the trash, or they put whole shells around their plants and think it helps right away. Here is what actually happens. Eggshells are almost pure calcium, the same thing as limestone. But a whole shell takes years to break down in the soil. So the secret is two things. Crush them very, very fine, almost like powder. And bury them in the fall, so they have the whole winter to start working.
My mother dried her eggshells on the windowsill, in a little tin. When the tin was full, she crushed them in her suribachi, the Japanese bowl with ridges inside that we use to grind sesame. You can use a rolling pin in a bag, or an old coffee grinder you keep only for this. Rinse the shells first, dry them, then crush. The finer the powder, the faster the soil can use it. A cup of crushed shells for every few feet of trench. Tomatoes, peppers and cabbage like calcium very much.
And one honest word. People say a ring of crushed eggshells keeps the slugs away. [laughs] In my garden, the slugs walk right over them. Use the shells for the soil, not as a fence.
Scrap number three. Used tea leaves. In Japanese we call them chagara, the leaves left in the teapot after you drink green tea. My mother drank green tea all day, so every day she had a little pile of wet leaves. She never threw them away. Tea leaves are soft, they break down fast, and they bring a little food to the soil, but mostly they make the soil dark, loose and soft, so it holds water and the roots can breathe. One teapot of leaves a day is a full bowl by the end of the week, and in a month it is a bucket.
If you drink black tea from bags, the leaves inside work too. Cut the bag open and use the leaves, because some tea bags are made with plastic and do not break down. Keep the used leaves in a jar with a loose lid on the counter, and every few days add them to your scrap bucket. Do not let them sit wet in a closed jar for weeks, they get moldy. My mother spread hers on a plate in the sun to dry when it rained for days, so they never smelled.
Scrap number four. Rice bran. Komenuka, in Japanese. This is the most Japanese one, and the most powerful. Rice bran is the brown outside of the rice grain, what comes off when brown rice is polished white. Japanese farmers have fed their soil with it for hundreds of years. It is rich in phosphorus, the thing that makes flowers and fruit. Many Japanese homes keep a bed of rice bran for pickles, nukazuke, and when the pickle bed gets too old, the old bran goes to the garden.
Here in America you can find rice bran at Asian grocery stores, and sometimes at feed stores. And here is why the fall is the right time for it. Rice bran is strong. When it starts to break down in the soil, it gets warm and it can burn young roots. If you put it next to a baby plant in May, you hurt it. If you bury it in October, by spring it is gentle, finished food. Use about a handful for every foot of trench, mixed into the soil, not in one lump. A lump gets moldy and smells. Mixed, it disappears.
Before I show you the trench, tell me in the comments. What do you do with your peelings now? The trash, the compost, the disposal under the sink? I read every comment, and I want to know how many good dinners for the soil are going to the truck every week.
Scrap number five. Vegetable and fruit peelings. Carrot tops, onion skins, potato peels, the outside leaves of the cabbage, apple cores, banana peels, the end of the daikon. This is the biggest pile, and it is the bulk of the meal. Peelings bring a little of everything back to the soil, nitrogen, potassium, all the small things, and they feed the worms. A soil full of worms is a soil that digs itself.
The secret is to cut them small. A whole cabbage leaf or a whole banana peel takes much longer than one cut into pieces the size of a coin. My mother chopped her peelings on the cutting board with two quick moves of the knife before they went into the scrap pot. And what never goes in. Never meat, never bones from meat, never cooked food with oil or butter, never dairy. They smell, they bring animals, and they rot instead of breaking down. Only raw plant scraps, the fish deep at the bottom, and nothing else.
And you do not need all five scraps on the same day. Keep them. A bucket with a tight lid by the back door, the fish in the freezer, the eggshells drying in a tin, the bran in a jar. Or dig a small hole, one foot deep, every time the bucket is full, and bury that one. My mother buried a little at a time when the pot in the corner was full, all fall, until the ground froze.
Now, my mother's trench. Choose the bed where you want to grow your hungriest plants next year. Dig a trench about one foot deep and the width of the shovel, as long as you have scraps for. At the bottom, the fish. Cover it with two inches of soil. Then the rice bran and the crushed eggshells, sprinkled along. Then the chopped peelings and the tea leaves, a layer no thicker than your hand. Then fill the trench back with the soil, at least eight inches of soil on top of the scraps, and step on it.
Mark it. My mother put a bamboo stick at each end of her trench, so in the spring she knew exactly where the food was. Next fall, dig the trench next to it, and the fall after, the next one. In three or four years you have fed the whole bed, and you start again at the beginning. [whispering] My mother called it walking the garden. Every year the trench walks one step. You only dig one trench a year.
And one more gift of the fall that costs nothing. The leaves. When the trees drop their leaves, rake them onto the bed over the trench, a blanket a few inches thick. They keep the soil a little warmer, so the worms keep working longer into the winter. By spring the bottom of the blanket is already soft and dark. My mother collected the persimmon leaves from our tree and the neighbors' maple leaves and every bed in her garden slept under a blanket.
And in the spring, what do you plant on top? The plants that eat the most. Tomatoes, squash, pumpkins, cucumbers, cabbage, eggplant. Plant them right over the trench, and their roots will grow down into the food. Wait until the soil is warm, and if you still see a big piece of something when you plant, push it back down and plant a little to the side. Things that do not like rich soil, like carrots and beans, go in last year's trench, not this year's.
If something goes wrong, it is not you. If an animal dug it up, the fish was too shallow. Next time deeper, and the stone on top. If it smells, the scraps were in a lump, or there was oil or meat. If in spring the scraps look the same as in the fall, your winter was very cold. That is fine. Plant beside the trench, and by summer it will be gone. And if your tomatoes still get dark spots on the bottom of the fruit, that is usually about water, not only calcium. Water deeply and evenly.
Let me do the arithmetic, because this house does not do villains, it does arithmetic. A bag of fertilizer, a bottle of plant food, another bag in the summer. For a family garden, easily fifty or a hundred dollars a year, every year. My mother's way. The fish bones, the eggshells, the tea leaves and the peelings you already throw away. A bag of rice bran, a few dollars, and it lasts all fall. In fifty years my mother spent less on her garden than one season of bottles.
I remember one October evening in Osaka. I was nine. My mother was digging her trench in the last light, and I was holding the bucket of scraps. She took the fish head out of the newspaper and put it at the bottom, and then she did something I never forgot. She put her hands together for a second, like at the temple, and bowed a little to the hole. I asked her why. [whispering] She said, this fish fed us today. Next summer it feeds us again. [whispering] You say thank you twice.
So, the five scraps one more time. Number one, raw fish scraps, at least one foot deep, with a stone on top. Number two, eggshells, rinsed, dried and crushed to powder. Number three, used tea leaves, dried if it rains. Number four, rice bran, a handful per foot, mixed in, only in the fall. Number five, raw peelings, cut the size of a coin, never meat or oil. One trench, a foot deep, eight inches of soil on top, a stick at each end, and tomatoes on top in the spring.
