This little glass jar, a roll of steel wool and a spoon of red chili. That is what my mother used to keep the mice out of her house in Osaka. No poison. No traps. Never once. And today the exterminator came to my neighbor's garage, walked around for twenty minutes, and asked her for four hundred dollars. Four hundred. Come, sit with me in my garage. I will show you what my mother did instead, and why it still works in an American house, fifty years later.
You know the feeling. You open the garage in the morning and there they are. Little black droppings on the shelf, like grains of dark rice. A torn corner on the bag of birdseed. Shredded paper in the box of old Christmas things. Maybe you hear scratching in the wall at night, and you lie in bed and pretend you did not hear it. And then you stand in your own garage, and you do not want to touch anything.
I want to tell you something, and please listen. It is not because your house is dirty. It was never you. A mouse does not come in because you are a bad housekeeper. It comes in because it is getting cold, because there is a door you cannot see, and because somewhere in that garage there is dinner. Three reasons. And my mother had one answer for each one.
So today I will show you all three things, one by one. What each one does, exactly where it goes, and how much to use. I will show you the tiny door almost every garage has, the one you walk past every day. The one mistake that makes everything useless. And at the end, how to clean up after them without breathing that dust. The second thing is the one that surprises people most, because it is already under your kitchen sink.
You know me, I am Sachiko. On my kitchen channel I cook the old Japanese way, the way my mother cooked. But my mother's house was more than her kitchen. Behind our little wooden house in Osaka there was a tiny yard with a persimmon tree, a few rows of daikon, and a wooden shed where my father kept his bicycle. I can still smell that shed. Dry straw, machine oil, and the rice bran my mother kept for her pickles.
Quick, while I have you. If you want to keep these three things, I wrote all of them in the description, with every measure, so you can find them again in the spring. And if you like the old ways, please subscribe. This is my garden channel, and here we go outside, to the yard, the garage and the shed. Now, back to the mice.
When I was a little girl, every house on our street had mice. In Japanese we say nezumi. The houses were wood and paper, the winters were cold, and every kitchen had rice. The neighbors bought poison and set traps. And every winter they had mice again. My mother did not. She said, you do not fight the guest. You make the house a place where the guest does not want to stay. Close the door. Take away the dinner. Make it unpleasant. That was her whole plan, and it fits in one small sentence.
My mother lived through the hungry years after the war, before I was born. When food was that precious, you did not share it with a mouse. Every grain of rice was counted. So she learned to protect food better than anybody I ever met. When I came to America in nineteen seventy, with my husband, we rented a tiny apartment with mice in the walls. I wrote to my mother. Her letter came back three weeks later. Three lines. Glass and tin. Iron wool in the holes. Chili on the seeds. I still have that letter.
Number one. Glass jars and tins with tight lids. This is the most important one, so I start with it. Most people keep the birdseed, the grass seed and the dog food in the bag they came in, on the floor of the garage. Paper, plastic, cardboard. To a mouse, that bag is not a wall. It is a door with dinner behind it. A mouse can chew through a paper bag in one night. Through a plastic bin with a thin lid, too, if it has time. Through glass and metal, never.
So here is what my mother did, and what I do. Everything a mouse can eat goes into glass or metal, with a lid that closes tight. A big glass jar for the birdseed. An old metal trash can with a lid that fits for the dog food or the chicken feed. A tin box for grass seed. My mother kept her rice in a komebitsu, a wooden rice chest lined with tin, and nothing ever got inside. Here in my garage I use old pickle jars, coffee cans with plastic lids taped down, and one metal bin I bought thirty years ago.
And do not forget the things you do not think of as food. Bulbs for the spring garden, tulips and crocus, in a paper bag on the shelf. Mice love them. Bags of potatoes and onions in a cold garage. Wild birdseed spilled under the feeder outside the garage door. Pet bowls left full overnight. A mouse only needs a little bit every day, so a handful of spilled seed is a whole week of dinner. Walk your garage with a flashlight tonight and look for anything soft that holds food. Everything soft goes into something hard.
One more thing about number one. Mice also want a bed. Cardboard boxes on the floor, old newspapers, rags, the insulation in an old cooler. My mother never kept paper on the floor of the shed. She said, a pile of newspaper is a hotel. So lift your boxes off the floor, onto a shelf, with a little space behind them against the wall. When you can see behind and under everything, a mouse has nowhere to hide, and you can see the droppings the day they appear.
And if you park a car in the garage, open the hood once in a while in the fall and look. A warm engine on a cold night is a cozy bed, and mice like to bring seeds and bits of insulation in there and chew on what they find. My neighbor had a nest on top of her engine, with dog food inside it. It came from the open bag on the floor, three steps away. Number one again. Close the food, and the car stays empty too.
Number two. Steel wool. The kind you use to scrub your pots. This is the one that surprises people, because it is already under your kitchen sink. My mother used the iron wool from her rice pot. Why steel wool? Because a mouse can squeeze through a hole the size of a dime. A dime. If its head goes through, the whole body goes through. And it can chew through wood, plastic, foam and rubber. But it cannot chew through steel wool. The sharp little wires hurt its mouth, and it gives up.
So first you find the doors. Take a flashlight, and go around the inside of your garage, low, on your knees if you can, or ask your grandson. Look where the pipes go into the wall. Where the electric cable comes in. Around the water heater. The corners where the wall meets the floor. Behind the dryer, where the vent goes out. And look for the small signs: droppings, a dark greasy rub mark on the edge of a hole, little chewed bits of wood or foam. A mouse uses the same path every night, so the greasy mark shows you the door.
Now you fill it. Pull a piece of steel wool from the pad, roll it a little, and push it into the hole with a screwdriver, hard, until it is packed tight. Then seal over it so it stays. A bead of caulk, or a little patching plaster, or for a big gap, a piece of metal mesh screwed over it. My mother used plaster. I use caulk from the hardware store. Do this in the fall, before the first cold nights, because that is when they start looking for a warm place for the winter.
And now the big door, the one almost every garage has. Look at the bottom of your garage door. The rubber strip along the floor. After a few winters it cracks, it shrinks, and at the corners there is a gap. Lie down and look from inside with the door closed. If you see daylight, a mouse sees a door. Changing that rubber strip is easy and cheap, and it closes the biggest door in your whole house. My neighbor's exterminator found exactly this gap. Four hundred dollars to tell her what you just learned.
Before I show you number three, tell me in the comments. Where do they come in at your house? The garage, the shed, the basement? I read every comment, and I want to know. And if you have a friend who is fighting mice right now, send this to her. It may save her four hundred dollars.
Number three. Red chili. Togarashi, in Japanese, the red pepper my mother hung to dry under the roof in long strings. This one is for the outside, for the bird feeder and the seed. Here is the problem. You feed the birds in winter. Seed falls on the ground. And at night, the mice come for the seed, and the squirrels in the morning. The seed under your feeder is a restaurant, and your garage is right next door.
My mother did something very simple. She mixed dried red chili into the seed she put out for the sparrows. A tablespoon of ground chili powder or cayenne for every two cups of birdseed, mixed well in a jar with a lid, shaken until every seed is red. Here is why it works, and it is a beautiful thing. Birds do not feel the heat of chili. They do not have the part in the mouth that feels it. But a mouse, a squirrel, a rat, they feel it very much. So the birds keep eating, and the mice taste it once and leave.
Two things to be careful about. Wear gloves when you mix it, and never touch your eyes. Mix it outside, with the wind behind you, because the powder flies. And this does not replace number one. The chili seed is for the feeder only. Inside the garage, the seed still lives in the glass jar. Also keep the ground under the feeder clean. Once a week I sweep the fallen shells and seed into the trash, closed, so the restaurant closes at night.
And look at what touches your house from the outside. My mother kept a strip of bare ground, a little gravel, along the bottom of the shed. Nothing growing against the wall. Ivy and thick bushes against the house are a covered hallway for a mouse. Cut them back so there is open ground between the plants and the wall. Firewood, the same. Stack it up off the ground, on a rack or old bricks, and away from the house, never against the garage wall. A woodpile against the wall is a mouse apartment with a door into your garage.
Now, you will hear people say peppermint oil keeps mice away, cotton balls in the corners. I tried it. In my experience it smells strong for a few days and then it is gone, and the mice come back. My mother did not trust smells. She trusted glass, iron and chili. A smell is a sign on the door. Steel wool is the door itself.
The one mistake that makes everything useless. Closing the doors but leaving dinner inside. If you seal the holes and the birdseed bag is still open on the floor, the mice that are already inside have everything they need, and they stay. Always number one first, the food into glass and metal. Then number two, the holes. Then number three outside. In that order. My mother said, first you empty the table, then you lock the door.
How do you know it worked? Here is my little test. After you clean, put a few sheets of white paper on the garage floor, along the wall, near where you saw droppings. Sprinkle a little flour on them, very thin. Check in the morning. If you see little footprints, there is still a door somewhere close. If the flour is clean three mornings in a row, you won. My grandson thinks this is like a detective story. It is.
If you still hear scratching after two weeks, it is not you. Look higher. Mice climb rough walls very well, and they jump up to about a foot. Look at the top of the walls, where the roof meets them, and around the attic door. And if you have many inside your house walls, in the kitchen, every night, then my garage way is slower, and that is the moment to ask a professional to look. For the garage and the shed, these three things are enough.
Now, the cleaning, and this is important. Never sweep or vacuum mouse droppings dry. The dust goes in the air, and it is not good to breathe. Open the garage door for thirty minutes first. Put on rubber gloves. Mix one part bleach with ten parts water in a spray bottle, spray the droppings and the nest until they are wet, and wait five minutes. Then pick them up with paper towels and put them in a plastic bag, closed, in the trash. Wash your gloved hands, take off the gloves, and wash your hands again.
Let me do the arithmetic, because this house does not do villains, it does arithmetic. The exterminator, four hundred dollars, and next fall maybe again. My way. A roll of steel wool pads, a few dollars. A tube of caulk, a few dollars. A rubber strip for the garage door, maybe twenty. Old glass jars, you already have them. A spoon of chili per bag of seed. Less than fifty dollars, and it lasts for years, because the door stays closed.
I remember one winter evening in Osaka. I was maybe seven. My mother and I were on our knees in the shed with a candle, looking at the bottom of the wall. She found a hole behind my father's bicycle, very small. She did not say anything. She just took the iron wool from her apron pocket, pushed it in with the handle of a spoon, and patted it twice, like a baby. Then she blew out the candle and said, now the house is closed. We never saw a mouse in that shed again.
So, here is everything one more time. Number one, glass jars and tins with tight lids for everything a mouse can eat, and nothing soft on the floor. Number two, steel wool packed into every hole the size of a dime, sealed over, and a good rubber strip under the garage door. Number three, red chili mixed into the birdseed, a tablespoon for two cups, and the ground under the feeder swept. Food first, then doors, then the outside.
