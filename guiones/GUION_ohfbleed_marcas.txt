So let us start with the question that matters most. Is it hot water, or is it steam?

Here is the ten second test. Look at the pipes going into the radiator.

If there are two pipes, one going in near the bottom on one side, and one coming out on the other side, it is almost always hot water. Water comes in, gives off its heat, and goes back to the boiler.

If there is only one pipe, going into the bottom of one end, it is very likely steam. Steam comes up that one pipe, gives off its heat, turns back into water, and the water runs back down the same pipe.

Now look at the top of the radiator, on the end away from the pipe. On a hot water radiator, you will find that little bleed valve with the square in it, or sometimes a slotted screw. On a one pipe steam radiator, you will find something different. A little metal vent, about the size of a big thumb, sometimes shaped like a bullet, sometimes like a little can, sticking out of the side. That is a steam vent. It lets air out by itself, and closes when the steam gets there.

And one more check, down in the basement. Look at the boiler. A steam boiler has a little glass tube on the side, a sight glass, with water in it about halfway up. A hot water boiler does not have that. It has a gauge that reads pressure and temperature, and usually a pump, a small motor, on one of the pipes.

If you see a sight glass and a vent on the radiator, it is steam. Do not put a key on it. Do not unscrew that vent while the heat is on. Steam is hotter than boiling water and it comes out fast. We will come back to steam in a few minutes, because there is a fix for that too.

Step one. Turn the heat off and let the radiators cool down. Turn the thermostat down so the boiler stops calling, and give it a while. Two reasons. Hot water from a bleed valve can burn your hand. And when the pump stops, the air in the system drifts up and collects at the high points, which is exactly where you are going to let it out.

Step two. Pick the order. Start with the radiators on the lowest floor, the ones closest to the boiler, and work your way up and out. The ones upstairs, and the ones farthest from the boiler, go last. Air rises. If you start at the top, you just move air from downstairs up to where you already bled. Work bottom to top, near to far.

Step three. Get ready. Fold the towel under the valve. Hold the cup right under the little hole, which is usually on the side of the valve body, pointing down or sideways. Look at it before you start so you know where the water will come out.

Step four. Put the key on the square and turn it counterclockwise. A quarter of a turn. That is all. Maybe a half turn if nothing happens. Never more than that.

And here is the mistake I promised you. The one that soaks a ceiling. People keep turning. They want it to go faster. And on a lot of old valves, the little screw inside comes right out. And now water is coming out of a hole the size of a pencil lead, it is hot, and the screw is somewhere in the towel. You will not get it back in against that pressure with wet fingers. So the water runs until somebody shuts off the boiler and the water feed, and by then it is through the floorboards. A quarter turn. Let the air do the work.

Step five. Listen. You will hear a hiss. That is the air. It might hiss for a few seconds, or for a while on a radiator that was really full. Let it. Do not close it yet.

Step six. Watch for water. The hiss gets sputtery. A little spit of water, then air, then water. When you get a steady little stream of water with no air in it, close the valve. Clockwise. Snug, not hard. These are small brass parts, and they strip.

Now, people always ask me where the air comes from in the first place. It is a closed system. How does air get in?

A few ways. Fresh water has air dissolved in it. Every time the system takes on new water, through that fill valve, or after somebody drains it to fix something, a little air comes in with it. When the water heats up, that air comes out of the water, the same way bubbles form in a pot before it boils. And it rises, and it collects at the tops of the radiators on the top floor.

Air also gets in through small leaks. A valve stem that weeps a little. A fitting that drips once in a while. If you have to bleed the same radiator every couple of weeks, that is not normal. Something is letting air in, or letting water out, and it is worth having somebody look before it gets worse.

So how often should you bleed? Once at the start of the heating season is the usual habit. Before the first real cold night, go around the house in the right order. And then any time you hear that trickling sound, or feel a radiator that is hot at the bottom and cold at the top. Some houses go years without needing it. Some need it every fall. You get to know your house.

Now, the last step. The one everybody forgets.

Go back down to the boiler and look at the pressure gauge.

Every bit of air you let out was taking up space in the system. When it leaves, the pressure drops. On most hot water systems, there is a valve on the cold water line that feeds the boiler, called a fill valve or a pressure reducing valve, and it is supposed to top the system back up on its own. But on old systems, those valves get stuck, or somebody closed the shut off next to it years ago.

So look at the gauge. On a lot of two story houses, a cold system usually sits somewhere around twelve to fifteen pounds. Your boiler may have a sticker or a manual that says the right number for your house. If the needle is sitting way low, well under where it normally lives, the system did not refill. And if you leave it like that, the water will not push all the way up to the top floor, and that upstairs radiator will go cold again in a day or two.

That is the thing. They bleed, it works, then it fades, and they think bleeding does not work. It did. The pressure was never put back.

If your boiler pressure is low and you are not sure how your fill valve works, that is a good point to call your heating company. It is a quick visit. If the pressure keeps dropping week after week, that means there is a leak somewhere, or a valve dripping in the basement, and that also needs somebody to look at it.

And if the gauge ever reads high, up near thirty, which is usually where the relief valve sits, stop and call. Do not bleed to fix high pressure. That is a different problem.

Then turn the heat back on, let it run, and go feel your radiators. Hot all the way to the top. That is what you want.

Now, what if it is still cold after you bled it?

If the top is hot now but the bottom stays cold, that is not air. That is usually sludge, years of rust and grit settled in the bottom of the radiator. Bleeding will not touch it. That radiator needs to be flushed, and in an old system, that is a job for somebody who does boilers.

If the whole radiator is cold, top and bottom, check the valve on the pipe going into it. Somebody may have closed it to cool down a room years ago. Open it all the way.

If it is only the radiator farthest from the boiler, or only the top floor, and the pressure is right, the system may need balancing. That is the little valves on each radiator being adjusted so the near ones do not steal all the hot water. That is worth a professional too.

Now, steam. Because a lot of old houses in the north are steam, and if that is you, I did not forget you.

You do not bleed a steam radiator with a key. The vent on the side does the bleeding by itself. Every time the heat comes on, it lets the air out and then closes when the steam hits it. So when a steam radiator stays cold, the first suspect is that vent. They clog with mineral and rust over the years, or they get painted shut, or they stick closed.

The fix is to replace the vent. With the heat off and the radiator cold, it unscrews by hand or with a wrench, and a new one screws in. Take the old one to a heating supply house or a good hardware store so they can match the size and the type. They come in different speeds, and the ones near the end of a long run are often a faster type.

Two other things on steam. First, the valve at the bottom of a one pipe steam radiator has to be all the way open or all the way closed. Never halfway. Halfway traps water in the radiator, and that is where the banging comes from. And second, a one pipe steam radiator should tilt very slightly toward that pipe, so the water can run back down. If somebody shimmed it level or the floor settled the wrong way, you get water trapped in it, cold spots, and noise. A thin wood shim under the feet on the far end usually fixes that.

And again, never take a steam vent off while the heat is on.
