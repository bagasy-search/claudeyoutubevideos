Now let me tell you why the old way fails, because it's not your fault. It's what everybody was taught.
A dry bean is a seed. It's been dried until it's hard as a rock, so it'll keep for a year in a sack. To cook it, you have to get water back inside it, all the way to the middle. That's what soaking is for. The skin softens, water soaks in slowly, and the bean swells back up.
The problem is, soaking all night is slow, and you have to remember to do it the night before. Most people don't. So the beans go in the pot hard, and they boil and boil, and the outside cooks to mush while the middle is still chalky.
And then people pour off the soaking water and salt at the end, because somebody told them salt makes beans tough. That's not true. That's an old story. Cooks have tested it a hundred ways. Salt in the water at the start actually makes the skins softer, not harder, and it seasons the bean all the way to the middle. Salt at the end only sits on the outside. That's why your beans taste like nothing, and the broth tastes like salt.
The last mistake is the big one. Boiling. A hard, rolling boil bangs the beans against each other and against the pot. The skins split, the outside falls apart, and the middle still isn't done. Beans want a lazy bubble. Not a boil.
Don Ernesto, the cook who taught me, used to say, "Beans don't like to be rushed, and they don't like to wait." It took me a while. Here's what he meant.

A couple of questions people always ask.
Do I have to use the dried chiles? No. They add color and a little sweetness. Leave them out and the beans are still good.
Can I use a pressure cooker or a slow cooker? You can. They work. The pressure cooker is fast and the slow cooker is easy. But you'll lose a little of that thick, red broth that comes from simmering with the lid cracked. If you use a slow cooker, do the two-minute boil and the one-hour rest in a pot on the stove first. Raw beans that never get to a full boil don't cook right in a slow cooker.
What about canned beans? Canned beans are fine in a pinch. I've opened plenty of cans. But a can of beans costs about what a whole pound of dry beans does, and a pound of dry beans makes three cans' worth. And they don't taste like this.
So how do you pick a good bag at the store, if old beans are the enemy? Look through the plastic. Fresh pintos are smooth and a little shiny, speckled pink and brown, all about the same size. Old ones look pale and dusty, and a lot of them are wrinkled or cracked. Turn the bag over. If there's a pile of broken pieces and bean dust in the corners, put it back. And if the bag has a date on it, read it. Same price, same shelf. One bag cooks in two hours. The other one never gets there.
And the salt question. You've heard you can't salt beans at the start or they'll never get soft. Don Ernesto believed that, and so did I for years. Then one roundup I ran out of time and salted a pot at the start, and I cooked a second pot the old way, same beans, same day.
