[OLD | a bag of store rolls on a counter, a hand squeezing one and it stays squashed] So let me tell you the old way that doesn't work, the way most people try it today. They heat the milk until it is good and hot, because they think hot makes the yeast work faster. They add way too much flour, because the dough is sticky and they don't like sticky. And they put the rolls far apart on a cookie sheet so each one has room.
[OLD | a thermometer in a pan of steaming milk reading too high] And every one of those things is backwards. Hot milk kills yeast. Yeast is alive, honey, and above about a hundred and thirty degrees it dies. So your rolls never rise, and you think it was a bad packet.
[OLD | a heavy ball of dry dough on a floury board, a hand pushing on it and it doesn't give] Too much flour makes the dough tight and dry, so the rolls are heavy. And rolls that are far apart on a cookie sheet bake a hard crust all the way around. When they are touching in a pan, they hold each other up, they rise tall, and the sides stay soft.
[TRICK | she lifts the lid off a pot of boiled potatoes, cloudy water steaming] Now here is the thing. Here is what Irene kept in her kitchen. Potato water. The water you boil your potatoes in. And one boiled potato, mashed. That's the whole secret of a church roll.
[TRICK | she pours the cloudy water through a strainer into a glass measuring cup] The starch from the potato keeps the roll soft and moist, and it keeps it from going stale. And the yeast loves it. It eats the potato and it rises like it is in a race. Every farm wife in Iowa knew this, because nobody threw anything away, and that water was free.
[TRICK | an index card in faded pencil that says potato water, underlined twice] When Irene gave me her recipe card, she had written potato water across the top and underlined it twice. And she said, Loretta, if you ever pour this down the sink, don't you dare tell me.
[RECIPE | the floury table set with flour, sugar, butter, an egg, a little packet of yeast and a bowl of mashed potato] So here is the recipe. This makes two dozen rolls. You will need one medium potato, peeled. One packet of active dry yeast, that is two and a quarter teaspoons. One cup of milk. A third of a cup of sugar. A third of a cup of butter. One egg. A teaspoon and a half of salt. And about four and a half to five cups of all-purpose flour.
[RECIPE | a potato boiling in a small pot] First, boil the potato in about two cups of water until it is soft, fifteen or twenty minutes. Save the water. Mash the potato until it is smooth, and measure out half a cup. And measure half a cup of that potato water and let it cool.
[RECIPE | milk heating in a saucepan, small bubbles at the edge] Next, warm the milk with the butter and the sugar, just until the butter melts. Then let it cool down until it is warm, not hot. And here is how you know. Put a drop on the inside of your wrist. If it feels warm, like a baby's bath, it is right. If you have a thermometer, you want about a hundred and ten degrees.
[RECIPE | she sprinkles the yeast over the potato water in a bowl] Now sprinkle the yeast over the warm potato water, with a pinch of the sugar, and wait ten minutes. It should get foamy, like the head on a root beer. If it doesn't foam, your yeast is old, and you stop right there and get new yeast. That ten minutes saves you a whole morning.
[RECIPE | she stirs the milk, the egg and the mashed potato into the big bowl] In your big bowl, stir together the foamy yeast, the warm milk, the egg, the half cup of mashed potato and the salt. Then add the flour one cup at a time, stirring with a wooden spoon, until you have a soft dough that pulls away from the bowl.
[FLOUR | she spoons flour into a measuring cup and levels it with the back of a knife] And let me tell you how to measure the flour, because this is where most heavy rolls come from. Don't scoop the cup down into the flour bag. That packs it in, and you get way too much. Spoon the flour into the cup, and level it off with the back of a knife. That is how Irene did it and that is how the recipe works.
[FLOUR | a can of shortening and a crock of lard beside a stick of butter on the table] Now at the church, back in the fifties, we used shortening most of the time, or lard from somebody's hogs, because it was cheap and the rolls came out very tender. Butter tastes better. I use butter now. Any of the three works the same in this recipe, the same amount.
[KNEAD | she turns the dough out on the floured table and kneads with the heels of her hands] Turn it out on a floured table and knead it for eight to ten minutes. Push it away with the heels of your hands, fold it back, turn it a quarter turn, and push again. And here is the part people get wrong. The dough should still be a little sticky. Tacky, like the back of a postage stamp. Don't keep adding flour until it is dry. Sticky dough makes soft rolls.
[KNEAD | she presses a finger into the dough and it springs back] You know it is done when it is smooth and it springs back when you poke it. Irene used to say the dough should feel like the inside of your arm. And then she would poke my arm to show me, every single time, for twenty years.
[KNEAD | she holds up her two hands, flour to the wrists, and laughs] And if your hands are like mine and they don't want to knead for ten minutes anymore, you can use a stand mixer with the dough hook. Medium low speed, six or seven minutes. But I will tell you, honey, kneading by hand is the best ten minutes of quiet you will get all week.
[FRIDGE | she puts the dough in a buttered bowl and covers it with a plate, then into the refrigerator] And now here is the answer to the third thing, the one about no time on a holiday. You don't have to bake these today. Put the dough in a big buttered bowl, turn it over once so the top is buttered too, cover it tight, and put it in the refrigerator. Overnight, or for up to three days.
[FRIDGE | an old refrigerator full of covered bowls of dough on the shelves] We called these refrigerator rolls. The cold slows the yeast way down, so the dough rises slowly in the refrigerator, and the slow rise gives it more flavor. At the church we would mix forty pounds of dough on a Friday, fill every refrigerator in the basement, and bake on Sunday morning. It is the oldest make-ahead trick there is.

[SHAPE | the cold dough on the floury table, she cuts it with a bench scraper] Now, baking day. Take the dough out of the refrigerator. You can shape it cold, it is easier cold. Punch it down and cut it into twenty four pieces. Cut it in half, then each half in half, and keep going, and they will come out even.
[SHAPE | she rolls a piece of dough into a smooth ball under her cupped hand] To shape a roll, take a piece and pull the edges under, so the top is smooth and tight. Then put it on the table under your cupped hand and roll it in little circles until it is a round ball. A tight top is what makes a roll rise up instead of out.
[SHAPE | the balls of dough set in rows in a buttered nine by thirteen pan, all touching] Put them in a buttered nine by thirteen pan, four rows of six, so they are just touching. Close. Like a church pew at Christmas. Cover them with a dish towel and let them rise in a warm place until they are doubled, about an hour, maybe an hour and a half because the dough was cold.
[SHAPE | she sets the pan inside the oven with only the oven light on] And if your kitchen is cold, here is a trick. Put the pan in the oven with the oven turned off and only the light on. The little bulb makes it just warm enough. Just remember to take them out before you turn the oven on. I forgot one Easter, and I baked a dish towel.
[BAKE | the oven dial turned to three hundred seventy five] Bake them at three hundred seventy five degrees for about eighteen to twenty minutes, until the tops are a deep golden brown. If you have a thermometer, the middle should read about one hundred ninety degrees. If you don't, tip one out and tap the bottom. It should sound hollow, like knocking on a door.
[BAKE | she brushes melted butter over the hot rolls, the pan steaming] And the minute they come out, brush them with melted butter. Right away, while they are hot. [SHAPES | three different shapes of rolls on a wooden board: cloverleaf, folded and crescent] Now, the same dough makes all the church rolls. [SHAPES | she folds a buttered circle of dough in half and presses the edge] For Parker House rolls, the ones that look like a little purse, you roll the dough out half an inch thick, cut circles with a glass, brush them with butter and fold each one in half. [SHAPES | a pan of cinnamon rolls with white icing dripping down] And on the last Sunday of the month, we used a third of the dough for cinnamon rolls. [MISTAKES | Loretta at the table, holding up one hand and counting] Now, five things that go wrong, and how to fix them. One. If the dough won't rise, it is the yeast, or it was too hot, or your kitchen is cold. Test your yeast first, and use the oven light.
[MISTAKES | she shows two rolls side by side, one tall and one flat] Two. If your rolls spread out flat, the dough was too soft or the rolls were not shaped tight on top. Pull that top tight. Three. [MISTAKES | she cuts a roll open showing a gummy pale middle] Four. If the middle is gummy, they were not baked long enough. Go by the color and the hollow knock, not the clock. And five. If they come out hard, you baked them too far apart, or you forgot the butter and the towel.
