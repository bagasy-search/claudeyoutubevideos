Creamed chipped beef on toast. Red-eye gravy over country ham. A crispy slab of fried mush with syrup running off the side. Twenty-five breakfasts that every diner in America served at six in the morning, and you can't find most of them anywhere today. I cooked every single one. And this week I'm going to show you how to bring them back, in your own kitchen, for less than a dollar a plate.
A few months ago my grandson took me out for breakfast. Nice place, music, plants hanging from the ceiling. Two eggs, a little pile of potatoes, one slice of toast cut in a triangle. Nineteen dollars. Coffee was four more. I looked at that plate and I thought, in nineteen sixty-nine I served a trucker more food than this for sixty-five cents, and he left a dime under the cup. I'm not saying that to be a grumpy old man. I'm saying it because those breakfasts were cheap for a reason. They were built out of leftovers, cornmeal, a can of something, and a cook who knew what he was doing.
And maybe you remember one of them. Your dad ordered something at the counter, you were seven years old, and you never saw it again. You tried to make it at home and it came out wrong, and you figured you just didn't have the knack. Listen to me. It was never you. Nobody wrote these down. They lived in the hands of guys like me, and we're running out.
So here's what I'm going to do. Twenty-five forgotten diner breakfasts, counting down from twenty-five to number one. What each one is, who ordered it, what it cost on my menu, and the one thing you have to get right to make it at home. Number nineteen, one man ordered every single morning for thirty-one years. And number one is the plate my wife still asks me for every Sunday. A couple of these, the young cooks are going to tell you are old-fashioned. Let them.
You know me, I'm Lou. Fifty years behind a griddle, starting in nineteen sixty-nine, when I was seventeen and couldn't crack an egg with one hand. I still remember the smell when I unlocked the door at five in the morning. Cold grease, bleach on the floor, and the coffee urn hissing like it was mad at me. Grab a cup. Let's go.
Number twenty-five. Milk toast. This was the cheapest thing on my board, fifteen cents, and it was for the sick and the old. Two slices of toast, buttered hot, in a bowl, and you pour warm milk over the top with a pinch of salt and a little pepper. That's it. Old men with bad stomachs ate it every day. Mothers ordered it for a kid who'd been throwing up. The trick is the milk can't boil. You warm it until it steams at the edges and you take it off. Boiled milk gets a skin and tastes scorched. Steam, not bubbles.
Number twenty-four. Broiled grapefruit. Half a grapefruit, cut around every section with a little curved knife, a spoon of brown sugar on top, under the broiler for about four minutes until the sugar bubbles. A maraschino cherry in the middle, because it was nineteen seventy. Ladies who were watching their figure ordered it with black coffee. It cost a quarter. The mistake at home is cutting the sections after you broil it. Cut first, then the sugar melts down into every cut. Knife first, fire second.
Number twenty-three. Cinnamon toast fingers. Thick white bread, buttered all the way to the crust, sugar and cinnamon mixed four to one, and you lay it butter side down on the griddle, not in a toaster. The sugar turns into a thin crackly shell, like the top of a fancy custard. Then you cut it in three fingers. Kids came in with their grandpa and got this with a glass of milk for twenty cents. At home, use a pan on medium low. Too hot and the sugar burns before the bread warms through.
Number twenty-two. Fried mush. You cook yellow cornmeal the night before, one cup of meal to four cups of water and a teaspoon of salt, stir it twenty minutes until a spoon stands up. Pour it in a loaf pan and put it in the icebox overnight. In the morning it's a brick. You slice it half an inch thick, dust it with flour and fry it in bacon fat until it's crispy on both sides. Then butter and syrup, or a fried egg on top. Thirty cents. The farm guys loved it. If yours falls apart in the pan, it wasn't cold enough. Overnight, not two hours.
Number twenty-one. Buckwheat cakes with molasses. Not pancakes, buckwheat cakes. Darker, a little sour, with a taste like the ground. The old way, you mix buckwheat flour with water and a spoon of yeast the night before and let it sit on the counter. By morning it's bubbling and smells a little like beer. A pinch of baking soda right before they hit the griddle, and they puff up. Dark molasses on top, not syrup. The railroad men ordered a short stack and a pork chop. If they come out heavy, you skipped the night. The overnight is the whole recipe.
Number twenty. Corn fritters. A can of corn kernels, drained hard, an egg, half a cup of flour, a teaspoon of baking powder, salt, and a splash of the corn water. You drop spoonfuls into hot fat, about three hundred and fifty degrees, and they come out like little golden clouds with corn popping inside. Powdered sugar or maple syrup. Thirty-five cents for six. People forget them because nobody wants a pot of hot fat at seven in the morning. Use a skillet with half an inch of oil. You don't need a fryer.
Number nineteen. Fried bologna and egg on toast. This is the one. A man named Walt, a mailman, sat on the third stool from the end every morning for thirty-one years and ordered this. Thick bologna, and here's the trick. You cut a little slit from the edge toward the middle, four slits around the slice. If you don't, it puffs up like a dome in the pan and only the middle browns. With the slits it lies flat and gets crispy all over. Fried egg on top, yellow mustard, white toast. Forty cents. When Walt retired, he came in one last time and paid for the whole counter.
Number eighteen. Egg in a basket. Some folks call it egg in a hole. You take a glass, press out a circle from the middle of a slice of bread, butter both sides, put it on the griddle and crack the egg right in the hole. Then you fry the little circle next to it, and that's for dipping. The kids thought it was magic. At home, here's where people mess up. They crack the egg in too early. Let the bread toast for a minute first, then a little butter in the hole, then the egg. Flip it once. Soft yolk, crispy bread.
Number seventeen. Eggs Goldenrod. This one came out of the hard times of the thirties, and by my time it was an Easter morning special. Hard-boiled eggs. You chop the whites and stir them into a white sauce, two tablespoons of butter, two of flour, a cup of milk. Pour that over toast, and then you push the yolks through a strainer right over the top, so it looks like goldenrod flowers in a field. That's the name. Forty-five cents. The secret is the yolks. Don't chop them. Push them through the strainer. That yellow powder on top is the whole show.
Number sixteen. The Western sandwich. Before anybody called it a Denver omelet, it was a sandwich. Diced ham, green pepper and onion, cooked on the griddle for a minute, then two beaten eggs poured right over them in a square the size of the bread. Fold the corners in, flip it once, and slap it between two slices of buttered toast. Fifty cents, and a man could eat it with one hand on the steering wheel. The trick is cooking the onion and pepper first. Raw onion in an egg tastes sharp all day.
Number fifteen. Salmon croquettes and eggs. A can of salmon, the cheap pink kind, bones and all, because the little soft bones mash right in. One egg, half a cup of cracker crumbs, a little chopped onion, pepper. You shape them into patties, chill them twenty minutes, and fry them in a little bacon fat until they're brown. Two croquettes and two eggs, sixty cents. Fridays, all the Catholic families. If they fall apart, they weren't chilled. Twenty minutes in the cold and they hold together like they're glued.
Number fourteen. Codfish cakes and baked beans. This was a Saturday morning thing, and it came from New England. Salt cod, soaked overnight in cold water, changing the water twice, then flaked and mixed with mashed potatoes, about half and half, and an egg. Fried in patties, with a scoop of baked beans next to them and a slice of brown bread. Fifty-five cents. Most people today never soaked salt cod. If you skip the soaking it's like eating the ocean. Two changes of water, overnight. Taste a flake before you mix it.
Number thirteen. Kippers and eggs. Smoked herring, split open, out of a flat can. The old men from the rail yard ordered it, and you could smell it from the door. You just warm the kippers on the griddle with a pat of butter, two minutes a side, and put them next to two eggs over easy and buttered toast. Fifty cents. Young people make a face, I know. But it's the same fish oil the doctor tells you to buy in a pill. And a can is still about two dollars. Squeeze of lemon. Done.
Number twelve. Scrapple. If you're from Pennsylvania, you just smiled. Pork scraps cooked down, mixed with cornmeal and buckwheat flour and spices, set in a loaf, and sliced. We bought it in bricks from a farm. The secret to frying scrapple is leaving it alone. Slice it a quarter inch thick, a little fat in a hot pan, and do not touch it for five minutes. If you move it, it sticks and falls apart. When the edge is dark brown, flip it once. Crispy outside, soft in the middle. Two slices, two eggs, fifty cents. Apple butter on the side, or ketchup if you're a heathen.
Number eleven. Fried apples and sausage links. You slice two apples, skins on, cook them in the sausage grease with a spoon of brown sugar and a pinch of cinnamon until they're soft and shiny. Sausage links on one side of the plate, fried apples on the other, and a biscuit. In the fall, when apples were cheap, this was forty-five cents. The trick is the grease. You cook the sausage first, take it out, and the apples go into that same pan. That pork fat with the sugar is what makes people close their eyes.
Number ten. Hangtown fry. This one goes back to the California gold rush. The story is a miner struck it rich and asked for the most expensive breakfast in the house, and that was eggs, bacon and oysters. You dredge a few oysters in flour and fry them in the bacon fat for one minute, then pour beaten eggs right over them and the bacon, and cook it like a flat omelet. On my menu it was a dollar twenty-five, the most expensive thing on the board. A canned smoked oyster works at home. One minute on the oysters. They get rubbery fast.
Number nine. Country ham with red-eye gravy and grits. You fry a thick slice of salty country ham until the fat around the edge is clear and brown. Take the ham out. Then you pour half a cup of black coffee into that hot pan, and it hisses and lifts up all the brown bits. Let it bubble a minute. That's red-eye gravy. Thin, salty, a little bitter, and you spoon it over the grits and the ham. Seventy cents. People ask me where the name comes from. When it settles in the bowl, the fat makes a little circle in the middle, like a red eye looking at you.
Number eight. Pork chop and eggs. A thin bone-in chop, maybe half an inch, salt and pepper and a dusting of flour, on a hot griddle three minutes a side. Two eggs, toast and potatoes. Ninety cents. This was a working man's breakfast, guys who were going to swing a hammer for ten hours. At home, here's the thing. People cook it too long because they're scared of pork. Use a thermometer and pull it at one hundred and forty-five degrees in the middle, let it rest three minutes. Juicy, not shoe leather.
Number seven. Shirred eggs. Fancy name, simple dish. You butter a small baking dish, pour in two tablespoons of cream, crack two eggs on top, a little salt and pepper, and it goes in a hot oven, three hundred and seventy-five degrees, for about twelve minutes, until the whites are set and the yolks still shake. Toast fingers for dipping. Sixty-five cents. The lady from the bank ordered it every Tuesday, with her gloves still on. The mistake is waiting until the yolk looks done. It keeps cooking in the hot dish. Take it out while it still wiggles.
Number six. Golden Buck. Hardly anybody alive has eaten this. It's Welsh rarebit with a poached egg on top. You melt sharp cheddar into a little warm milk with a spoon of mustard and a dash of hot sauce, pour that over toast, and set a poached egg right in the middle. Seventy-five cents. When you cut the egg, the yolk runs into the cheese, and I'm telling you, that's breakfast. The secret is low heat on the cheese. If it boils, it splits into grease and rubber. Melt it slow and stir the whole time.
Number five. Creamed chicken on waffles. The Pennsylvania Dutch did this, and on Sundays the after-church crowd would line up out the door. A crisp waffle, and over it, chunks of chicken in a thick gravy made from the chicken broth, butter and flour, with a little black pepper. Eighty-five cents. Sweet and salty together, before anybody called it brunch. At home, use leftover roast chicken and a cup of broth. The waffle has to be crisp, so it doesn't turn to mush under the gravy. Make the gravy thick enough to coat a spoon.
Number four. Chicken fried steak and eggs. A cube steak, that's a cheap round steak run through a machine that tenderizes it. Dip it in flour, then egg and milk, then flour again, and fry it in a skillet until it's golden and crunchy. Then you make cream gravy in that same pan, with the drippings, flour and milk, lots of black pepper. Two eggs on the side. A dollar ten. The truckers ordered this at four in the morning. The trick is the double dip. Flour, egg, flour. One dip and the crust slides right off.
Number three. Sausage and biscuits with sawmill gravy. Biscuits from scratch, two cups of flour, a tablespoon of baking powder, a stick of cold butter cut in, and three quarters of a cup of cold buttermilk. Don't knead them, fold them three times and cut straight down. Then the gravy. Crumble a pound of breakfast sausage, brown it, sprinkle in a quarter cup of flour, stir one minute, and pour in three cups of milk. Stir until it bubbles and thickens. Eighty cents. If your biscuits come out flat, you twisted the cutter. Push straight down, lift straight up.
Number two. Creamed chipped beef on toast. The soldiers coming home from the war had a name for it, and I can't say it on here, but you know it. Thin dried beef from a jar, rinsed once so it's not too salty, torn up and dropped into a white sauce, butter, flour and milk, with a lot of black pepper. Ladled over toast. Sixty cents. The veterans sat together on Saturdays and ordered it and complained about it and cleaned the plate. The secret is cooking the beef in the butter for a minute before the flour. It crisps the edges and the sauce tastes like beef, not like paste.
Number one. Corned beef hash. Not the kind out of a can. Real hash, from the corned beef you cooked on Sunday. You dice the leftover beef and boiled potatoes the same size, a little onion, and you press it into a hot buttered pan like you're mad at it. Then you leave it alone. Eight minutes, no stirring, until the bottom is a dark brown crust. You flip it in big pieces, crust side up, and put two eggs on top. Seventy-five cents in nineteen seventy. My wife June still asks for it every Sunday. And every Sunday I tell her the same thing. The crust is the hash. Everything else is just potatoes.
Now let's talk money, because that's the part nobody tells you. A breakfast out for two today, with coffee and tip, that's forty dollars without trying. Now look at what we just did. A pot of cornmeal mush is maybe fifty cents and it's six breakfasts. A can of pink salmon is about four dollars and it makes eight croquettes. A dozen eggs, a pound of sausage and a bag of flour, and you've got biscuits and gravy for a family of four for under eight dollars. Every one of these was invented by people who didn't have money, to feed people who worked hard. That's why they were cheap then, and that's why they're cheap now.
Here's how I'd do it. Sunday, cook a corned beef, and that's Monday's hash. Sunday night, a pot of mush in the icebox for the week. Keep a can of salmon and a can of kippers in the cupboard for the morning you've got nothing. You don't need a diner. You need a cast iron pan, a spatula and twenty minutes.
