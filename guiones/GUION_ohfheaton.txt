So let us start with the part that nobody explains, because once you see it, the rest of this is easy.

Your house does not use heat. Your house loses heat. All day, all night, through every wall, every window, the attic, the floor, every crack around every door. Heat moves from warm to cold. That is all it does. It never stops.

And here is the key. How fast your house loses heat depends on how big the difference is between inside and outside. If it is sixty-eight inside and twenty outside, that is a big difference, and heat pours out fast. If it is sixty inside and twenty outside, the difference is smaller, and heat leaks out slower.

Think of a bucket with a hole in the bottom. The fuller the bucket, the harder the water pushes out of that hole. Your furnace is the hose filling the bucket. Your thermostat setting is how full you keep it. Keep it full all the time, and you are pushing water out of that hole all the time.

So when you turn the heat down, the house cools off, and as it cools, it loses heat more slowly. Every hour the house sits cooler, it leaks less. And the furnace does not have to put back what never leaked out.

Now, here is where the brother-in-law comes in. He says, but then the furnace has to work so hard to heat it back up. And he is right that it runs for a while in the morning. But think about what it is putting back. It is putting back the heat the house lost overnight. And the house lost less heat overnight because it was cooler. So the heat you put back in the morning is less than the heat you would have lost by keeping it warm all night.

That is the whole thing. A cooler house leaks less. The recovery in the morning only puts back what leaked. So, for most homes, turning it down saves money. Not the other way around.

And in an old house, here is something most people never think about. An old house usually leaks more than a new one. Older windows, less insulation, more gaps. That means it loses heat faster when it is warm. And that means turning it down usually matters more in an old house, not less.

So for the most common setup in America, a gas or oil furnace with ducts, the answer is pretty simple. Turn it down at night. Turn it down when nobody is home. And let it come back up before you get up or before you walk in the door.

But here is the part where I have to slow down. Because there are three kinds of heat where you cannot just copy that advice.

Number one. A heat pump.

A heat pump is not a furnace. It moves heat from outside into your house, even when it is cold out. It is very efficient when it is running steady. But most heat pumps in cold country have a backup. Electric heating strips. Basically a big toaster in the air handler. And if you drop the temperature eight or ten degrees overnight, then ask for it all back at once in the morning, a lot of thermostats call in those strips to catch up. And those strips are expensive to run.

So with a heat pump, the savings from a deep setback can disappear in one morning. What works better is a small setback, two or three degrees, or a thermostat that is made for heat pumps and brings the temperature back slowly without calling in the backup heat. If your thermostat has a light that says aux heat or emergency heat, and you see it come on every morning, that is your sign.

Number two. Old boilers and cast iron radiators. Like the upstairs of our house.

Radiators hold a lot of heat. That is the good part. They keep a room comfortable long after the boiler shuts off. But it also means they are slow. If you let a radiator house cool way down overnight, it can take hours to come back up. You will be sitting in a cold kitchen at breakfast wondering why the thermostat says it is calling for heat.

A cooler house still loses less heat with radiators too. The physics does not change. But the comfort does. So with radiators, people usually do better with a smaller setback, a few degrees, and the warm up set to start earlier, maybe an hour or two before they get up. Some of the newer boiler controls handle this on their own. If you have one, read the manual for the word outdoor reset. That is a good feature on a boiler.

Number three. Radiant floors. Heat in the floor, with hot water tubing under it, or in a concrete slab. Those are the slowest of all. A slab can take most of a day to really change temperature. For radiant floors, most people leave the temperature close to steady, or use a very small setback. Deep setbacks just do not have time to pay off.

Now, the one number I promised you. The one you never go below in an old house in winter.

Fifty-five degrees.

When you leave for a weekend, or a week, or you go to stay with your daughter for the holidays, you will be tempted to turn the heat way down. Or off. Do not. Set it no lower than about fifty-five. And here is why.

The thermostat measures the air in one spot. Usually a hallway, in the middle of the house. But your water pipes do not live in the hallway. They live in outside walls. Under kitchen sinks on outside walls. In crawl spaces. Along the rim of the basement where the cold comes right through the foundation. Those spots can be a lot colder than the thermostat. If the hallway is fifty, the pipe behind the kitchen sink on the north wall can be close to freezing.

And an old house is the worst for this. Pipes that were run before anybody worried about insulation. Plumbing added in a back porch that got closed in. A bathroom tucked under the eaves.

And a frozen pipe does not just stop the water. Water expands when it freezes. The pipe splits. And you do not find out until it thaws and the water starts running inside the wall. That is a repair bill that will wipe out every dollar you ever saved with a thermostat.

So, fifty-five as the floor. And if you are leaving in a deep cold spell, open the cabinet doors under the sinks on outside walls so the warm air can get to the pipes.
