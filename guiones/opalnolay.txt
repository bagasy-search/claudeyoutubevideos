Now, when the eggs drop off, I don't panic and I don't buy anything. I check seven things, in order. Cheapest first. Most of them don't cost you a penny. Just your eyes and a little patience.
Number one is free. Count your daylight. A hen needs about fourteen hours of light to lay steady. That's not a guess, that's how her body works. The light coming in through her eyes tells her it's spring, it's safe, raise some chicks.
When the days get shorter than twelve hours, most hens slow way down. Some just stop. In June we had over fifteen hours of light here. By the first week of October we were down around eleven and a half. That's a big drop in a few months, and the hens feel every minute of it.
I keep a little calendar tacked up inside the coop door. Every morning I write down how many eggs, and once a week I write down when the sun came up and when it went down. You'd be surprised how fast you learn to read it. When the egg number drops and the daylight number drops right along with it, I don't have to guess anymore.
Here's how you know it's the light and not something worse. The whole flock slows down at once. Nobody looks sick. They're eating, they're scratching, they're fussing at each other like always. They just aren't laying. That's the light, honey.
Now, can you add light in the coop? You can. A plain timer and a regular LED bulb. I've done it. You turn it on early in the morning, never at night, so the hens still go to roost when the sun goes down on its own. If the lights go off on them in the dark, they can't find the roost.
But I'll be honest with you. I don't light my older hens anymore. A hen is born with only so many eggs in her. Winter is her rest. I let my old girls rest, and they come back in the spring when the days get long. That's my choice. Yours might be different, and that's alright.
Number two, also free. Look for the molt. And this is where most folks get scared, because a molting hen looks just awful.
Once a year, usually in the fall, a hen drops her old feathers and grows a whole new coat. And while she's growing feathers, she stops laying. Her body can't do both. It puts everything into the feathers.
Here's what you'll see. Feathers all over the coop floor, like somebody had a pillow fight in there. It usually starts at the head and the neck, then goes down the back. And then you'll see these. Pin feathers. They look like little straws, little quills poking out of her skin.
Now this is important. A molting hen has bright eyes, she's eating, she's moving around. She looks rough, but she acts fine. The bald spots are even on both sides. No scabs, no crusty skin.
If you see scabs, or one bare spot in the middle of her back and nowhere else, that's not the molt. That's usually a rooster, or the other hens picking on her. Different problem, different fix.
And if she's sitting puffed up in a corner with her eyes half closed, not eating, tail pointing down, that's not the molt either. That's a sick hen. You pick her up, you bring her somewhere quiet and warm, and you look her over from beak to vent. We'll do a whole week on that one, because it deserves its own.
And please, don't pick up a molting hen and squeeze her. Those pin feathers are tender. They have blood in them. Handle her gently, or just leave her be. A molt can take eight weeks. Some hens take twelve, even sixteen. My old hens take the longest.
Some hens do what we call a hard molt. They drop nearly everything in a couple of weeks and look like they've been through a wringer. Others do a soft molt, a few feathers at a time, and you hardly notice. Both are normal. A hard molt usually means she'll grow back faster.
Number three. This one cost me money. Look at your feed bag. Turn it around and read the tag.
Feathers are almost all protein. About eighty-five percent. So when twelve hens are growing new feathers all at the same time, they need more protein than they did in July.
Most layer feed is sixteen percent protein. That's fine for a laying hen in summer. But in the molt, I switch to an all-flock feed. Eighteen or twenty percent. Some stores call it a feather fixer.
At my feed store, a fifty pound bag of layer is about seventeen dollars right now. The twenty percent all-flock was twenty-four dollars. That was the twenty-four dollars. That was the whole fix for the protein.
Don't switch the feed all in one day, though. Hens don't like surprises. I mix the new feed in with the old, a little more each day, over about a week. By the end of the week they're on the all-flock and nobody's turned up her beak at it.
One thing you have to remember. All-flock feed doesn't have the calcium a laying hen needs. So I put out oyster shell on the side, in its own little dish. The hens take what they need. They're smarter about that than we are.
And here's the one that hurt my pride. Scratch grains. I love throwing scratch. The hens come running, it's the best part of my morning. But scratch is mostly corn. It's low in protein. It's candy, honey.
Every handful of scratch is a handful of feed they didn't eat. So in the molt, the scratch goes way down. A little in the afternoon, that's all. If you want to treat them, a small handful of dried mealworms is all protein. They go crazy for them.
Number four is free too. Find out if the eggs are really gone. Because sometimes the hens are laying just fine, and you're just not getting the eggs.
Two things to look for. First, egg eating. Look in the nest boxes for wet shavings, a little yolk, bits of shell. Look at your hens' beaks. A hen with yolk on her face is a hen who's found out eggs taste good.
And second, hidden nests. A hen can decide one day that the nest box isn't good enough for her anymore. Under a bush. Behind the mower. Back in the barn. She'll lay there every day, as happy as can be, and you'll never know it.
I checked every nest box that week. No yolk, no shells, no hen with egg on her face. So I crossed it off my list. And that was a mistake, because I only checked the nest boxes. I didn't check the barn. Remember that.
Number five. This one you do at night with a flashlight. Mites.
Red mites are sneaky. They hide in the cracks of the roost bars in the daytime, and they come out at night to feed on your hens while they sleep. So in the day, you won't see a thing.
Here's a test anybody can do. After dark, take a plain white paper towel and wipe it hard along the underside of the roost. Then look at it with the flashlight. If you see little red or brown smears, that's blood. That's mites.
There's another kind that lives right on the bird. Northern fowl mites. Part the feathers under her tail, around the vent. If you see dirty gray clumps on the feathers, or little specks moving on the skin, you've got them.
A hen with mites gets pale. Her comb goes from bright red to pink, almost white. She stops laying because she's losing blood every night. That's a real problem, and it needs treating. A clean coop, fresh bedding, and a poultry dust or spray made for chickens. Read the label, every word.
When I've had mites, I don't just dust the hens. I take every roost bar out, scrub it, and let it dry in the sun. I throw out all the bedding and put down fresh. Then I treat again a week later, because the first treatment doesn't kill the eggs, and the eggs hatch in about a week. If you only do it once, you'll have them right back.
I did the paper towel test on a Wednesday night. Clean. White as a sheet. Thank the Lord. I checked under the tails too. Nothing.
Number six. Stress. And in the fall, stress usually means something is visiting your coop at night.
A raccoon pulling at the wire. A fox walking the fence line. Even a neighbor's dog. Nothing has to get in. Just a bad night of something trying, and your hens can quit laying for a week or more.
So look for the signs in the morning. Tracks in the mud by the door. Dirt dug out along the bottom of the run. A few feathers outside the fence. Hens that won't come out, or all bunched up in one corner.
I found raccoon tracks by my water barrel that week. Little hands, like a baby's. Nothing got in. My latch is a two-step latch. A raccoon can open a hook, honey, they're clever with those hands. But two steps, they can't figure. Still, those hens had a bad night, and I'm sure it didn't help.
And number seven. The one nobody wants to hear. How old is your hen?
A hen lays the most in her first two years. After that, every year she lays a little less. By five or six, a lot of hens lay a handful of eggs in the spring and not much else.

Now, with eggs you've found in a hidden nest, you don't know how old they are. So you do the water test. Fill a bowl with cold water and lay the egg in it gently.
If it sinks and lies flat on the bottom, it's fresh. If it sinks but stands up on its end, it's older. Still fine to eat, but use it soon, or hard boil it. And if it floats, honey, it goes on the compost, and you don't crack it open in the kitchen.
