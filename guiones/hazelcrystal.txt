Here's what that means. From about the time of the Centennial in eighteen seventy-six, up until the First World War, American factories made the finest cut glass in the world. Places like Corning in New York, Toledo in Ohio, and little towns in Pennsylvania.
Every piece was cut by hand. A man held a heavy blank of lead glass against a spinning stone wheel, and he cut every one of those stars and fans and diamonds into it, one at a time. A big bowl could take days. Those pieces were wedding gifts for well-to-do families.
And then, of course, somebody figured out how to make it cheap. Pressed glass. They poured hot glass into a mold that already had the pattern in it. It looked fancy, it sold for pennies at the five and dime, and every grandmother in America had some. That's what most of us have. And that's why you need to know the difference.
Test number one. Pick it up. Real brilliant cut glass is made from heavy lead glass, and it's thick, because they had to cut deep into it. When you lift it, it's heavier than it looks. Much heavier. Your arm will tell you before your eyes do.
Pressed glass feels lighter for its size. Not always, some of it is thick too. But if a big fancy bowl feels light in your hands, that's the first strike against it.
Test number two. Your thumb. Run your thumb gently along the edges of the pattern. On hand cut glass, the edges are crisp and sharp. Sharp enough to catch your skin. That's where I got bit on June's bowl.
On pressed glass, the edges are softer and rounded, because the glass flowed into the mold and cooled. It feels smooth, almost slippery. And look for a seam, a faint line running up the side where the two halves of the mold came together. A seam means pressed. Hand cut glass doesn't have one.
Test number three. Your ear. Hold the bowl from underneath, on your fingertips, and give the rim a gentle flick with your fingernail. Lead crystal rings. A long, clear ring, like a little bell, that hangs in the air. Plain glass just goes clunk. A short, dull note.
Now, be careful with this one. Don't do it on a piece that has a crack. A crack will kill the ring, and a hard flick can make it worse. And if the piece doesn't ring at all, look for a crack. A cracked piece loses most of its value.
Test number four. The window. And this is the one most people skip. A lot of the best factories signed their glass with a little acid stamp. It's not cut, it's etched, very faint, and you almost can't see it unless the light hits it just right.
So you take it to a window, or under a lamp, and you tilt it slowly, looking at the center of the bowl inside, and near the rim. You're looking for a name in script, or a little logo. Libbey. Hawkes. Clark. Hoare. Those are a few of the good ones.
On June's bowl, the word was Libbey, in that little script, right in the center. You could only see it at one angle. Most folks never would have found it.
Now, a signature is wonderful, but don't let it fool you either way. Plenty of fine pieces were never signed at all. And yes, there are fake signatures out there, etched on later. So a name helps, but the glass itself has to pass the other tests too.
Test number five. Look at the pattern up close. Brilliant cut glass has deep cuts that come to a sharp point, and the pattern is busy. Stars with lots of points, little fans, crosshatched diamonds, all fitted together. Collectors call the big stars hobstars. Look at the center of the stars. On cut glass they're cut sharp. On pressed glass the stars often have a flat, rounded center, and the whole pattern looks a little blurry around the edges, like a copy of a copy.
Test number six. Turn it over. The bottom of a good cut glass piece usually has a cut star or a pattern on it too, and it's polished. Pressed glass often has a rough little ring, or a mold mark, or a bottom that's just plain and cloudy.
So, heavy, sharp, rings like a bell, maybe a signature, deep busy pattern, and a cut bottom. If your piece passes most of those, set it aside. Don't put a sticker on it. Not yet.
Now let me tell you what isn't worth much, because I want you to trust me on the good stuff. Pressed glass imitations. I see them at every sale. Most of those bowls sell for five, ten, maybe twenty dollars. They're pretty, and they're fine to use, but they're not a fortune.
Department store crystal from the seventies and eighties. Those sets of stemware with the gold rim, the wedding gift sets in the box. People think they're valuable because they cost a lot back then. Today a lot of those glasses sell for a few dollars apiece, if they sell at all.
And damaged cut glass. A chip on the rim, a crack, or what we call sick glass, where it's gone cloudy inside and won't wash clear. Any of those can take most of the value away. A chipped bowl that would bring three hundred might bring fifty.
Now, in fairness to pressed glass, a little of it is collectible too. Some of the early American pressed glass from the eighteen hundreds, and certain patterns people collect, can bring good money. So pressed doesn't always mean worthless. It just means it's a different market, and a much smaller price for most pieces.
And one more thing people get wrong. Colored glass from the Depression years, the pink and green dishes, that's a whole different story, and I'll do a week on that one. Today we're talking about the clear, heavy, sparkly cut glass.

Now, where do you sell it? Not at your own garage sale with a sticker on it, that's for sure. For a good piece, the best place is usually a regional auction house that holds glass or decorative arts sales. They know the collectors, and the collectors know them. Expect them to take a commission, often somewhere around a quarter of the price, so ask before you sign anything.
There are also cut glass collectors' groups, with shows and member sales. Those folks know exactly what they're looking at, and they'll pay for quality.
And if you want to know what yours might bring, look up sold listings online. Not the asking prices, the sold ones. Search the maker or the pattern, and see what pieces like yours actually sold for. That's the real number.
If you try to sell it yourself, photograph it on a plain black cloth in daylight, so the cuts show up. And always show the signature and any flaws. Collectors will find a chip anyway, and they'll trust you more if you show it first.
And please, while it's still in your house, take care of it. Never put cut glass in the dishwasher. The heat and the soap can cloud it and crack it. Wash it by hand in warm water, not hot, with a towel in the bottom of the sink so it can't bang against anything. And never pour hot water into cold crystal, or cold into hot. A sudden change in temperature can crack a hundred-year-old bowl right down the middle.
