Let's start with the pot. On the boat we had a big old aluminum pot with a basket that sat down inside it. That basket is the secret to doing this without burning yourself. You lift the basket out and everything comes up with it, and the water stays in the pot. If you don't have one, any big stock pot will do, and you scoop everything out with a big strainer.
For a crowd, you want something that holds at least a few gallons of water. And you'll want an outdoor propane burner, the kind people use for frying turkeys. A kitchen stove takes forever to bring that much water to a boil. Set it up outside on flat ground, away from the house, away from the kids. A pot that size full of boiling water is no joke.
Now, the shrimp. This is the part that matters most, and you already know what I'm going to say. Get shrimp with one ingredient on the bag. Shrimp. Not soaked, not treated. And if you can, get them with the heads on and the shells on.
I know, some folks get squeamish about the heads. But here's why we leave them on. The head holds a lot of the flavor, and the shell protects the meat while it cooks and soaks. A peeled shrimp in a boil turns tough and dry before it ever gets any flavor in it. A shell-on shrimp stays juicy, and the seasoning gets in between the shell and the meat. That's the whole game.
About the size, because the bag will have numbers on it. Those numbers tell you how many shrimp make a pound. A bag that says sixteen twenty means sixteen to twenty shrimp in a pound. Figure about a pound of shrimp per person if that's the main thing you're eating, a little less if you've got plenty of potatoes and corn. Big shrimp work best. Little ones overcook too fast.
Now the rest of the pot. Small red potatoes, whole. Corn on the cob, broken in halves. Smoked sausage, cut in chunks about as long as your thumb. A couple of onions cut in half. Two or three whole heads of garlic, cut across the middle so the cloves show. And lemons. Lots of lemons, cut in half.
Then the seasoning. You can buy a crab boil seasoning in the bag or the bottle at most grocery stores down here, and plenty of folks use that, and it's fine. But here's what we used on the boat, and you can put it together from your own kitchen.
Salt, and a lot more than you think. Cayenne pepper. Black pepper. Paprika. A handful of bay leaves. Some mustard seed and coriander seed if you have them. And a few dried red chili peppers if you like it hot. Here's how you know you've got enough salt. Taste the water before anything goes in. It should taste like the Gulf. Salty enough that you notice it. If it tastes like soup, you need more. The potatoes and the shrimp are going to drink that water, and if the water's bland, everything's bland.
Squeeze those lemons into the water and then throw the rinds in too. Drop in the onions and the garlic. Put the lid on, light the burner, and bring it to a hard rolling boil. Let that seasoning boil for about ten minutes before any food goes in, so the water really takes on the flavor. You'll smell it from across the yard. The neighbors will start finding reasons to walk by.
Now here's the order, and this is where a lot of people mess up. Everything takes a different amount of time, so it goes in the pot in steps. Hardest stuff first, shrimp last.
First, the potatoes. They take the longest. Drop them in when the water's boiling hard, and cook them about fifteen minutes, until a fork goes in with just a little push.
Second, the sausage and the corn. They go in with about five to seven minutes left on the potatoes. The sausage gives up its smoke and fat into the water, and that flavors everything else.
And last, the shrimp. And this is where you have to pay attention, because shrimp cook fast. Two to three minutes. That's it. Drop them in, stir them so they're all under the water, and watch them. When they turn pink and the shells start to pull away from the meat just a little, they're done. Not one minute more.
Now. Here's the step the restaurant skipped. The one that makes all the difference. The minute the shrimp turn pink, turn off the burner. Don't lift them out yet.
Dump a big bag of ice right into the pot. Or a couple buckets of cold water. That stops the cooking right away, so the shrimp don't turn tough. And then you let everything sit in that seasoned water. Ten minutes, fifteen, even twenty. We call it the soak.
During the soak, the shrimp sink to the bottom, and they drink that water. The salt, the pepper, the lemon, the garlic, the sausage smoke, all of it goes right through the shell and into the meat. That's why when you peel one of ours, the flavor's inside. Every bite.
And here's how you know it's ready. Pull out one shrimp and taste it. Not the shell, the meat. If it tastes like the pot, it's done. If it still tastes plain, give it five more minutes. And when most of the shrimp have sunk to the bottom, that's another good sign they've soaked enough.
You can see now why the restaurant kind tastes like nothing. They cook the shrimp plain in hot water, scoop them out right away, and pour sauce on top. The sauce tastes good on your fingers, but the shrimp never got a chance to drink anything. And here's a little history, because people ask me about the butter sauce. That style, with the shrimp in a bag and the garlic butter poured on top, came from Vietnamese American families who came to work the Gulf Coast. Some of the best shrimpers I ever knew were Vietnamese men who came here with nothing and outworked everybody on the water. When their butter sauce is done right, on good shrimp that have soaked in a seasoned pot first, it is delicious. The problem isn't the sauce. The problem is when somebody skips the soak and uses the sauce to cover up shrimp that never had any flavor of their own.
Now, a few things I've learned over the years that'll make yours better.
Don't use tiny shrimp. They're done before the water even comes back to a boil, and they turn to rubber.
Don't stir too hard once the potatoes are soft, or you'll have mashed potato soup in your pot. Lift the basket gently, and let things settle.
Don't overcrowd the pot. If you've got a big crowd, do it in two batches. Too much cold food at once and the water stops boiling, and everything gets soggy.
Don't skip the ice. If you're nervous about the shrimp overcooking, the ice is your insurance. A little too soft is better than tough as a boot.
Season the water again for the second batch. Every batch drinks some of that salt and pepper out of the water. Taste it again before you start over, and add more if it went flat.
And keep it outside. All that steam and spice in a kitchen will make your eyes water for a week, and your house will smell like a seafood shed. Which, to be honest, I don't mind, but my wife sure did.
Folks always ask me what else they can throw in the pot. Here's my list. Whole mushrooms soak up that water like a sponge and they're wonderful. Brussels sprouts, believe it or not, are real good. Whole artichokes if you can find them, but they need to go in with the potatoes because they take a while. Some people throw in boiled eggs, and the kids fight over them. What I don't put in is anything that falls apart, like green beans or peeled carrots. They turn to mush and make the water cloudy. And never, ever put frozen shrimp straight from the freezer into the pot in a big frozen block. Thaw them first, in the refrigerator overnight or under cold water, or the outside cooks while the middle is still ice, and the water stops boiling.
And to drink? Down here it's sweet tea for the kids and the grandmas, and a cold one for whoever's standing over the burner. My job was always the burner. My wife's job was telling me when to drop the shrimp, because she said I always waited too long. She was usually right.
Now, a couple of words about safety, because I've seen people get this wrong at parties. Don't leave a pile of boiled shrimp sitting out in the hot sun all afternoon. Eat it while it's warm, and anything that's left, get it into the refrigerator within a couple of hours. On a really hot day, sooner.
