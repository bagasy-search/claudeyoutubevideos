A lady came down to my dock last spring with a grocery receipt in her hand. She'd bought two pounds of shrimp at the store the day before, sixteen dollars a pound. And she'd heard a fella at church say you could buy them right off the boat in Biloxi for five. She held that receipt up and said, is that true? Are y'all getting rich off me?
I had to laugh, because nobody on my dock is getting rich. But she asked a fair question. Where does the other eleven dollars go? Who gets it? And is there anything she could do to pay less and get better shrimp?
So I sat her down on an ice chest and walked her through it. Every hand that shrimp passes through between my net and her kitchen. And by the end she said, Earl, I'm never going to look at that price tag the same way again.
So today I'm going to walk you through the same thing. Where your sixteen dollars really goes, step by step. Who's honest, who isn't, and what you can do about it. And I'll tell you how to buy shrimp closer to the dock price, even if you live a thousand miles from the water.
My name's Earl. I ran a shrimp boat out of Biloxi, Mississippi for thirty one years, and I sold off my own dock for a good part of that. I've been the fella getting paid five dollars, and I've watched the same shrimp go out the door to people who'd charge three times that. So I know both ends of this.
Let's start where the shrimp starts. On the boat.
When you hear five dollars a pound at the dock, you've got to understand what that price is. That's what a boat gets paid for whole shrimp, heads on, straight out of the hold, iced down. Size matters a lot. Big shrimp bring more, small shrimp bring less. And some years it's five, some years it's four, some years it's seven. It moves with the season and with how many boats are catching.
Now think about what it took to get that pound of shrimp to the dock. Fuel. A shrimp boat burns a lot of diesel, and on a bad night you can burn more money than you catch. Ice. Nets that tear and have to be mended. The crew, who get paid a share of the catch. Insurance. Repairs on an engine that always seems to break at the worst time. When the fuel price goes up and the shrimp price doesn't, a lot of boats just stay tied up at the dock. I've seen good men sell their boats because the numbers stopped working.
So that five dollars isn't profit. Most of it is already spent before the shrimp ever hits the ice. When people say the shrimper gets the smallest slice, they're usually right.
Now here's the first thing that happens after the dock. The heads come off.
Most shrimp you buy at a store don't have heads. And the head is a big part of the weight. Roughly a third of a whole shrimp, sometimes more, is head. So a pound of whole shrimp at the dock is not a pound of tails at the store. It's more like two thirds of a pound.
Do that math on the price. If whole shrimp are five dollars a pound, the tails from that pound are really costing somewhere around seven and a half dollars a pound, before anybody's added a penny. That's not anybody cheating you. That's just what happens when you take the heads off.
And somebody has to take them off. That's a processing plant. People standing at long steel tables, heading shrimp by hand, or running them through machines. Then they get sorted by size, washed, graded, and most of the time frozen. Peeled shrimp lose even more weight, and somebody has to be paid to peel them. Peeled and deveined shrimp cost a lot more for a good reason.
Then the freezing. Most shrimp in this country are sold frozen, and honestly, that's not a bad thing. Shrimp frozen hard right on the boat or at the plant, and kept frozen, can be better than so called fresh shrimp that's been sitting on ice for days. But freezing costs money. Big freezers, electricity, boxes, bags, labels.
Then comes the trucking. Shrimp from my dock might end up in a warehouse in another state, then on another truck to a distribution center, then on another truck to the store. Every one of those trucks has to stay cold the whole way. That's fuel, drivers, refrigeration, and a warehouse somewhere charging rent to keep it frozen.
Then the wholesaler, the middleman. The fella who buys from plants, sells to stores and restaurants, and makes sure there's shrimp on the shelf in Ohio in February. People love to hate the middleman, but somebody's got to do that job. He takes a margin, usually a fair one.
And then the store. The store has its own costs. The freezer case, the electricity, the people working the seafood counter, the shrimp that don't sell and get thrown out. And the store takes its margin too. Seafood is one of the places a lot of stores make good money.
So let me put it all together the way I did for that lady on the ice chest. Five dollars for whole shrimp at the dock. Take the heads off and you're near seven fifty. Add the plant, the peeling, the freezing, the packing. Add the trucks and the warehouses. Add the wholesaler. Add the store. And by the time it's in a bag in the freezer case, sixteen dollars isn't crazy. A lot of people in that chain are getting paid a little bit, and the shrimper is the one with the smallest share.
But here's where I'm going to be honest with you, because that's not the whole story.
Not every bag of shrimp at sixteen dollars started out at my dock. And this is where you can get taken.
A lot of the shrimp sold in this country comes from overseas. Most of it, in fact. A lot of it is farmed in big ponds and shipped here frozen. There's nothing wrong with imported shrimp by itself. Some of it is good. Some of it isn't. But imported farmed shrimp usually cost the store a lot less than wild Gulf shrimp. And sometimes it's sold at nearly the same price, with a picture of a shrimp boat on the bag, and the customer thinks they're buying something they're not.
So the first thing I told that lady was, read the bag. By law, the package has to say where the shrimp came from and whether it's farm raised or wild caught. It's usually in small print on the back. Look for the country of origin. Look for the words wild caught or farm raised. If it says product of the USA and wild caught, you're paying for Gulf or Atlantic shrimp. If it says product of some other country and farm raised, you're paying wild prices for farmed shrimp, and that's where some of your eleven dollars is going.
Second thing on the bag. Look for the word ingredients. Shrimp should have one ingredient. Shrimp. Sometimes salt. If you see a list of chemicals, some of them are there to make the shrimp hold water. Water you pay for by the pound. I made a whole video about that soapy taste, and it's the same trick. Watery shrimp shrink in the pan and you just paid shrimp prices for water.
Third thing. Look at the count on the bag, not the words. Jumbo, colossal and extra large don't mean anything. There's no rule for those words. The count, like sixteen twenty or twenty six thirty, tells you how many shrimp in a pound. Compare the price per pound for the same count. A cheap bag of small shrimp isn't a bargain if you wanted big ones.
And restaurants. Don't get me started. A plate of fried shrimp might have a dozen shrimp on it, and you might pay twenty some dollars. But you're paying for the cook, the oil, the rent, the waiter, the air conditioning. That's fine. What bothers me is a menu that says Gulf shrimp and serves something that never saw the Gulf. It happens. If you care, ask them where the shrimp came from. A good restaurant will tell you. A restaurant that gets nervous when you ask has told you something too.
Now, let me tell you how to pay less and get better shrimp. Because there's a lot you can do.
Number one. If you live anywhere near the coast, buy from the dock or a seafood market that buys from boats. In Biloxi, in Louisiana, in Texas, along the Carolinas, there are docks and little markets where you can buy shrimp the day they came in. Call ahead and ask when the boats come in. Ask what size they've got. Buy heads on and take the heads off yourself. It takes a few minutes, and you can use those heads for a stock that'll make the best gumbo you ever had.
Number two. Buy in bulk, at the right time. Shrimp season in the Gulf has a rhythm. When the season opens and the boats are catching a lot, the price at the dock drops. That's when you buy ten or twenty pounds, take them home, and freeze them right. I'll show you how in a minute. Some families on the coast buy all their shrimp for the year in a couple of weekends.
Number three. If you live inland, look for shrimp that are frozen at sea or individually frozen, from the USA, wild caught. A lot of fishing families now sell direct, and they'll ship shrimp to you frozen in a cooler with dry ice. It costs more than the dock, because of the shipping, but often less than the store, and you know exactly where it came from.
Number four. Don't buy the thawed shrimp in the seafood case unless you need them today. Most of that shrimp came in frozen, and the store thawed it and put it on ice. It's been thawed for who knows how long. Buy the same shrimp still frozen in the bag, take it home, and thaw it yourself when you're ready. You'll pay the same or less, and it'll be fresher.
Number five. Look at the whole price, not just the per pound price. Heads on shrimp look cheaper, but a third of what you're paying for is head. Peeled and deveined look expensive, but there's no waste. Do the math. If you've got the time, heads on is almost always the better deal. If you don't, buying peeled might be worth it to you.
Number six. Learn to read a bag of frozen shrimp with your hands and your eyes. Pick it up and feel it. The shrimp should move around loose inside the bag, like marbles. If it's one solid brick, it probably thawed at some point and froze again, and that's bad for the shrimp. Look through the plastic. A little frost is normal. A lot of ice crystals and snowy white shrimp means it's been sitting in the freezer too long, or warming up and freezing again. White, dry looking spots on the shrimp are freezer burn. Put that bag back and grab another one.
And watch out for the sign that says fresh. People pay more for fresh, but on a seafood counter that word doesn't always mean what you think. Unless you live near the water and the store tells you exactly when those shrimp came off the boat, fresh usually just means thawed. Some stores are honest and put a little card that says previously frozen. Those are the good ones. If you ask the person behind the counter when the shrimp came in and whether they were frozen, and they don't know, that tells you something.
Now let me tell you how to freeze shrimp if you buy a lot at once. This is how we did it on the coast.
Take the heads off but leave the shells on. The shell protects the meat from the freezer. Rinse them in cold water. Put them in a freezer bag or a plastic container, a meal's worth in each one. Then fill the bag with cold water until the shrimp are covered, squeeze out the air, and freeze it flat. The ice protects the shrimp from freezer burn. When you want them, put the bag in a bowl of cold water in the sink, and in about half an hour you've got shrimp that taste like the day they came off the boat.
And keep your freezer cold. Zero degrees or colder. Eat them within a few months. They'll keep longer than that and be safe, but they won't taste as good.
