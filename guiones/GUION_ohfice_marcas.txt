So let us start with what an ice dam is, because once you see how it works, the fix makes perfect sense.

Picture your roof covered in snow. Under that snow are the shingles, and under the shingles is the roof deck, the wood. And under the wood, in most of the roof, is your attic.

Now, if warm air from your house is getting up into the attic, and in an old house it almost always is, that attic gets warm. Warmer than outside. And that warm attic heats the roof deck from underneath. So the snow on the roof melts from the bottom. Not from the sun. From your house.

That melted water runs down the roof, under the snow, toward the edge. And at the edge, the roof sticks out past the wall of the house. That part, the overhang, the eave, has no warm attic under it. It is cold. As cold as outside. So when that water reaches the cold edge, it freezes.

It freezes a little more every day. A ridge of ice builds up along the edge. That is the dam. And now the water still melting up the roof runs down, hits the dam, and stops. It pools behind it.

Shingles are made to shed water running downhill. They are not made to hold back a puddle. That pooled water backs up under the shingles, finds the nail holes and the seams, and runs into the roof deck, the wall below, the insulation, and your ceiling. That is the brown stain.

And the icicles? The icicles are the overflow. Water that made it over the dam and froze on the way down. Big icicles along the eaves are one of the clearest signs that your attic is losing heat, and that ice is building up behind them.

Now. What do you do today, with water coming in?

First, inside. Put a bucket and some towels under the drip. Move anything you care about. Take pictures. Of the stain, the ceiling, the outside of the house. Today, with the date. You may need them later.

Second, outside. The goal today is simple. Get the snow off the lower part of the roof, the first few feet up from the edge. Less snow there means less water feeding the dam, and less weight.

And you do this from the ground. With a roof rake. That is a long pole with a wide, flat blade on the end, made for exactly this. You stand on the ground, a few feet back from the house, reach up, and pull the snow down off the edge of the roof. You do not need to clear the whole roof. The first three or four feet is what matters. Pull gently, and do not scrape all the way down to the shingles hard, because you can tear them in the cold, when they are brittle.

Two safety rules with a roof rake. Look up first for power lines, and if there is a wire anywhere near where that pole will go, stop. And step back as you pull, because a slab of snow and ice can come down all at once, and it is heavier than you think. Watch the icicles too. A big one falling from two stories can hurt you badly.

Third, make a channel through the dam, so the trapped water can drain. The common way to do this from the ground is with a calcium chloride ice melt, the kind made for roofs and sidewalks. Not regular rock salt, which can damage the roof and the plants underneath. You fill a long nylon stocking with the calcium chloride, tie it off, and lay it on the roof so it runs across the dam, from above it down over the edge. You can push it up there with the roof rake. Over a few hours, it melts a channel through the ice, and the water behind the dam can drain out through that channel instead of backing up under the shingles.

Here is why that is the worst idea. First, the ladder. A ladder set on frozen ground, leaning on an icy gutter, with you swinging a hammer at the top of it, is one of the most dangerous things you can do around a house in winter. People fall doing this every year.

Second, the roof. That ice is frozen right onto the shingles. When you chop it, you chop the shingles. You crack them, you tear them, you punch holes in them. In January, they are brittle. So you turn an ice dam leak into a roof leak that is still there in July.

And third, it does not fix anything. The dam comes back with the next snowfall, because nothing about the attic changed.

So no chopping. No ladder on the ice. No climbing on a snowy roof. No heat gun, no torch, no open flame anywhere near a roof. And if the dam is big and the leak is bad, there are roofers who remove ice dams with low pressure steam, which melts the ice without tearing the shingles. That is the right call for a bad one, not a hammer.

And here is something you can do on the very next snowy morning, before any stain shows up. Read your roof.

A day or two after a snowfall, stand across the street with a cup of coffee and look at your roof next to your neighbors' roofs. A cold, well sealed attic keeps an even blanket of snow on the roof. If your roof has bare patches, or stripes where the snow melted, while the house next door is still white, those patches are right over the places where heat is pouring into your attic. Over the attic hatch. Over a bathroom. Over the kitchen ceiling lights. The snow is drawing you a map.

Then, on a really cold day, go up into the attic with a flashlight, if you can do it safely, stepping only on the joists. Look at the underside of the roof boards. If you see frost on them, or frost on the tips of the roofing nails poking through, that is warm, wet air from the house hitting the cold wood and freezing. In the spring, that frost melts and drips, and that is how old attics end up with dark stained boards and mold. Frost up there is the same problem as the ice dam, just showing up in a different place.

Now. The real fix. The one that keeps this from ever happening again.

Remember where it starts. Warm air from the house getting into the attic. So the real fix is in the attic, and most of it is something you can do when the weather is mild, or have someone do for you.

One. Stop the warm air going up. This matters more than anything else. In an old house, warm air leaks into the attic through every hole in the ceiling. Around the attic hatch or the pull down stairs. Around ceiling light fixtures. Around plumbing vent pipes and the chimney. Along the tops of interior walls. And from bathroom fans that were never ducted outside and just blow warm, wet air into the attic. Every one of those is a little chimney of warm air heating your roof. Seal them. Weather strip the hatch. Seal around pipes and wires with caulk or foam. Around the chimney, it has to be fire rated material, so that one is for someone who knows the rules. And make sure every bath fan goes all the way outside.

Two. Insulation. Once the leaks are sealed, enough insulation on the attic floor keeps the heat in the house, where you are paying for it to be. Many old houses have a few inches of old, matted insulation up there, or none at all in spots. Adding more is one of the best things you can do for an old house, for the heating bill and for the roof.

Three. Ventilation. A healthy attic in a cold climate is cold in winter, close to the outside temperature. Air comes in low, at the soffits under the eaves, and goes out high, at the ridge or gable vents. That flow keeps the roof deck cold, so the snow does not melt from below. If the soffit vents are blocked with old insulation or painted over, the attic stays warm. Baffles at the eaves keep the insulation from blocking that air path.

Do those three, and on most houses the ice dams get much smaller, or stop.

And two more things for the long run. When the roof is replaced someday, ask about a waterproof membrane along the eaves under the shingles, often called ice and water shield. In cold places, building codes often require it now, and it is a big reason newer roofs leak less from ice dams. And roof heat cables, the zigzag ones along the edge, can help in a problem spot, but they treat the symptom, not the cause, and they cost money to run. Fix the attic first.

And the best time to do the attic work is the fall, before the first real snow, on a mild dry day. An attic in August is too hot to work in, and an attic in January is too cold and the damage is already happening. October is perfect.
