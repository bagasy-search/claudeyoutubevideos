It was the last hour of the last day of an estate sale in a little town outside Columbus. Everything was half price, the tables were picked over, and the family was tired. In the back of a hall closet, on the top shelf, behind a stack of old hat boxes, there was a small wooden box with a brass keyhole and no key.
The man running the sale said it was full of old junk jewelry and I could have the whole thing for twenty dollars. I almost said no. My feet hurt and I wanted to go home. But something about that box made me hand him the twenty.
When I got it home and pried the lid open at my kitchen table, I sat there for a long time. Because what was inside that box was not junk. Some of it was worth real money. And one thing in there was worth more to one family than any price you could put on it.
So today I'm going to open that box with you, piece by piece. I'll show you how I figured out what each thing was, the simple tests anybody can do at home, and what pieces like these really sell for. And I'll tell you about the one thing in there I could never sell.
I'm Hazel. I ran estate sales in Ohio for forty years. I've opened a lot of old jewelry boxes. This one I'll never forget.
First, the box itself. It was walnut, about the size of a loaf of bread, with a little brass escutcheon around the keyhole and a velvet lining that had faded from red to almost pink. The hinges were hand cut, and the bottom had a paper label from a jeweler in Cincinnati. Boxes like that were made in the eighteen hundreds, and even empty, a nice one can sell for fifty to a couple hundred dollars to people who collect them. So my twenty dollars was already safe before I looked inside.
Now, the inside. There was a top tray with little compartments, and under it a deeper space with a tangle of chains and brooches and things wrapped in yellowed tissue paper.
The first thing I picked up was a brooch. Black, oval, with a little window in the middle. And behind the glass, there was a lock of brown hair woven into a tiny pattern, like basket weaving.
If you've never seen one, that's called a mourning brooch, or hair jewelry. In the eighteen hundreds, when someone in the family died, people often kept a lock of their hair and had it worked into a brooch or a ring, so they could carry that person with them. It sounds strange to us now. But it was very common, especially after the Civil War.
On the back of this one, engraved in tiny script, it said, In memory of our dear Samuel, eighteen sixty four.
Now, what is something like that worth? Mourning jewelry has a real collector market. A simple hair brooch in gold or gold filled might sell for a hundred to a few hundred dollars. One with a name, a date, and a good story can sell for more. And the black part matters. Some are made of jet, a kind of fossilized wood from England. Some are made of black glass, which was cheaper. Here's an easy test. Jet feels warm and very light in your hand. Black glass feels cold and heavier. This one was jet.
But I put that brooch aside, because I had a feeling someone might want it more than a collector.
The next thing was a cameo. Everybody's grandmother had a cameo, so let me tell you what to look for, because most of them aren't worth much, and a few are worth a lot.
A cameo is a carved picture, usually a lady's profile, raised up from a background of another color. The best old ones were carved by hand from shell or from stone. Newer ones, and a lot of cheap ones, are molded plastic or glass.
Here's the test I use. Hold the cameo up to a strong light, like a flashlight, from behind. If it's carved from shell, the light usually glows through the thin parts, pinkish or orange. If it's stone, like agate, light won't come through much at all, and it'll feel cold and heavy. If it's plastic, it'll feel light and warm, and if you look closely with a magnifying glass, you'll often see a seam from the mold or little air bubbles. Hand carved cameos have tiny tool marks and fine details in the hair and the face.
This one glowed a soft pink when I held my flashlight behind it. Shell, hand carved. The lady had little curls in her hair you could only see with a loupe. And the frame was stamped on the back with a tiny fourteen K.
A good hand carved shell cameo in a gold frame can sell for a hundred to a few hundred dollars. A stone cameo by a known carver can sell for much more. A molded plastic one, maybe ten or twenty dollars. So turn every cameo over and put a light behind it before you sell the box.
Now let me talk about the gold, because this is where families lose the most money.
In that tangle of chains, some of it looked like gold, and some of it said gold on it. But not all gold is gold.
Here's what the stamps mean. If you see ten K, fourteen K or eighteen K, or the numbers four one seven, five eight five or seven five zero, that's solid gold, and it's worth at least its weight in gold, which right now is a lot. If you see the letters G F, that means gold filled, which is a thin layer of gold bonded over another metal. If you see R G P, that's rolled gold plate, an even thinner layer. And if you see G P or H G E, that's gold plated or gold electroplate, and there's almost no gold in it at all.
Gold filled jewelry isn't worthless. Old gold filled pieces, like watch chains and lockets, sell well to collectors because they're pretty and they last. But they're not sold by weight like solid gold. So the stamp makes a big difference.
And here's something people don't know. A lot of old jewelry from the eighteen hundreds isn't stamped at all. The laws about stamping gold in this country came later. So no stamp doesn't mean no gold. That's when you take it to a jeweler and ask them to test it. Most jewelers will do it for free or a few dollars, and it takes a minute.
In my box, there was a long watch chain stamped G F, a pretty little locket that was solid fourteen K, and a thin chain with no stamp at all. My jeweler tested that one. It was ten karat gold.
Then there was a pocket watch, wrapped in its own piece of tissue. A gold colored case, a white enamel face with black Roman numerals, and a tiny second hand at the bottom. It didn't run.
Pocket watches are a whole world, and I'm not a watch expert. But here's what I tell families. Open the back of the case carefully. Inside, there's usually a name, like Elgin or Waltham, and a serial number. With that serial number, you can look up when the watch was made and how fancy the movement is. Look at the inside of the case too. You'll often see the words fourteen K, or the words guaranteed twenty years, which means gold filled.
Most old pocket watches in gold filled cases sell for fifty to a couple hundred dollars, running or not. A solid gold case is worth at least its gold, and sometimes much more. And a few rare ones, from fancy railroad grade movements, can sell for a lot more than that. Don't try to fix it yourself, and don't let anybody pry it open with a kitchen knife. Take it to someone who works on watches.
This one was a gold filled case with an ordinary movement. A nice piece. A hundred dollars, maybe a little more.
Next, there was a little pouch of old silver spoons, small ones, with thin handles and a name engraved on each one. On the back they said the word coin, and a maker's name.
Coin silver is something a lot of people have never heard of. Before sterling silver was common in this country, silversmiths often melted down silver coins to make spoons and cups. Coin silver is a little less pure than sterling, about ninety percent silver. It's often marked with the word coin, or just the silversmith's name. Old American coin silver spoons by known makers can sell for more than their weight in silver, especially from certain towns and states. Ordinary ones sell close to the silver value.
Those spoons had a maker's mark from Ohio. That made them a little more interesting to collectors here.
Then there was a strand of tiny pearls, so small they looked like seeds. Seed pearls were very popular in the eighteen hundreds, sewn onto brooches or strung into delicate necklaces.
Here's the old test for real pearls, and jewelers still use it. Rub the pearl gently against the edge of your front teeth. A real pearl feels a little gritty, like fine sand. A fake pearl, made of glass or plastic, feels smooth and slippery. Don't bite it. Just rub it lightly.
These were gritty. Real. But seed pearls by themselves don't usually sell for a lot. The value is in the piece they're part of.
Now, here's where my heart started beating a little faster.
At the very bottom of the box, under the velvet lining, which had come loose at one corner, there was a small ring. A thin gold band, worn smooth on the inside, with one stone. The stone was cut in an old fashioned way, kind of squarish with rounded corners, and when I put it under my lamp it threw little flashes of color, like a tiny fire.
I've learned not to get excited too fast. A lot of old rings have glass stones, or white sapphires, or a clear stone called paste that was made to look like a diamond. So I did what I always do. I took it to my jeweler.
She put it under her microscope and tested it, and she looked up at me and smiled. It was a real diamond, cut the old way, by hand, in the eighteen hundreds. Jewelers call it an old mine cut. Not huge, a little under half a carat. But old cut diamonds have a real following. A lot of young people today want them for engagement rings, because each one is a little different and they have a soft, warm sparkle you don't see in modern diamonds.
She told me a ring like that, in that condition, could sell for somewhere around a thousand dollars, maybe more to the right buyer.
Twenty dollars at an estate sale.
But here's what I want you to understand. I almost missed it. It was under the lining. If I'd dumped that box into a bag and sold the whole thing to someone else for thirty dollars, they'd have gotten that ring. So when you go through your mother's or your grandmother's jewelry, lift the lining. Look in the little compartments. Check the hidden drawer some boxes have in the bottom. Old ladies hid their best things.
There was one more thing in that box. And it's the reason I'll never forget it.
Tucked in a slot in the lid, folded small, there was a letter. And a tiny photograph on a thin sheet of metal, the kind they call a tintype, of a young man in a uniform.
The letter was written in pencil, faded but still readable. It was from a soldier named Samuel to his mother, and it ended with, Tell little Mary I will bring her a ribbon when I come home.
Samuel. The same name on the mourning brooch. Eighteen sixty four.
I sat at my kitchen table and cried a little. Because I knew what happened. He never came home. His mother kept his letter and his picture in her jewelry box, and a lock of his hair in a brooch, for the rest of her life. And somehow, all of it ended up behind the hat boxes in a hall closet, a hundred and sixty years later, priced to sell for twenty dollars.
Tintypes of Civil War soldiers sell to collectors, sometimes for a few hundred dollars or more. Soldier letters do too. But I wasn't going to sell those.
I called the man who ran the estate sale and asked about the family. It took some looking. The lady who had passed was a great great niece, and her children lived in Indiana and Tennessee. They'd had no idea any of it was in that box. They'd never heard of Samuel.
I drove the letter, the tintype and the mourning brooch to the daughter's house in Indiana myself. When she read that letter at her kitchen table, she put her hand over her mouth. She said her own little girl's name was Mary.
I sold the diamond ring, the cameo, the locket and the gold chain, and I split the money with that family, because it felt right. The pocket watch went to a young man who collects them. The box sits on my dresser.
And one more thing before I give you the list. If you find a piece you think is valuable, like that diamond ring, don't sell it to the first person who makes an offer. Get it looked at by an independent appraiser, someone who charges a flat fee and doesn't buy jewelry, so they have no reason to tell you it's worth less than it is. Get two opinions on anything big. And if you decide to keep it, ask about adding it to your insurance. A lot of family jewelry sits in a drawer for years with no protection at all, and if the house burns or it gets stolen, it's just gone.
