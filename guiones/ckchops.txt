Now let's gather what we need, and I'll say each one the way the book says it. Six bone-in pork chops, one to one and a quarter inches thick. Four cups of cool water. A quarter cup of salt and two tablespoons of brown sugar, for the brine. One teaspoon of pepper, one teaspoon of paprika and half a teaspoon of garlic powder for the seasoning. Two tablespoons of oil and two tablespoons of butter. Two large onions, sliced. Two tablespoons of flour. One and a half cups of chicken broth. Half a cup of milk or cream. And one teaspoon of fresh thyme, or half a teaspoon if all you have is dried. That is the whole list. No fancy anything.
Pick chops that are at least an inch thick. A thin chop is what makes people say the pork was dry, and I have made that mistake more times than I can count. So buy the thick ones. You will want a skillet that is good and heavy, too. Cast iron is my favorite. I have had the same one on this stove since before Harold and I bought the farm, and it has never once let me down.
Step one is the brine. Stir the salt and the brown sugar into the four cups of cool water until both have melted in. Put in the chops and let them soak for thirty minutes. Never more than two hours, honey, or they get too salty. Now, why do we do this? Because the salt water goes right into the meat and holds on to the juice. When that chop hits the hot pan, the water stays put, and the middle of a thick chop stays tender all the way to the bone.
When the thirty minutes are up, lift the chops out of the water and rinse them under the cold tap. Then pat them very dry with paper towels. Dry all over. Pat them more than you think you need to. A wet chop will steam in the pan and never make a crust, and the crust is where the flavor lives. Set them on a clean plate while you get everything else ready.
Heat your oven to three hundred twenty-five degrees. While it comes up, mix the pepper, the paprika and the garlic powder in a little dish. Do not add any more salt, because that brine already seasoned the meat. Rub the spice mix into both sides of every chop, and set them back on the plate. The brine did the salting, and the spice does the flavor, so there is no need for a second pinch of salt.
Put your two tablespoons of oil in the cast-iron skillet and let it get hot, but not smoking. Lay the chops in, and let them sear about three minutes on the first side, until it turns a deep golden brown. Then turn them and do the other side, about three minutes more. Do not fuss with them. Let the pan do the work. When both sides are golden, lift the chops out and set them aside on a clean plate.
Why do we sear them before they go in the oven? Because that hot pan browns the outside and gives the chop its flavor, and the oven finishes the middle slowly and gently. If you skip the sear, you have a pale, plain chop with nothing to talk about. If you only sear and never finish in the oven, you have a chop that is still cold in the middle. The book says both steps, and the book is right, honey.
Lower the heat now, and add the two tablespoons of butter to that same skillet. Put in the two large sliced onions. Cook them about eight minutes, until they are soft and golden, and scrape the bottom of the pan with your wooden spoon while they cook. Every brown bit stuck to that bottom is flavor, honey. Do not let your pan go clean. That scraping is the whole secret, right there.
Sprinkle in the two tablespoons of flour and stir it for one full minute, so it loses that raw, pasty taste. Then pour in the chicken broth, the half cup of milk or cream, and the thyme. Stir it all together and let it simmer until it thickens a little. You will know it is ready when the gravy drips slowly off the back of the spoon, instead of running off like water.
Nestle the chops back down into the onions and the gravy, and spoon some of that gravy over the top of each one. Cover the skillet with its lid, or with foil if your lid does not sit tight. Then put it in the oven and bake fifteen to twenty minutes. We are going for one hundred forty-five degrees in the thickest part of the chop, so push your meat thermometer in sideways and let it tell you the truth. A thermometer costs less than one ruined pork chop, and it is the only sure way to know.
When the thermometer reads one hundred forty-five, take the skillet out and let those chops rest five minutes, right there in the pan. Do not cut into them the minute they come out of the oven. Let the juices settle back where they belong. Then spoon the onions and the gravy over every plate, and you have a supper that will make people go quiet for a minute. A little tip from the church basement: while the chops rest, you can make the rice or the mashed potatoes, so the gravy is still hot when the plates go out.
