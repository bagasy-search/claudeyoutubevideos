Now first, why does the oven dry it out? Because an oven heats the air, honey, and hot dry air pulls the water right out of the meat. [warmly] Every time you open the door to look, the heat runs out, and then it comes roaring back. And most people cook it too hot, so the meat squeezes up and pushes all its juice out into the pan.
A slow cooker is the opposite. The heat is low and gentle, the lid holds the steam in, and the meat sits there for eight hours in its own juice. It can't dry out, because there's nowhere for the water to go. That is why Bernice's roasts were always better than mine.
Now, the meat. [warmly] This is the most important thing I will tell you today. Buy a chuck roast. That's the shoulder. It might say chuck roast, or shoulder roast, or seven bone roast. Three or four pounds. And look for these little white lines of fat running all through it. That is what melts in the pot and makes it tender.
[emphatically] Don't buy the lean roasts. The round, the rump, the eye of round. They look nice and they cost more, but they have no fat inside, and in a slow cooker they come out dry and grainy. [chuckles] The cheap cut is the right cut, honey. The butchers always knew that.

Step one. Take the roast out of the package and pat it dry with a paper towel, all over. A wet roast won't brown. Then salt it on every side. [emphatically] About two teaspoons of salt, and a good amount of pepper. More than you think. That is a big piece of meat, and the salt has to go all the way through it.
Then roll it in a little flour, just a dusting, and shake off the extra. That flour browns in the skillet and it helps thicken the gravy later. My mother did that, and her mother did that.
Step two, and this is the one people skip. Brown it. [emphatically] Get a heavy skillet very hot with two tablespoons of oil, and lay the roast in. Don't touch it. Four or five minutes, until it is a deep dark brown, and then turn it and do the other side, and the edges too.
Now somebody always asks me, Loretta, do I really have to brown it? [chuckles] Yes, honey. That brown crust is where all the flavor comes from. If you put a gray roast in the pot, you will get gray gravy. [emphatically] Ten minutes in the skillet is the difference between a good roast and a church supper roast.
Now here is the first church trick. The vegetables go on the bottom. Onions first, then the carrots and the potatoes. The meat goes on top of them. Most people do it backwards, and they wonder why the potatoes are hard and the meat is boiled.
[warmly] The bottom of the pot is the hottest, and vegetables take longer to cook than you think. So they go down there, close to the heat. And they make a little rack, so the roast sits up on top and cooks in the steam, instead of sitting in water. Cut the carrots and potatoes big, two inches. Little pieces turn to mush in eight hours.
And now Bernice's trick. The coffee. [emphatically] Pour one cup of strong black coffee into that hot skillet you browned the meat in, and scrape up all the brown bits stuck to the bottom with a wooden spoon. That is all flavor, honey. Don't you wash that down the sink.

So pour the coffee from the skillet over the roast, and then the cup of broth and the Worcestershire. Put in the garlic and the bay leaves. And here is the second trick. [emphatically] Don't drown it. The liquid should only come about halfway up the vegetables. Not over the meat.
People think more water makes it juicier. It doesn't. The roast makes its own juice in there, a lot of it. [warmly] If you cover it with water, you are boiling it, and you get a gray roast and a watery gravy. Two cups is plenty for a four pound roast.
And the third trick. Cook it on low. Not high. Low, for eight or nine hours. On high it gets done faster, but the meat tightens up and comes out stringy. Low and slow lets all that fat and the tough parts melt. [chuckles] It is the same as people, honey. You can't rush the tough ones.
And the fourth one, and this is the hardest one. [emphatically] Don't lift the lid. Every time you lift it to look, all the steam goes out, and it takes about twenty minutes to get the heat back. Bernice used to put a dish towel over the lid at church so nobody would peek. And they still peeked.
[warmly] Now how do you know it's done? Not by the clock. Take a fork and push it into the roast, and give it a little twist. If it falls apart, it's done. If it's still tough, it is not ready yet. Put the lid back on and give it another hour. A tough pot roast just needs more time.
That surprises people. They think tough means it cooked too long. With a chuck roast, it is the other way around. [emphatically] It is tough until it gets to about two hundred degrees inside, and then all at once it goes soft. So if it is tough, wait.
