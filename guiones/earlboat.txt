I'll show you what comes up in that net that you'll never see in a grocery store. And at the end, I'll do the math on a good night, so you know exactly how much of what you pay ever gets back to the man on the boat. It's less than you think.
My name's Earl. I captained the Lady Beth out of Biloxi for thirty-one years. Today I sell what the boats bring in, right here in this shed. But tonight, we're going out.
I learned it from my daddy. He worked the boats out of Pascagoula, and I was on deck with him when I was twelve years old, picking shrimp in the summertime when the other boys were playing ball. By the time I had my own boat, I'd already been doing it for twenty years.
First, the boat. The Lady Beth was a sixty-eight foot shrimp trawler. Wood hull when I started, steel later on. You've seen boats like her tied up behind me. Those big arms sticking up on each side, those are the outriggers. When we're working, they swing out wide like wings, and each one pulls a net.
On a night like this, there were three of us. Me in the wheelhouse, and two deckhands. On the coast we call them strikers. Good strikers are worth their weight in gold. I had one man work with me for nineteen years. Never late once.
Before we leave, we take on ice. Tons of it, blown right into the hold from the ice house on the dock. We fill the fuel tanks. And we load the groceries, because you're not coming back for supper. Coffee, mostly. A lot of coffee.
And every spring, before the season, the whole town comes down to the water for the Blessing of the Fleet. The boats get washed and painted and dressed up with flags, and they parade past and a priest blesses every one of them. My wife used to make me wear a clean shirt for it. Then opening day comes, and every boat in the harbor goes out at once, like a race. You don't sleep much the first week of the season.
So, three thirty, we pull out. We run down the channel, past the islands, out into the Mississippi Sound and then the open Gulf. Depending on where the shrimp are, that run can take an hour, or it can take four.
And here's the thing nobody tells you. A lot of the job is just finding them. Shrimp move. They move with the tides, the water temperature, the rain, the moon. What was good last Tuesday might be empty tonight.
So before we drop the big nets, we drop a little one. A try net. It's a small net you can pull up quick, every fifteen, twenty minutes. You look at what's in it. If there's a handful of shrimp in the try net, you're in the right place. If it's empty, you keep moving.
When the try net tells you it's good, the strikers swing the outriggers out and drop the big nets. They sink to the bottom, and the boat pulls them along nice and slow, about walking speed. And now we wait. A tow usually runs two, three, four hours.
Now, every net on a shrimp boat in this country has two things sewn into it that most people have never heard of. The first one is a turtle excluder. It's a metal grid in the neck of the net with a flap, and when a sea turtle swims in, it hits that grid and slides right out the opening. The law requires it, and I'll tell you the truth, the old-timers grumbled about it, but it works.
The second one lets a lot of the small fish swim back out before the net comes up. Fewer fish we didn't want, and the shrimp come up cleaner. Those two things are a big reason wild Gulf shrimp are something you can feel good about eating.
While we tow, I'm in the wheelhouse watching the depth, watching the weather, watching the other boats on the radio. And the strikers grab a little sleep on the bunks, because once those nets come up, nobody sleeps.
Then it's time. We winch the nets up. The bags come swinging over the deck, heavy and dripping, and you pull the knot at the bottom, and the whole catch spills out across the deck under the lights. Shrimp, crabs, fish, everything, flopping and sliding around your boots.
And this is the part I promised you. Here's what comes up in that net. Croakers, those little silver fish that grunt when you pick them up. Blue crabs. Squid. Flounder flat on the deck. Small sharks. Jellyfish, sometimes so many the whole pile is slick with them. Stingrays, and you watch your feet. Once in a great while, something you've never seen before in your life.
Now, the shrimp go in the baskets. And most everything else goes right back over the side, as fast as we can work. Pelicans and gulls follow the boat all night just waiting for us to sort. You'll never see most of that in a store. But it's down there, and it all comes up together.
We sort by hand, bent over a table under the work lights. It's wet, it's cold even in the summer once the wind picks up, and your back tells you about it the next day. Then the shrimp get rinsed and go straight down into the hold, packed in ice. Or on the newer boats, into a freezer right on board.
I'll tell you about one night in September, years ago. We pulled the nets about two in the morning and the bag came up so heavy the winch groaned. When we dumped it, there was a hammerhead shark in there, longer than I am tall, thrashing around in the middle of all those shrimp. My striker went one way and I went the other. We got a rope on his tail and slid him back over the side, and he swam off just fine. Then we sorted the best shrimp of the whole season. That's what a good night looks like.
And there were plenty of bad nights too. Nights you burned through the fuel and came home with enough shrimp for supper. Nights the fog came in so thick you couldn't see the bow from the wheelhouse, and all you had was the radar and the radio and your own two ears.
And there were nights you didn't go out at all, because the Gulf can kill you. A storm comes up fast out there. I've seen grown men cry in a wheelhouse, and I've been one of them. After the big hurricane in two thousand five, there were shrimp boats sitting in the middle of the highway right here in Biloxi, miles from the water. Some of my friends never fished again.
And when the season's closed, the work doesn't stop. You mend the nets on the dock, knot by knot. You scrape and paint the hull. You fix the engine, again. A shrimp boat is never finished. My wife used to say the Lady Beth was the other woman in my life, and she wasn't wrong.
Then, if it's a good night, you drop the nets again. And again. Tow, pull, sort, ice. Tow, pull, sort, ice. All night long. Around five in the morning, the sky starts to turn gray over the water, and you know it's time to head in.
In between tows, somebody cooks. On the Lady Beth, that was usually me. A pot of red beans, or fried croaker right out of the net, or a pan of shrimp with butter and hot sauce at three in the morning. I'll tell you, nothing in any restaurant ever tasted as good as that shrimp out there in the dark.
By the time the sun is up, we're coming back up the channel. Pelicans on the pilings. The other boats coming in behind us. The coffee's cold, and everybody's quiet. That's my favorite part of the whole night. I still miss it.
At the dock, we unload. The shrimp come up out of the hold in baskets, and they get weighed and graded by size. And here's where you learn what the night was worth. The dock pays by the pound, and the price depends on the size and what the market's doing that week.
So let me do the math on a good night. Let's say we brought in twelve hundred pounds of shrimp, heads on. This summer, the boats around here were getting about three, four dollars a pound at the dock for medium shrimp. Let's say three and a half. That's about forty-two hundred dollars for the whole night. Sounds pretty good, doesn't it?
Now take out the fuel. A boat like the Lady Beth could burn three hundred gallons in a night. At three fifty a gallon, that's over a thousand dollars, gone. Take out the ice, a couple hundred more. Groceries, the nets you tore up on a snag, the engine that needs fixing again.
Then the crew. On most shrimp boats, the crew gets a share of the catch, not a paycheck. So my two strikers each get their share. And whatever's left goes to the boat, the payments, the insurance, and the captain. On a good night, a captain might clear a few hundred dollars. On a bad night, you lost money for the privilege of not sleeping.
And remember, that's twelve hundred pounds of shrimp with the heads on. By the time they're headed and peeled, you've got a whole lot less shrimp. Then somebody processes them, freezes them, trucks them, and the store puts them out for twelve, fourteen, sixteen dollars a pound. Out of every one of those dollars, the man who caught it got a small slice.
