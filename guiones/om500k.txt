Number six. The first five minutes of every drive.

Number five. Let your engine breathe.
Every drop of air your engine burns goes through one paper filter first. It sits in a black plastic box under the hood, about the size of a thin paperback book.

And please, don't blow out a paper filter with compressed air and reuse it. You make tiny holes you can't see, and from that day on, the dirt goes straight into your engine.

Number three. The fluids that go bad on a calendar, not on the odometer.
Most people think fluids only wear out with miles. Two of them wear out with time, even if the car sits in the garage. Brake fluid, and coolant.
Brake fluid pulls water out of the air. Little by little, through the hoses and the cap. Coolant is the colored liquid in the plastic tank under the hood. Green, orange, pink or blue, depending on your car. It carries heat away, and it keeps the inside of the engine from rusting. And it has a shelf life. Most cars, around five years.

Number two. The five dollar valve.
Now, about this little valve I showed you at the start. It's called the PCV valve. Positive crankcase ventilation. Don't worry about the name. Here's what it does.
When your engine runs, a little bit of gas and pressure always sneaks past the pistons into the bottom of the engine. The PCV valve lets that pressure out and sends it back to be burned. It's how the engine breathes from the inside.
When the valve clogs with sludge, the pressure has nowhere to go. So it pushes oil out wherever it can. The gaskets start leaking, the seals start weeping, and the engine starts using oil between changes. People think the engine is worn out. A lot of the time, it's a five dollar valve.
On many engines, it's easy to find. A small valve pushed into a rubber grommet on top of the engine, with a hose on it. Pull it out with the engine off and shake it. If it rattles, it's still moving. If it's silent, or sticky with black goo, it needs to be replaced.
Now, I'll be honest. On some newer engines, the valve is built into the engine cover, and it's not a five dollar job anymore. If you can't find it, ask your mechanic to check it at the next oil change. It takes them two minutes.
My rule is to have it checked every two or three oil changes, and replaced around every fifty thousand miles, or sooner if the engine starts using oil.
