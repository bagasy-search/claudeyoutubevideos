[S03] First, let's talk about why your chops come out dry. It's not your fault. Nobody told you.
[S03] A pork chop is a lean piece of meat. Not much fat running through it, not like a steak. When it gets hot, the muscle squeezes, like a fist closing, and the water inside gets pushed out. A little squeezing is fine. That's cooking. But the hotter the meat gets in the middle, the harder that fist closes. And when you fry a chop in an open pan, you have to keep it on the heat until the middle is done. By the time the middle gets there, the outside half inch has been cooking way past done for ten minutes. That's your gray, dry ring.
[S03] And then there's the second problem. Most folks were taught that pork has to be cooked to one-sixty, or even one-seventy. My mother cooked it to one-seventy-five and served it with gravy because she had to. That was the old advice. The USDA changed it years ago. Whole cuts of pork, chops, roasts, tenderloin, are safe at one hundred and forty-five degrees, with a three-minute rest. One-forty-five. That's fifteen to thirty degrees less than what most people cook it to. And those fifteen degrees are the difference between juicy and sawdust.
[S03] So, two things are drying out your chops. Too long in the open pan, and too hot in the middle. We fix both.
[S04] Here's what we did on the ranch. I call it brine, sear, splash, lid.
[S04] Don Ernesto taught me this. He was the cook before me, an old man when I was a young one, and he didn't own a thermometer in his life. He used to say, "The fire makes the color. The steam makes the juice." It took me about ten years to understand what he meant. Let me show you.
[S05] Step one. The brine. Four cups of water. A quarter cup of kosher salt. Two tablespoons of brown sugar. Stir it until it's dissolved. You can't see any grains on the bottom, then you're done.
[S05] If you only have table salt, use a little less. About three tablespoons. Table salt is finer, so a spoonful of it is saltier than a spoonful of kosher.
[S06] Now the chops go in. Four bone-in chops, an inch to an inch and a quarter thick. Thirty minutes. Not longer.
[S06] Why does this work? Salt water gets into the meat and loosens up those muscle fibers a little, so they don't squeeze so hard when they get hot. The meat holds on to more of its own juice. And the bit of sugar helps it brown in the pan.
[S06] But I said thirty minutes, and I mean it. People think, if thirty is good, overnight must be better. It isn't. Leave a chop in that brine all night and it comes out tasting like ham. Spongy. Too salty. Thirty minutes. Set a timer. Go feed the dog.
