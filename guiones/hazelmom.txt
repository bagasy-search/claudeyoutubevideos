First, a word about the dumpster. Rent it last, not first. I know, you're tired, you live three hours away, and you want it done. But the dumpster in the driveway is where the money goes. Go through the house first, one room at a time, and nothing leaves until somebody who knows what to look for has looked.
Number one. The button tin. Every grandmother had one. A cookie tin or a coffee can full of buttons. Most of them are worth very little, but mixed in there you'll often find Bakelite.
Bakelite buttons are heavy for their size, with rich colors, butterscotch, cherry red, dark green, sometimes carved. Here's the test. Run one under hot tap water and smell it. Old Bakelite gives off a sharp chemical smell, a little like formaldehyde. Plastic smells like nothing.
Most single Bakelite buttons sell for a few dollars to maybe twenty-five. Carved ones, big ones, unusual colors, can bring fifty to a hundred and more. A tin with a good mix of them can bring a nice little sum. Sell them as a lot online, or to button collectors. Yes, there are button clubs, and those ladies know their stuff.
Number two. The mixing bowls. Old Pyrex. The milk glass bowls with the colored patterns, nested together on the top shelf. Common patterns usually sell for ten to forty dollars a piece. A complete nesting set in a pattern collectors want can bring a few hundred. And a few rare patterns, the ones that were only made for a short time, have sold for thousands.
Here's how to tell if yours have value. Look for bright, glossy color. If the pattern is faded and dull, that bowl spent years in a dishwasher, and that takes most of the value away. Look on the bottom for the Pyrex mark. Then look up the pattern name and check the sold listings. Sell them locally or online, packed very carefully.
Number three. The recipe box and the church cookbooks. Most community cookbooks, the spiral ones the church ladies put together, sell for five to twenty dollars. But some cookbooks are worth real money, especially first editions of famous ones in good condition. Look at the copyright page. First edition, first printing, with the dust jacket, that's what collectors want.
And please, look through the recipe box itself. The handwritten cards might not sell for much, but they're your family's history, in your mother's handwriting. I've seen grown men cry over a recipe card for pot roast. Don't throw those away. Scan them, and give copies to the family.
Number four. The war box. Somewhere in that house, usually in a closet or a dresser drawer, there's a box with Dad's or Grandpa's military things. Medals, patches, ribbons, a uniform, letters home, photographs.
Collectors of military history pay real money for these, and a complete group from one soldier, with his name and his papers, is worth much more together than broken apart. Depending on what's there, a group can be worth a few hundred dollars, and some are worth much more.
But I'll tell you the truth. Before you sell any of it, ask the family. Ask the grandkids. That box is often worth more to somebody in the family than to any collector. If you do sell, go to a reputable militaria dealer or an auction house that handles military items, and never break up a named group.
Number five. The Christmas boxes in the attic. Old glass ornaments, especially the ones still in their original boxes from the forties and fifties, sell to collectors. A box of a dozen common ones might bring twenty to sixty dollars. Older hand-blown European glass ornaments and unusual shapes can bring more. Old light-up decorations, the plastic ones that glow, have a following too.
Look for thin glass, original hangers, and the original box. Sell them in the fall, when people are thinking about Christmas. That's when the prices are best.
Number six. The quilts. In the cedar chest, or folded on a shelf. A handmade quilt, stitched by hand, in good condition, can sell for a hundred dollars to several hundred, and some older ones bring more. Look at the stitching. Hand quilting has small, slightly uneven stitches. Machine quilting is perfectly even, in long straight lines.
Stains, holes and fading lower the value a lot. And never wash an old quilt in a machine before you sell it. Let the buyer decide.
Number seven. The records. Here's where I have to be honest with you. Most old record albums sell for a dollar or two. Easy listening, Christmas albums, the big orchestras. Nobody wants them.
But certain records, early rock and roll, blues, jazz, some original first pressings in great condition, can bring fifty, a hundred, sometimes much more. Check the condition first. Hold the record up to the light. If it's scratched and dull, it's worth very little no matter what it is. If you've got a big collection, have a local record store look at it before you sell it as a lot.
Number eight. The toys in the attic. Toys from the fifties through the eighties, in their original boxes, can be worth money. Tin toys, old board games complete with all their pieces, toy trains, and the action figures your brother kept in a shoebox.
The box matters. A toy in its original box, with the box in good shape, can be worth several times the same toy loose. Check the sold listings for the exact name and year.
Number nine. The silverware drawer. I talked about sterling in my first video, but I'll say it again because I see it every week. Turn over a fork. If it says sterling, or 925, it's solid silver, and even a broken set is worth at least its weight in silver. If it says silverplate, or EPNS, it's worth very little. Families throw sterling away mixed in with the everyday stuff all the time.
Number ten. The paper. Old letters, documents, deeds, stock certificates, and envelopes with old stamps. Most of it is worth nothing to anybody but you. But old envelopes with the stamps still on them, especially from before the nineteen thirties, can interest stamp collectors. And old documents sometimes turn out to be important, like a savings bond nobody cashed, or a deed to a piece of land somebody forgot about.
Go through every box of paper before it goes in the recycling. Every one. I've seen families find uncashed savings bonds in a coffee can in the basement.
Now, what isn't worth your time. And this is going to hurt a little. The good china. Most families have a set, and most china sets today sell for very little, sometimes nothing at all. Replacement services buy some patterns, so check before you donate it, but don't expect much.
Hummel figurines and collector plates. Most of them sell for a few dollars each now. The people who collected them are getting older, and their kids don't want them.
Encyclopedias, old magazines like the yellow stacks of National Geographics, and the big wooden furniture, the dark bedroom sets and china cabinets. They're very hard to sell. Brown furniture, we call it in the business. Donate it, or give it to family who wants it, and don't feel bad.
Now, where do you sell all this? For a whole houseful, call an estate sale company. Get two or three to come look. A good one takes a percentage, often somewhere around a third, and in exchange they price it, run the sale and clean up. Ask what they do with what doesn't sell.
For the special pieces, the military group, the Pyrex set, the rare records, you'll usually do better selling them separately, to collectors or through an auction house that knows that category. And always check the sold listings first, so you know what you're holding.
