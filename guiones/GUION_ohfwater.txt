Now, why does a pilot go out? Especially this week?

The most common reason in a cold snap is a draft. Cold, heavy air coming down the flue, or a strong wind gusting at the vent cap on the roof, can blow a small pilot right out. Older houses with a water heater in a drafty basement get this every winter. A door left open to an outside bulkhead. A basement window cracked for a dryer. The wind hits, the pilot goes out, and by morning the tank is cold.

The other common reason is a worn out part called the thermocouple. I will explain that one in a minute, because it is the reason a pilot will not stay lit.

Now. How to relight it. And I am going to say this as plainly as I can. The label on your water heater is the boss. Every control is a little different. Read the label, and follow it step by step. What I am going to give you is the general idea, so the label makes sense when you read it.

Step one. Turn the gas control knob to off. Then wait. The label will tell you how long, usually several minutes, so any gas that collected around the burner can clear out. Do not skip the wait. And sniff again at the bottom. No gas smell before you go on.

Step two. Turn the knob to pilot.

Step three. Press the knob down, or press the separate pilot button, depending on your control, and hold it. That lets a little gas flow to the pilot.

Step four. While you are holding it, press the red igniter button. Click. Click. Look through the window. You are watching for a small blue flame at the end of the pilot tube. On some older heaters there is no igniter button, and the label tells you to use a long lighter through the access opening. Follow the label.

Step five. Once the pilot is lit, keep holding the knob down. This is the important part. Keep holding it for the amount of time on the label, usually somewhere around thirty seconds to a minute.

Step six. Let go, slowly. The pilot should stay lit. Then turn the knob from pilot to on. You will hear the main burner light with a soft whoosh. Put the access cover back on. Set the temperature where you want it.

Then give it time. A full tank of cold water takes a while to heat back up, often an hour or more. Do not expect hot water in five minutes.

Now, I promised you the thing almost everybody does in the last ten seconds. Here it is.

They let go too soon.

The pilot lights. It is right there, blue and pretty. And they let go of the knob after five seconds. And the pilot goes out. So they do it again. And again. And they decide the heater is broken.

Here is why. That thing I mentioned, the thermocouple, is a thin metal rod sitting right in the pilot flame. When it gets hot, it makes a tiny electric signal, and that signal tells the gas valve, yes, there is a flame, keep the gas on. It is the same idea as the flame sensor in the furnace, a safety that proves the fire is there. But it needs time in the flame to heat up. Thirty seconds, a minute. If you let go before it is hot, the valve thinks there is no flame, and shuts the pilot gas off.

So, hold it. Count it out loud if you need to. The full time on the label.

Now. What if you held it the full time, and the pilot still goes out the moment you let go?

Then the thermocouple is probably worn out, or the pilot flame is too small to heat it, because the pilot opening is dirty. A healthy pilot flame wraps around the tip of the thermocouple. A weak, small, yellow pilot does not.

A thermocouple is an inexpensive part, and replacing one is a very common repair. But it means working on the gas control of the heater. If you are handy and you are comfortable with that, the part comes with instructions and you match it at the hardware store. If you are not, this is a good moment to call somebody. Water on the floor around the bottom of the tank. If the tank itself is leaking, not a fitting, not the valve on the side, the tank, that heater is done. Tanks rust from the inside out, and once they leak, they do not get better.
