Let's talk about why grilled ribs at home come out tough.
A rib is a working muscle. It's full of what the butchers call connective tissue. That's the stringy, chewy stuff that holds the meat to the bone. It's tough. You can't chew it. But if you cook it low and slow, with some moisture around it, it melts. It turns into gelatin. And gelatin is what makes a rib feel juicy and sticky and tender in your mouth.
Here's the thing. That melting needs time. Hours. And it needs to stay wet. On a grill, over direct fire, the outside of the rib is getting blasted. It dries out and burns long before the inside has had time to melt. So you've got two choices on a grill. Pull them early, and they're tough. Or leave them on, and they dry out. Pitmasters with smokers get around it with ten hours of low smoke and a lot of skill. We didn't have ten hours. We had a crew coming in at six.
So we did it the other way around. Melt them first, wet and slow, in a covered pot. Then give them the fire at the very end, just long enough to make a crust.

And a word about the temperature. The USDA says pork is safe at a hundred and forty-five, with a three-minute rest. Ribs too. But a rib at a hundred and forty-five is tough.
