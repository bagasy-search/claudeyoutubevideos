First, why windows at all. A front door is in public. The neighbors see it, the street sees it, the doorbell camera sees it. A back window is private. It is behind a fence, or behind a bush, or down at the level of the flower bed where nobody ever looks.

And a burglar wants two things. Time and privacy. He does not want a fight with a lock. He wants a place where he can take his time without anybody watching him do it. The back window gives him both, and it gives him both for free.

Now I want to be careful here. I am not going to show you how anybody gets through a window. That is not what this channel is for. I am going to tell you why the weak spots are weak, and how to close them, and you can do every one of these on a Saturday.

There are three windows I would look at first in any house. The one at the back, facing the yard. The one in the basement, down low. And the bathroom window, because that is the one people leave cracked open for the steam and then forget about for a month.

Number one. The stick in the track. Zero dollars to about two.

If you have a sliding window, the kind that moves sideways in a track, it has a latch where the two panes meet. On a lot of older windows that latch is a thin piece of soft metal, and after twenty years of weather it does not grab the way it did when it was new.

The fix is almost embarrassing. You cut a piece of wood, a broom handle, a dowel, a length of old closet rod, so it fits snug in the bottom track between the moving pane and the frame. Now the window cannot slide open, latch or no latch. The wood is doing the job the latch was supposed to do.

Measure it with the window closed. You want it snug, not jammed. If you have to hammer it in, it is too long. If it rattles, it is too short. Cut it a hair long and sand the end down until it drops into the track with a little push.

And paint it. I mean that. A brown stick in a white track looks like junk, and sooner or later somebody in the house throws it out. A white stick looks like it belongs there, and it stays.

There is one more thing about sliding windows, and it matters. On some models there is a gap above the moving pane. The stick stops the slide. It does not stop the pane from being lifted up and out of that gap. So that brings us to number two.

Number two. The screw and the pin. About three dollars.

On a sliding window, the lift is stopped with a couple of screws set into the top track, just far enough down that the moving pane can still slide but has no room to rise. Some manufacturers sell little blocks made for exactly this. Check with the maker of your window first, because some frames have drain channels you do not want to put a screw through.

On a double hung window, the kind that goes up and down, the weak spot is the latch in the middle, the little half moon. So you pin the two sashes together. Where they overlap, you drill a small hole through the inside sash and partway into the outside one, and you drop in a steel pin.

Now the two sashes behave like one piece. That half moon latch can be as tired as it wants. The window does not open until you pull the pin out yourself, from the inside.

Drill at a slight downward angle so the pin stays put. Do not go all the way through the outer sash. Stop short, so the pin is buried where nobody outside can reach it. And if the window is the way out of a bedroom in a fire, use a pin you can pull with one hand in the dark. That part is not optional.

Number three. A real window lock. Somewhere between six and fifteen dollars.

Let us talk about that half moon latch, because everybody has one and almost nobody has looked at theirs in years. It is called a sash lock, and the thing to understand is that it is mostly a latch. It pulls the window tight against the weather. It was never really meant to hold against a determined person.

Go put your hand on yours. If it turns with one finger, if it wobbles on its screws, if the two halves do not quite meet anymore because the house has settled, it is not doing much. I replaced a lot of those, and most of them were not broken. They were just tired.

The replacement is cheap and it usually takes two screws. Get one with a key, or one with a button that has to be pressed before the latch will turn. For sliding windows there are clamp-on track locks that tighten right down onto the rail. Any of those is better than a latch that has quietly given up.

Keep the key on a hook near the window, not in the lock. Out of reach from the glass, but where anybody in the house can find it in a hurry. Same rule as the pin. A lock that traps you inside is worse than no lock at all.

Number four. A sensor that screams. About fifteen dollars.

If you saw my video about the car key, you know I like security that makes noise more than security that looks tough. Noise is the one thing he cannot plan around. He does not know who is home, and he does not know who heard it.

A window contact sensor is two small pieces. One goes on the sash, one goes on the frame. When they pull apart, it goes off. The simplest ones need no system and no monthly bill. They run on a battery with a siren built right in, and they are loud enough to wake you, and loud enough for him to hear it too.

Put one on the basement window and one on the bathroom window. Those are the two he counts on nobody watching. And change the batteries when you change the clocks, because a sensor with a dead battery is just a sticker.

Here is the trick with sensors. Test them. Open the window and let it scream once a month. The first time you do it, nobody in the house will thank you. The second time, they will all know what that sound means, and that is the whole point.

Number five. A bush with thorns. Twenty to forty dollars, and a Saturday afternoon.

This one sounds like gardening advice, and it is, and it is also one of the oldest ideas in home security. Under a ground floor window, a thorny shrub turns the one spot he needs to stand in into the worst spot in the whole yard.

Barberry, holly, a climbing rose, pyracantha where it grows. Ask your local nursery what does well where you live, because I am a locksmith, not a gardener. A few of these are considered invasive in some states, so check before you plant.

And here is the other half of it, the half people miss. Cut back everything else. The big shrub by the back window that you love, the one that hides the air conditioner, also hides a man. Keep the hedges below the window sill. If you cannot see that window from the street or from the neighbor's yard, neither can anybody else.

Number six. The one I told you to wait for. Security film. Thirty to sixty dollars a window.

Here is what happens when a regular window breaks. It breaks once, it makes one loud noise, and there is a hole. That is the whole event. A couple of seconds.

Security film is a clear layer that goes on the inside of the glass. When somebody hits it, the glass still cracks. It spiders out like a windshield. But the pieces stay stuck to the film, and the hole does not open. So now he is not done in two seconds. He is standing there, hitting it again, and again, making noise, out in the open, while the lights come on next door.

I want to be straight with you, because there is a lot of hype about this online. Film does not make glass bulletproof. It does not make it unbreakable. What it does is buy time, and time is the one thing he came to your back window to avoid.

Two things decide whether it works. The thickness of the film, and how it is held at the edges. The thin tint you buy to keep the sun out is not security film. Look for the word security on the box and a thickness in mils, and thicker is tougher. Some makers also sell an edge anchoring system that ties the film to the frame.

That is where people go wrong. They buy the thin stuff, they trim it short of the edge, and the whole sheet can be pushed in like a flap. Put it edge to edge. It is an afternoon job, and it is worth doing right. If you can only afford one window, do the one next to the back door lock, where a hole means a hand can reach in.

Number seven. Light. Twenty five to sixty dollars.

The last one is the oldest one, and it is still one of the best. A motion light over the back of the house. Not a porch light that burns all night long, which you stop noticing after a week. A light that comes on when something moves.

A light that snaps on does two things. It puts him on a stage. And it tells him the house is paying attention. It does not matter if you are sound asleep. He does not know that.

Mount it high, out of easy reach, so nobody can just unscrew the bulb. Aim it at the path he would walk in on, not at the window itself, so it catches him on the way, not when he is already standing there. And if you have a basement window well, make sure the light reaches down into it. That is the darkest hole on the whole house.

Now, a word about what does not work as well as people think. A sign in the yard for an alarm company you do not have. A fake camera with no wire running out of it. Bars on every window, which can trap you inside in a fire unless they have a quick release on the inside. I am not telling you never to use any of those.
