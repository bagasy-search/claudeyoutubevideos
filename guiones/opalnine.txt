Number nine. The wrong roost. When I was young, I nailed up a couple of old broom handles for perches, nice and high, up near the rafters, because I thought chickens liked to be up high. And they do. But a big heavy hen jumping down off a high, skinny pole lands hard, every single morning.
I had a big black hen named Bertha. Heaviest bird I ever owned. One spring she started limping, and when I turned her over there was a hard, round scab on the bottom of her foot, with the foot all swollen around it. That's bumblefoot. It's an infection that gets in through a little crack in the foot pad, and hard landings are a big part of how it starts.
It took me weeks of warm Epsom salt soaks and wraps, and a trip to the vet that cost me sixty dollars. She lived, but she walked funny the rest of her life.
The fix cost me about five dollars. A plain two by four, with the wide side up, so they sit flat-footed and their toes are covered by their feathers on a cold night. Low enough that a heavy hen can step down instead of jumping, two, three feet for the big ones. And give each hen about eight to ten inches of roost.
Number eight. Too many treats. I love my girls, and I used to give them everything. The bread crusts, the leftover noodles, the pancakes, a whole bucket of scraps every evening. And they come running, and it feels like love.
But a laying hen needs her feed. Layer feed is made to be balanced, about sixteen percent protein, with the calcium she needs to make shells. When she fills up on scraps, she eats less of it. The usual rule is treats and scraps no more than about ten percent of what they eat. A small handful, not a bucket.
I had a pretty golden hen named Goldie who was the first one at the scrap bucket every single night. She got heavy. She quit laying. And one morning she was dead under the roost, with no sign of anything. I opened her up to see, and her belly was full of yellow fat, and her liver was pale and soft and broken. That's called fatty liver. Overweight hens get it, and it can take them in a minute.
And there are a few things that never go in my scrap bucket. Nothing moldy, ever. No avocado, not the skin and not the pit. No raw dry beans. And nothing salty. Next month I'll do a whole video on my scrap bucket, because you've asked me about it a hundred times.
Number seven. No oyster shell. For years I figured the layer feed had all the calcium they needed. And for most hens, it does. But a hen that lays an egg almost every day pulls a lot of calcium out of her own body to make those shells, and some of them need more than the feed gives them.
The first sign was eggs with thin shells, soft shells, eggs that cracked in the nest box. I didn't think much of it. Then one afternoon I found Hattie, my best layer, sitting in the yard all puffed up, pumping her tail, standing like a penguin. She was egg bound. The egg was stuck inside her.
I brought her in and put her in a warm bath in the laundry sink, and I did everything I knew. I lost her that night. And over the years I lost one more the same way before I finally learned.
Now, don't mix that up with a broody hen. A broody hen sits flat in the nest box, puffs up and growls at you if you reach under her, but she's calm, and she'll get off to eat and drink once a day. An egg bound hen is restless. She's up and down, straining, her tail pumping, and she won't eat. If you see that, don't wait till morning.
The fix is a bag of crushed oyster shell. About eight dollars for a five pound bag at my feed store, and it lasts my flock for months. And here's the part people get wrong. You don't mix it into the feed. You put it in its own little dish, off to the side, and every hen takes what she needs. The ones that need it will eat it. The ones that don't, won't.
Number six. Too many roosters. One year a neighbor had extra roosters, and I took three of them for my fifteen hens, because I liked hearing them in the morning. That was a mistake.
The usual figure is about one rooster for every ten hens, sometimes a little more. With three roosters and fifteen hens, my girls never got a minute's peace. By midsummer half of them had bare backs, every feather scraped off by the roosters' feet and spurs.
And then I found Sadie in the corner of the run. A spur had torn the skin open on her back, and in that heat, the flies found it before I did. I couldn't save her. And later that summer I lost another one the same way.
So two hens. The fix was giving away two roosters, and it cost me nothing. If you've got hens with bare backs already, a little canvas hen saddle runs about eight or ten dollars and covers the back while the feathers grow in. And you know what? Your hens don't need a rooster at all to lay eggs. You only need one if you want chicks.
Number five. A wet coop. For years I kept my waterer right inside on the floor of the coop. And every day the hens would knock it, splash it, scratch shavings into it, and the floor around it stayed wet. I'd throw some fresh shavings on top and call it good.
In the spring of 2009, I had a batch of young pullets, about two months old. One morning one of them was standing hunched up by herself, feathers fluffed, eyes half closed. Her name was Peaches. By that night she was dead. And then the next one, and the next. And there was blood in the droppings.
That's coccidiosis. It's a tiny parasite that lives in the litter, and it loves warm, wet bedding. Grown hens usually handle it. Young birds can go down fast. I lost five pullets in a week before I knew what it was.
The treatment is a medicine called amprolium that you put in their water. A bottle cost me about twenty dollars, and it stopped it. But the real fix was moving the water. Now my waterer hangs outside in the run, up on a couple of cinder blocks, at the height of their backs, so they can't scratch dirt into it. And if I find a wet spot in the coop, I shovel it out that same day, I don't cover it up.
And every morning, before I even feed them, I look at the droppings under the roost. That's the cheapest check there is. Normal is a firm brown dropping with a little white cap on it. Watery and foamy now and then is fine too. But blood, or a hen standing off by herself all hunched up, that's the day you act, not the day after.
Number four. A coop that was shut up too tight. My first winter on this farm, I did what I thought was the kind thing. I stuffed rags in every crack, nailed plastic over the windows, closed every vent, and kept that coop as tight as a jar. I wanted them warm.
And by January the inside walls were wet with frost, the whole place stank of ammonia, and my hens had black tips on their combs. That's frostbite. Three of my hens got a cough and a rattle, and I lost all three before spring.
Here's what I didn't understand. It's not the cold that hurts them. It's the wet. Every hen breathes out water all night long, and their droppings are wet too. If the air can't get out, it sits in there, and it freezes on their combs and their wattles. And the ammonia burns their lungs.
The fix is vents up high, near the roof, above where they sleep, so the wet air goes out and no wind blows across them on the roost. A rule of thumb a lot of folks use is about a square foot of vent for every ten square feet of floor. And here's my test. If you walk in and you can smell ammonia up at your nose, it's a lot worse down where they're breathing.
The fix cost me a saw, an afternoon, and a piece of hardware cloth over each opening. And I haven't had a frostbitten comb in this coop in thirty years.
Number three. Chicken wire. If you watched my video about the three hens I lost to a mink, you know how I feel about this one. Chicken wire is made to keep chickens in. It doesn't keep anything out.
Over the years, before I learned, I lost seven hens to raccoons through chicken wire. A raccoon will reach right through it and grab a hen that's sleeping close to the fence. And a dog or a raccoon can tear it open at the seams.
The fix is half inch hardware cloth, screwed down with washers, over every window and every vent, and a wire apron along the bottom so nothing digs under. My last roll cost me about sixty dollars, and it was the best sixty dollars I ever spent on this farm.
Number two. The heat lamp. This is the one I still think about. It was the winter of 1987, the coldest one I can remember. It got down to twenty below. And I hung one of those red heat lamps in the coop, clamped to a rafter, because I was scared my hens would freeze.
I woke up at three in the morning and the sky out the window was orange. The clamp had let go, or a hen knocked it, and that lamp dropped right down into the pine shavings. Those bulbs get hot enough to start a fire in dry bedding. I lost the coop, and I lost nine hens. My husband and I stood out there in our coats and watched it burn.
And here's what hurts the most. They didn't need it. A grown hen in a dry coop with no draft, fluffed up on a wide roost, will get through twenty below just fine. They've got a down coat on. And if you keep them warm all winter and the power goes out on the coldest night, now they've never gotten used to the cold, and that's worse.
Now, baby chicks are a different story. Chicks don't have their feathers yet, and they do need heat for their first several weeks. But even for chicks, I quit using the hanging lamp. I use one of those flat heating plates they crawl under, like a mother hen, and it sits low and steady and can't fall into anything. It cost more than a lamp, about fifty dollars, and it's lasted me years.
But for grown hens, I don't use a heat lamp. Not ever. That fix cost me nothing at all. And in two weeks I'm going to show you exactly how my hens get through an Indiana winter with no heat at all, because a lot of you have asked.
Now, before number one, a word about what I won't buy. Every fall the feed store puts out the scented coop fresheners, the sweet-smelling powders and sprays. I don't buy them. If your coop stinks, it's wet. Perfume doesn't dry anything, and a cloud of sweet dust is the last thing those little lungs need. Dry bedding and fresh air, that's what a clean coop smells like. And number one. Bringing new hens straight home to the flock.
In the fall of 1998 I went to a poultry swap at the county fairgrounds and came home with three beautiful hens. I put them right in with my girls that same afternoon. I didn't think a thing about it. They looked perfectly healthy.
About two weeks later, one of my old hens was sneezing. Then another. Then the whole coop was sneezing and rattling. Bubbly, watery eyes. Swollen faces. Some of them would stand there with their eyes shut and that awful gurgle when they breathed.
It was a respiratory disease called mycoplasma. And here's the hard part about it. A bird can get over the worst of it and still carry it for the rest of her life, and give it to every new bird you bring in. Those three hens from the swap looked fine because they'd already had it.
I lost eleven hens that winter. And in the spring, on the advice of the extension office, I made the hardest decision I've ever made with chickens. I let the rest of that flock go, scrubbed the coop down to the bare wood, left it empty, and started over from chicks.
So now, every new bird that comes onto this farm goes into quarantine. Thirty days at least, as far away from my flock as I can get her. Different water, different feed, and I take care of them last, after my own girls, never first. Some folks keep separate boots just for the quarantine pen, and that's a good idea too.
And every day in that crate, I look them over. Is she sneezing? Any bubbles or wet at the corner of her eye? Any rattle when she breathes? I check her droppings. I part the feathers under her vent and under her wings, looking for lice and mites. And I look at her legs, because rough, raised scales can mean leg mites. If anything shows up, the clock starts over at thirty days.
Thirty days. Because that's long enough for most sickness to show itself. And that's what that old dog crate in the barn is for. It cost me nothing. And it would have saved me eleven hens.
