You might think a space heater is harmless until it starts cooking a cheap power strip behind your couch. I spent forty years climbing poles and pulling wire, and I have seen winter fires start from one bad outlet and one lazy cord. First, never plug a space heater into a power strip or surge protector. Those little boxes are not built for the steady heat draw of a heater. They can overheat, melt, and catch fire even if the breaker never trips. Same rule for those three-prong adapters that split one outlet into two. A heater needs a solid wall outlet, not a crowded strip. Do not use a light-duty extension cord, the kind you grab for a lamp or a phone charger. Look at the cord jacket. If it says fourteen or sixteen gauge, put it away. Check the label on the heater. If it says one thousand five hundred watts, that is about twelve point five amps at one hundred twenty volts. A heater usually pulls around twelve amps on a fifteen amp circuit, so it wants a heavier cord, no less than twelve gauge, and short. If the cord is thin, old, cracked, taped, or coiled up in a roll, it can get hot and fail. Never run it under a rug, carpet, or furniture where heat has nowhere to go. Run the cord flat, not in a loop. Avoid outlets that feel loose, buzz, discolor, or sit next to a damp area without proper protection. A heater plugged into a worn outlet is asking for trouble. If the plug falls out or the outlet gets warm, stop. Do not use a cord with a missing ground pin, a broken plug housing, or a cheap adapter that turns two prongs into three. Have an electrician fix it. Before the first cold night, walk to your breaker panel and label the circuit the heater will use. If that circuit also feeds the kitchen, bathroom, or a freezer, pick a different outlet. A heater can trip a breaker, and a breaker that keeps tripping is telling you something is wrong. Here is what to do instead. Plug the heater straight into a wall outlet, ideally one fed by its own circuit. Do not share that circuit with the fridge, microwave, hair dryer, or another heater. Keep the heater away from curtains, bedding, and paper. Turn it off when you leave the room. If you absolutely must use a cord, use a short, heavy-duty, grounded extension cord rated for the heater, lay it straight, and check it with your hand every few minutes. Warm cord means stop.
