The worst night of my career started with a sound like a rifle shot. Then another one. Then a dozen at once, all over the hillside. It was January, it was dark, and I was standing next to my bucket truck listening to trees explode. That's what ice does. It coats every branch, every wire, a quarter inch, half an inch, an inch thick, and the weight keeps building until something gives. And when a tree gives, it takes the power line with it.
We had forty thousand people in the dark that night. Some of them were out for two days. Some of them were out for two weeks. And I'll tell you something I learned up on those poles. The families who were fine weren't the ones with the biggest generator or the fanciest house. They were the ones who spent one evening getting ready before the ice came.
So if there's an ice storm in your forecast, this is that evening. I'm going to walk you through the checklist I'd do tonight, in my own house, in the order I'd do it. Water, heat, food, the pipes, the car, the generator, and the one thing about downed power lines that every lineman wishes every homeowner knew. Because that one could save your life.
My name's Harlan. I climbed utility poles and restored power for forty years, and I've seen what a cold, dark house does to people. And I've seen what gets them through it.
First thing tonight. Charge everything. Phones, tablets, the cordless drill batteries, the little battery packs you got for Christmas and never used. Every one of them, all the way to full. And if you've got a car, find the charger cord for it and put it in the glove box. Your car is a big battery on wheels. If the house goes dark for three days, that car can keep your phone alive, and your phone is how you call for help and how you find out what's going on.
Second, water. This is the one people forget, and it's the one that hurts the most. If you're on city water, it'll probably keep coming for a while. But if you're on a well, your well pump runs on electricity. No power, no water. Not to drink, not to cook, not to flush.
So tonight, fill your bathtub. Fill it right up. That's not for drinking. That's for flushing. A bucket of water poured into the toilet bowl will flush it just fine. Then fill every pitcher and big pot you've got with drinking water, and put a few jugs in the fridge. A person needs about a gallon a day, for drinking and a little cooking. Figure out how many people are in your house, and count the days.
Third, the freezer and the fridge. Turn them both down to the coldest setting tonight. Colder food stays cold longer. Then fill the empty space in the freezer with jugs of water or bags of ice. A full freezer holds its cold a whole lot longer than a half empty one. Those frozen jugs also become your ice for the cooler, and later, drinking water.
And here's an old trick my wife taught me. Freeze a cup of water. Once it's frozen solid, put a coin on top of the ice and leave it in the freezer. When the power comes back on, look at that coin. If it's still sitting on top, your freezer stayed frozen. If it sank to the bottom of the cup, everything in there thawed out and froze again while you weren't watching, and you need to think hard before you eat any of it.
And during the outage, keep those doors shut. Every time you open the fridge, you let the cold out. A closed refrigerator will keep food cold for about four hours. A full freezer that stays shut can hold for about two days. Plan your meals so you open them as little as you can.
Fourth, pick your warm room. This is where ice storms go from uncomfortable to dangerous. When the power goes out in January, your house starts losing heat right away. You can't heat the whole thing. So don't try.
Pick one room. A small one, toward the middle of the house, with as few outside walls and windows as you can get. Tonight, get it ready. Bring in blankets, sleeping bags, extra pillows. Hang a blanket over the doorway if it doesn't have a door. Roll up a towel and lay it along the bottom of the door to stop the draft. Close the curtains on the windows. Everybody sleeps in that room. Everybody's body heat stays in that room. People are surprised how warm a small room can stay with a few people and a dog in it.
Dress in layers, even inside. Wool socks. A hat. You lose a lot of heat through your head, and grandma knew it. And keep the kids and the older folks in the house in the warm room first.
Now, I have to talk about heat, because this is where every winter, somebody doesn't wake up.
Never, ever bring a grill, a camp stove, or a charcoal anything into the house to stay warm. Never heat the house with the gas oven. Those things put out carbon monoxide, and you can't see it, you can't smell it, and it puts you to sleep before you know anything's wrong. Every big ice storm, there are families who lose people this way. They were cold, they were trying to stay warm, and it killed them.
If you've got a kerosene heater or a propane heater rated for indoor use, follow the label to the letter, crack a window like it says, and keep it away from blankets and curtains. And tonight, check your carbon monoxide detector. Put a fresh battery in it. If you don't have one, get one. They're not expensive, and it's the cheapest life insurance you'll ever buy.
Fifth, light. Put away the candles. I know, candles are what everybody thinks of. But every ice storm, fire departments get called to houses that caught fire from a candle somebody fell asleep next to. And the fire trucks are slower that night, because the roads are covered in ice and trees.
Use flashlights, battery lanterns, and headlamps. A headlamp especially. When the lights go out, your hands have jobs to do. Carrying water, holding a baby, feeding the dog. Put a headlamp and fresh batteries in every bedroom tonight, right where you can reach them in the dark.
Sixth, your pipes. When the house gets cold, the water pipes on the outside walls can freeze, and frozen pipes burst. Then when it warms up, you've got water spraying in your walls on top of everything else.
So tonight, open the cabinet doors under your kitchen and bathroom sinks, so the warmer air from the room can get to the pipes. If the power goes out and the house starts getting cold, let the faucets on the outside walls drip, just a little, a slow trickle. Moving water is a lot harder to freeze.
And here's the big one. Know where your main water shutoff valve is. Go find it tonight, with a flashlight, while the lights still work. If a pipe does burst, you want to shut the water off in thirty seconds, not thirty minutes while you hunt for it in the dark.
Seventh, the car. Fill up your gas tank tonight. Gas station pumps run on electricity too. When the whole town is dark, the gas stations are dark, and the few that are open have lines down the road.
Park your car in the garage if you have one, or at least away from trees. I can't tell you how many cars I've seen with a big limb laying across the roof after an ice storm. And put a blanket, a flashlight, some water and a snack in it, in case you have to drive somewhere and get stuck.
Eighth, the things you can't get later. Refill any medicines that are running low, tonight, before the storm. Pharmacies close when the power's out. If somebody in your house uses oxygen or any medical equipment that plugs in, call your power company before the storm and tell them. A lot of them keep a list, and they'll want to know.
Get some cash out of the bank. When the power's down, card machines are down too. The little store that's open with a generator might only take cash. And grab a manual can opener if you don't have one. A pantry full of canned food doesn't do you any good if your can opener plugs into the wall.
Food you don't have to cook is your friend. Peanut butter, crackers, bread, canned fruit, granola bars, a jar of pickles if that's your thing. If you've got a gas stove with a pilot or a match light, you may be able to cook on the burners. But no oven for heat, remember that.
Ninth, the generator. Now we're getting into my world.
If you have a portable generator, it goes outside. Not in the garage. Not in the garage with the door open. Not on the porch under the roof. Outside, out in the open, at least twenty feet from the house, with the exhaust pointed away from every door and window. That same carbon monoxide we talked about comes pouring out of a generator, and a garage fills up in minutes.
And this next part is personal for me. Never plug a generator into a wall outlet in your house to power the house. Folks call it backfeeding. They use a cord with two male plugs, plug it into the dryer outlet, and think they've been clever. Here's what really happens. That power doesn't just go into your house. It goes back out through your meter, up into the transformer on the pole, and the transformer turns your little two hundred and forty volts into thousands of volts on the line.
And who's up on that line, in the dark, in the ice, trying to get your lights back on? A lineman. A man who thought that line was dead, because it should be.
I lost a friend that way. Not from that exact storm, but the same way. So please. If you want a generator to run part of your house, have an electrician put in a transfer switch. Otherwise, run extension cords from the generator to the things you need. The fridge, a lamp, a phone charger. That's it.
Tenth, and this is the one I told you about at the beginning. Downed power lines.
After an ice storm, there are wires on the ground everywhere. In yards, in roads, tangled in fallen trees. And here's the rule that every lineman lives by. Treat every wire on the ground as if it's alive. Every single one. Even if it's not sparking. Even if it's not humming. Even if your neighbor tells you the power's been out for hours.
Lines can be re-energized without warning, by a crew trying to restore power, or by somebody's generator backfeeding, like we just talked about. And the ground around a downed line can carry electricity too, especially when it's wet and icy.
So stay back. Thirty feet at least, about the length of a school bus. Keep the kids and the dogs away. Don't drive over a downed line. Don't touch a tree limb or a fence that's touching one. Call your power company or nine one one and tell them exactly where it is.
And if a line falls on your car while you're in it, stay in the car. The car is the safest place to be. Call for help and wait. The only reason to get out is if the car is on fire. And then you jump clear, both feet together, without touching the car and the ground at the same time, and you shuffle away with your feet close together. Never run.
Now, while you're waiting, here's how getting the power back actually works, so you know what to expect. Crews don't go house by house in the order people call. We start with the big stuff that feeds the most people. The power plants and the big lines first. Then the substations. Then the main lines that run down the big roads and feed whole neighborhoods. Then the smaller lines down the side streets. And last, the single wire that runs from the pole to your house.
So if your neighbors across the street have lights and you don't, it usually means your little line is still down, or the transformer that feeds just your street is broken. Call it in, even if you think someone already did.
And be patient with the crews. Those men and women are working sixteen hour days, in the ice and the dark, away from their own families, who are probably sitting in the dark too. If you see a bucket truck on your road, don't stop and ask them how long. Give them a wave, and maybe a cup of coffee if you've got a way to make one. I'll never forget a lady who brought our crew a thermos of coffee at three in the morning. I don't remember her name, but I remember that coffee.
Last thing tonight. Check on your neighbors. Especially the older ones who live alone. Get their phone numbers if you don't have them. Make sure they've got water, blankets and a flashlight. If their house gets too cold, bring them into your warm room. That's how people got through ice storms long before there were power companies, and that's still how they do it today.
Don't forget the animals. Bring the dogs and cats inside tonight, into the warm room with you. If you've got chickens or livestock, make sure they've got shelter out of the wind and water that won't freeze solid. A bucket of water turns into a block of ice overnight, so plan on carrying fresh water out to them twice a day, and break the ice on their troughs every morning.
And stay out from under the trees. During the storm and for a day after, those iced up limbs keep coming down, and a branch the size of your arm, with an inch of ice on it, can kill you. I've seen a big oak limb come down through a bedroom ceiling like it was paper. If you hear cracking in the trees, get away from that side of the house. Don't go out to look. Don't go out to clear the driveway. Wait until the ice melts off. The branches will still be there tomorrow.
Now, when the power does come back on, there's one more thing to do, and almost nobody does it. Before the power comes back, unplug your expensive stuff. The television, the computer, the microwave. Leave one lamp switched on so you'll know when the power's back. When the lines come back up, the power can surge and flicker for a while, and that's hard on electronics. Once it's been steady for a few minutes, plug things back in one at a time. Don't turn everything on at once. Give the system a chance to settle down. Your furnace, your water heater and your refrigerator will all be pulling hard to catch up.
