My name's Harlan. I climbed utility poles and restored power for forty years. And when you work on electricity your whole life, you get a real healthy respect for heat and wire. Because that's all a fire really is. Too much heat, in the wrong place, with nothing to stop it.
So here's the one thing. Never plug a space heater into a power strip or an extension cord. Never. Plug it straight into a wall outlet, by itself.
Let me tell you why, because once you understand it, you'll never do it again.
A space heater is one of the hungriest things you can plug into your house. Most of them pull fifteen hundred watts on high. That's about twelve and a half amps, every second it's running. Your toaster pulls a lot for a couple of minutes. Your space heater pulls that much for hours.
Now think about a power strip. Most power strips are made for little things. A lamp, a phone charger, a television, a computer. The wires inside are thin, and the little metal contacts that grip the plug are thin. When you push a space heater's twelve and a half amps through that for hours, those thin parts get hot. And a cheap power strip doesn't have anything to stop it from getting hotter and hotter, until the plastic melts and catches fire.
Same thing with most extension cords. The light, skinny ones people use for Christmas lights or a lamp aren't made for that much power for that long. The cord heats up. And if that cord is running under a rug, where the heat can't get away, that's how houses burn. I've pulled melted extension cords out from under carpet more times than I like to think about.
So, straight into the wall. And not into any wall outlet. Into a good one.
Here's what I mean. Before you plug a heater in, look at the outlet. Is it loose? Does the plug wiggle or fall out easily? Is the cover cracked? Is there any brown or black discoloring around the holes? If any of that's true, don't use that outlet for a heater. A loose outlet means a loose connection, and a loose connection makes heat.
And after the heater's been running for a while, here's a test I want everybody to do. Put your hand on the plug where it goes into the wall. A little warm is normal. If it's hot, too hot to keep your hand on, unplug it right away. Something's wrong, either with the outlet, the wiring in the wall, or the heater's plug. Don't use that outlet for the heater again until an electrician checks it.
Next, one heater per circuit. This one surprises people. The outlets in your house are connected in groups called circuits, and each circuit has a breaker in your electrical panel. Most bedroom and living room circuits are made to handle about fifteen amps. Your space heater's using twelve and a half of that by itself.
So if you plug a space heater into one outlet, and a hair dryer or a microwave or another heater into a different outlet on the same circuit, you've gone over. If you're lucky, the breaker trips and the power goes off in that part of the house. That's the breaker doing its job. If the breaker keeps tripping, don't just keep flipping it back on. It's telling you something. Move the heater to a different room's outlet, or turn something else off.
And never, ever replace a breaker with a bigger one, or tape it so it can't trip, so the heater stops shutting off. I've seen folks do it. That breaker is the only thing standing between too much current and the wires inside your walls getting hot enough to start a fire you can't see.
Now let's talk about the heater itself. Not all space heaters are built the same.
When you buy one, look for a label from a testing lab. In this country it usually says UL or ETL or CSA, in a little circle. That means somebody actually tested it. The cheapest no-name heater on the internet with no testing label is not worth the money you save.
Make sure it has a tip-over switch. That's a little switch on the bottom that shuts the heater off if it gets knocked over. A dog runs by, a kid bumps it, it falls over face down on the carpet, and it shuts right off instead of setting the carpet on fire.
And look for an automatic shutoff if it overheats. Most good ones have it now. If yours is old and doesn't have either of those, it's time for a new one.
People ask me what kind of heater to buy. Here's the short version. An oil-filled radiator, the kind that looks like an old-fashioned radiator on little wheels, heats slowly and stays warm, and the outside doesn't get as hot as some other kinds. It's what I keep in my own bedroom. A ceramic heater with a fan heats a small room fast and usually has good safety features. The ones with glowing red coils or bars right on the front, the old radiant kind, get very hot right at the front, and those are the ones I'd be most careful with around curtains and blankets. Whatever you buy, check the label and the safety switches first.
And if you've got little kids or pets, think about it from their eye level. A toddler will put their hand right on the front of a heater. A cat will curl up right next to it, or behind it. A dog's tail will knock it over. Pick a heater that stays cooler on the outside, put it where they can't get to it easily, and never leave them alone in a room with a heater running.
Next, give it room. Three feet. Keep everything that can burn at least three feet away from a space heater. Curtains, beds, blankets, couches, clothes, paper, the dog's bed, the Christmas tree. Three feet in every direction. I like to tell people, if you can't walk all the way around it without touching something, it's too close.
Put it on a hard, flat floor if you can. Not on a rug, not on a bed, not on a chair, and never on top of something else to get it higher. And keep it out of the walkway, where somebody's going to trip on it in the dark.
Next, and this is a big one. Never leave a space heater running when you leave the room or go to sleep. I know it's cold. I know it's nice to fall asleep warm. But a space heater running all night next to the bed while everybody's asleep is exactly how so many of these fires start. Turn it off, unplug it, and pile on an extra blanket. Warm the room up before bed if you want to, and then shut it off.
And make sure you've got working smoke alarms. Test them, change the batteries. That family I told you about at the beginning got out alive because of one smoke alarm. That's it. That's the only reason.
Now, bathrooms and kitchens. If you use a heater in a bathroom, it should be plugged into an outlet with those little test and reset buttons on it. Those are called ground fault outlets, and they shut off the power in a split second if water gets where it shouldn't. And never, ever have a heater anywhere near a bathtub or a sink full of water. That's not a fire risk. That's an electrocution risk.
Let me give you the warning signs, because your house usually tells you before it burns. A smell like hot plastic or fish when the heater's running. That fishy smell surprises people, but overheating electrical parts often smell like fish. Lights that flicker or dim when the heater turns on. An outlet cover or switch plate that feels warm. A buzzing or crackling sound from the outlet. Scorch marks on the plug. A breaker that trips over and over. Any one of those, turn the heater off, unplug it, and find out why before you use it again.
And a few myths I hear every winter. People think a heater that's been fine for years will always be fine. Not true. Plugs wear out, outlets loosen, cords get crushed. People think a heavy duty power strip with a big switch is fine for a heater. Most of them still say right on the label not to use them with heaters, so read it. And people think if the breaker hasn't tripped, everything's safe. A breaker protects against too much current, but a loose, hot connection at one outlet can start a fire without ever tripping a thing.
Let me talk about older houses for a minute, because a lot of you watching live in one, and so do I. If your house is old, the wiring might not be made for the kind of power we use today.
