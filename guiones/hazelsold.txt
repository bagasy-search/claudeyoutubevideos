A woman called me last month nearly in tears. Her mother had passed, and she'd been told for thirty years that the china cabinet was her inheritance. Hummel figurines, a Waterford vase, a full set of fine bone china, and a stack of Beanie Babies in plastic cases. Her mother used to say, that's your retirement, honey.
She asked me to come take a look. And I had to sit down at her kitchen table and tell her the hardest thing I tell families. Most of it was worth less than she paid for the gas to drive over.
But there was one thing in that house she almost gave to a neighbor for free. And it was worth more than everything in the china cabinet put together.
So today, I'm going to go through ten things families are sure are valuable. The things everybody's grandmother collected, the things people store in the attic for decades waiting to sell. I'll tell you what they've really been selling for lately, sold prices, not asking prices, and which ones are actually worth your time. Some of these are going to sting a little. And a couple are going to surprise you.
I'm Hazel. I ran estate sales in Ohio for forty years, and I've had this conversation at more kitchen tables than I can count. I'd rather you hear it from me now than from a stranger at a yard sale later.
Before we start, one thing. When I say what something sells for, I mean what people actually paid. Anybody can put a price on something online. Go to eBay, search for your item, and then use the filter that shows only sold listings. Those are real prices. The asking prices are just wishes. Keep that in mind for everything I tell you today.
Number one. Hummel figurines. The little German children with the rosy cheeks, the boy with the umbrella, the girl feeding the geese. Every grandmother had a shelf of them, and for a long time they really were valuable. Collectors paid hundreds of dollars.
Here's what happened. The people who collected them got older, and their kids didn't want them. Now there are far more Hummels for sale than people who want to buy them. Most common Hummels sell for somewhere between ten and fifty dollars. A lot of them don't sell at all.
Now, there's one thing worth checking. Turn it over and look at the mark on the bottom. The oldest ones have a crown mark or a full bee. Those early marks, from before the late nineteen fifties, can still bring more, especially large or unusual figures. The newer ones with the plain stylized bee or the words Goebel Germany are the common ones. So check the mark, but don't expect a fortune.
Number two. Precious Moments. The little teardrop-eyed porcelain children. Same story, even more so. They were made by the millions, people bought them as gifts for every occasion, and almost everybody kept them in the box. Most sell for five to twenty dollars, and many sell in big lots for even less. If you have a whole cabinet, sell them as a group to one buyer and let them go.
Number three. Fine bone china. The full set of twelve, with the gold rim, the one that came out only at Thanksgiving. This is the one that breaks hearts, because it means so much to families. But young people today don't want a set of china they can't put in the dishwasher or the microwave. A full service of common bone china often sells for a few hundred dollars at most, and sometimes much less. Individual plates and cups often go for a few dollars each.
Now, here's where you should look closer. Some famous makers and some patterns still sell well, especially to people trying to replace a broken piece in their own set. There are companies that buy and sell replacement china, and they'll tell you what your pattern is worth. Look on the back of a plate for the maker and the pattern name, and look that up before you donate it.
Number four. Waterford crystal. People are always shocked by this one. Waterford was expensive when it was new, and it's beautiful. But today, a lot of Waterford vases, bowls and glasses sell for twenty to sixty dollars. Some of the big presentation pieces do better, but most don't come close to what someone paid in the store.
Here's a tip. Real Waterford usually has a little acid-etched mark on the bottom, very faint. Hold it up to the light and turn it. If you find it, you know what you have. Then check those sold listings, and you'll know what it's really worth.
Number five. Beanie Babies. I had to include them, because every week somebody tells me they saw a Beanie Baby online for ten thousand dollars. Here's the truth. Somebody listed it for ten thousand dollars. That doesn't mean anybody paid it. Look at the sold listings, and you'll see most Beanie Babies sell for a dollar to five dollars, if they sell at all. A big bag of them often goes for twenty dollars. The ones with the tag errors that people talk about almost never sell for those crazy prices.
So if you have a closet full of Beanie Babies in little plastic cases, I'm sorry. Give them to the grandkids, or donate them, and enjoy the closet space.
Number six. Encyclopedias and old book sets. The leather-looking set of encyclopedias, the Reader's Digest condensed books, the matching set of classics with the gold lettering. I watch families carry these out to my sale tables, box after box, and I have to tell them nobody's going to buy them. Most book dealers won't take them. Many donation centers won't either.
There are exceptions. Very old books, first editions of famous novels, signed books, and some old children's books can be valuable. If you've got a book that looks really old, or has an author's signature, look it up. But the matching sets from the seventies and eighties? Recycle them, or use them to prop up a wobbly table like I do.
Number seven. The old upright piano. This one hurts, because a piano feels like it must be worth something. It's big, it's heavy, it's made of real wood, and somebody paid a lot of money for it once.
But today, most old upright pianos have little or no value. Many piano movers will tell you the same thing. Families often have to pay someone to haul them away. A free piano listed online can sit there for weeks. If your piano is a famous brand and in great shape, have a piano technician look at it, because a few are worth something. But most of the time, the kindest thing you can do is find a church, a school, or a young family who will play it, and let them have it.
Number eight. National Geographic magazines. The yellow spines lined up on the bookshelf, decades of them. People keep them because they're beautiful and it feels wrong to throw them away. But they printed millions of copies of every issue. Most sell for a dollar or less each, and big collections often don't sell at all. A few very early issues are different, but the ones from the sixties onward almost never are.
Number nine. Collector plates. The ones that came in the mail every month, with a painting of a lighthouse, or Elvis, or a little girl with a puppy, and a certificate saying it was a limited edition. Here's the thing about limited editions. They were limited to however many people ordered them, and a lot of people ordered them. Most collector plates sell for a few dollars, and many don't sell at all. The certificate doesn't make it worth more.
Before I get to number ten, let me answer three more that I get asked about every single week.
Depression glass. The pretty pink and green glass from the nineteen thirties, often given away free in boxes of oatmeal or at the movies. Most common pieces sell for five to twenty five dollars. Some rare colors and patterns, and complete sets of rare patterns, can bring more. And watch out, because a lot of reproductions were made later. Real old Depression glass often has tiny bubbles and slight wobbles in it. If it looks too perfect and too heavy, it may be newer.
Costume jewelry. The big rhinestone brooches and the clip-on earrings in grandma's jewelry box. Most of it sells for a few dollars a piece. But some signed pieces, with a maker's name stamped on the back of the clasp, can sell for real money. So turn every piece over and look for a name or a mark before you sell the whole box for ten dollars. And always pull out anything that might be real gold or silver. Look for tiny stamps like fourteen K or sterling or nine twenty five. Those sell by weight.
Old quilts. This is one where it depends. A machine made quilt from a store, in a common pattern, often sells for not very much. But an old hand stitched quilt, with tiny even stitches, in good condition, made by someone with real skill, can sell for hundreds. Look at the back. If you can see thousands of tiny hand stitches, that's somebody's winter of work. Even if you don't sell it, take care of it. Keep it out of the sun, and never store it in a plastic bag where it can't breathe.
And while we're at it, sewing machines. If you watched my last video, you know most old Singers sell for forty to a hundred and fifty dollars, but the little Featherweights and the pale green ones can sell for a lot more. Read the serial number before you sell.
So that's nine things families think are valuable that usually aren't. Now let me tell you about the tenth thing, the one I promised. The surprise.
Number ten. The furniture nobody wanted. In that woman's house, out in the garage, there was a long, low wooden dresser with skinny tapered legs, and a matching pair of side tables. Her mother had bought them in the nineteen sixties. They were scratched and dusty, and she was going to give them to the neighbor's son for his first apartment.
That was mid-century modern furniture. Simple, clean lines, often made of teak or walnut. Some of it is Danish, some of it is American made by companies like Lane, Broyhill, and Drexel. Young people today love it. They decorate their apartments with it. And good pieces in decent shape sell for hundreds, sometimes thousands of dollars.
Her dresser and side tables, cleaned up and photographed in good light, sold for more than everything in the china cabinet put together. The Hummels, the Waterford, the china and the Beanie Babies. Added up.
So here's the lesson. What was valuable to our parents isn't always what's valuable today. And sometimes, the thing they thought was ordinary is the thing people are looking for now.
Let me give you a few more things that do sell, so you know what to look for when you go through a house. Cast iron skillets, especially old ones with smooth insides and makers' names on the bottom. Old Pyrex in bright colors and rare patterns, like we talked about. Sterling silver, which sells by weight even if nobody wants the pattern. Gold jewelry, even broken pieces, for the same reason. Old video games and game systems in good condition. Vintage denim jeans and old concert t-shirts. Old tools, especially hand planes and good wrenches. And mid-century modern anything. Lamps, chairs, coffee tables.
And here's what I always tell families. Don't decide what something's worth by how old it is, or how much it cost, or how much your mother loved it. Look at the sold listings. The market tells you what people want right now.
Now, I know what some of you are feeling, because I've watched families go through it. It feels like you're letting your parents down if you donate the Hummels or give away the piano. So let me say this, as someone who's helped hundreds of families through it.
Your mother didn't buy those Hummels as an investment. She bought them because they made her smile. She bought that china because she wanted a beautiful table for her family at Thanksgiving. The value was never really the money. It was the life she made with them.
So keep one or two pieces that mean something to you. One teacup, one figurine, one plate. Put it somewhere you'll see it. And let the rest go to someone who'll enjoy it, without guilt. That's what she'd want.
And if you need to sell, here's where. China and crystal, try the replacement companies first, then local online marketplaces. Hummels and figurines, sell them as a group on an online auction site, or to a local consignment shop. Books, a local used book store might take the good ones. Furniture, especially mid-century, list it on a local marketplace with good photos in daylight, and price it after you check what similar pieces really sold for. You'll be surprised how fast the good stuff goes.
And a word about selling online, because it makes a big difference in the price. Take your pictures in daylight, near a window, not under a yellow kitchen light. Put the piece on a plain background, a white sheet works fine. Take a picture of every side, the bottom, any marks or signatures, and any chips or cracks. Be honest about the damage. Buyers forgive a chip they knew about, but they send back the one you didn't mention. Write down the measurements. And in the title, use the words a buyer would type, like the maker's name and the pattern, not just old vase. A good listing with clear photos can sell for twice what a blurry one does.
And if all this sounds like too much, that's what estate sale companies are for. A good one will sort, price and sell a whole house for a share of the money. Get two or three to come look, ask what they charge, and ask what happens to the things that don't sell.
And before you donate anything, open every drawer, every box, every book. I've found cash in books, jewelry in candy tins, and savings bonds in the back of china cabinet drawers. Look before you let it go.
