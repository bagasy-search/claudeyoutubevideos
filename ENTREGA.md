🍲 POTATOES THROUGH THE WINTER ($0) — page 11 of the book
• Potatoes dug at least 2 weeks after the vines died, or bought in big sacks
• A dark, airy spot at 50 – 60°F for curing
• Wooden crates, burlap or paper sacks
• Newspaper or a burlap cover
1. Do not wash them. Brush off loose dirt only.
2. Cure 1 to 2 weeks in the dark at 50 – 60°F with good air. Small cuts heal and the skin toughens.
3. Sort: eat the cut, bruised or green ones first. Store only perfect potatoes.
4. Put them in crates no more than 2 or 3 layers deep and cover against light.
5. Keep at 38 – 45°F. Colder than 38°F turns them sweet; warmer than 50°F makes them sprout.
6. Every 2 or 3 weeks, check and remove any soft or sprouting ones.
Ole's trick: Put one apple in a crate of potatoes only if you WANT them to sprout for seed potatoes in spring. Otherwise keep fruit far away.

🍲 CARROTS, BEETS & PARSNIPS IN DAMP SAND ($5 for sand) — page 13 of the book
• Fresh, unwashed roots with no cracks or soft spots
• Clean play sand, peat moss or sawdust
• Wooden boxes, plastic tubs or buckets
• A spray bottle of water
1. Twist or cut the greens off, leaving 1/2 inch of stem. Greens left on pull water out of the root.
2. Brush off loose dirt. Do not wash: the skin protects them.
3. Dampen the sand until it barely holds together when squeezed, like a wrung-out sponge.
4. Put 2 inches of sand in the box, then a layer of roots not touching, then sand to cover. Repeat.
5. Keep the box in the coldest, moistest part of the cellar.
6. Take from the top. If the sand dries out over winter, mist it lightly.
Ole's trick: Stand the carrots upright in a bucket of sand, the way they grew. They take up less room and you can pull one without digging.

🍲 APPLES & PEARS IN THEIR OWN ROOM ($0) — page 15 of the book
• Late keeping apples (Fuji, Granny Smith, Northern Spy, Winesap, Rome) with no bruises
• Newspaper or tissue squares
• Shallow boxes or crates
• A separate cool room, porch or the far end of the cellar
1. Pick or buy apples that are firm and unbruised. Handle them like eggs.
2. Wrap each apple in a square of newspaper.
3. Lay them in a single or double layer in shallow boxes.
4. Keep them as cold as possible without freezing, away from potatoes and carrots.
5. Check every 2 weeks and use the ones that soften first.
Ole's trick: One bad apple really does spoil the barrel: the gas from a rotting apple ripens its neighbors fast. The paper keeps each one in its own little room.

⚠️ Safety: never keep garlic or herbs in oil at room temperature; never can without a tested recipe (USDA / NCHFP); pickled eggs and cooked food stay in the refrigerator; if it looks or smells wrong, throw it out without tasting.
