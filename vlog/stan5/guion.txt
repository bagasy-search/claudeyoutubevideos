[ONE] Trick number one. The meat. Never make meatballs with only lean beef. Lean beef makes a hard, dry meatball, every time. What you want is a mix. Two parts beef chuck, eighty twenty, and one part pork shoulder. The beef gives you the flavor, and the pork gives you the softness and the juice. Mrs. Lombardi used a little veal too, but veal is expensive now, and pork does the same job for half the money.
[ONE] Why does it matter so much? Because a meatball is just ground meat with nowhere to hide. A steak has its own fat running through the muscle. A meatball only has the fat you give it. Twenty percent fat is not too much. It melts while the meatball cooks, it keeps every bite wet, and most of it ends up in the sauce, which is exactly where you want it.
[ONE] And here's the sentence for the counter. You say, can you grind me two pounds of chuck and one pound of pork shoulder together, one time through? Together, so it's already mixed by the grinder. And one time through, not twice. Ground twice, the meat turns into paste, and paste makes a dense, heavy meatball. Once through the coarse plate. That's how we did it.
[ONE] At the store, if you can't get it ground, you buy one package of ground beef and one of ground pork, and you mix them with a fork, gently. Same idea. Two to one. Write that down. Two beef, one pork.
[TWO] Trick number two. Your hands. This is the one that ruins more meatballs than anything else. People squeeze. They grab the meat in a fist and they knead it like bread dough, and they roll each ball hard between their palms, round and tight. And the harder you squeeze, the harder the meatball. You're pushing all the air out, and you're turning the meat into sausage.
[TWO] Mrs. Lombardi mixed with her fingertips. Like she was tossing a salad. Just until everything came together, about one minute, no more. Then she wet her hands with cold water, and she rolled every ball with an open palm, light, like she was holding an egg. Cold, wet, gentle. When I learned that, our meatballs went from good to the best on the South Side.
[TWO] And keep everything cold. The meat, the bowl, your hands. Warm fat smears, and a smeared meatball turns tough in the pan. In the summer we put the steel bowl in the cooler for ten minutes before we started. You can put yours in the freezer for five.
[TWO] Here's how you know you mixed it enough. Pick up a little of the mix and press it between your fingers. If it holds together and feels tacky, like the back of a sticker, stop. That's it. If you can still see separate pieces of beef and pork, give it ten more seconds. If it feels smooth and shiny like dough, you went too far, and the only thing you can do is be gentler with the next batch.
[THREE] Trick number three. The wet bread. Not dry breadcrumbs from a can. Those suck the juice right out of the meat. You take two thick slices of plain white bread, no crust, and you tear them up and soak them in half a cup of whole milk for five minutes, until it's soft like oatmeal. Then you mash it with a fork, and that goes into the meat.
[THREE] That bread holds on to the juice while the meatball cooks. It's like a little sponge inside every one. And for two pounds of meat, Mrs. Lombardi added one more thing. Half a cup of ricotta cheese. Not for the taste. For the softness. A meatball with ricotta in it, you can cut it with the side of a fork. I promise you, once you try it, you'll never go back.
[THREE] And two eggs for every three pounds. Not more. Too many eggs and they go spongy and bounce. The eggs hold it together, the bread keeps it soft. That's the balance.
[FOUR] Trick number four. The test meatball. This is the one that saves a whole batch, and almost nobody does it. You can't taste raw meat, so how do you know if you put enough salt in? You don't. So before you roll a single meatball, you pinch off a little piece, the size of a quarter, flatten it, and you fry it in a pan for one minute on each side. Then you eat it.
[FOUR] Too bland? Add salt. Not enough garlic? Add garlic. Then you fry another little one. It takes three minutes, and it's the difference between a meatball people remember and one they leave on the plate. In the shop, I fried a test piece from every batch for thirty years. My lunch, most days, was test meatballs.
[FOUR] And while we're talking about flavor, grate your onion and your garlic. Don't chop them. Chopped onion makes little pockets, and the meatball cracks open in the sauce right where the onion is. Grated, it melts into the meat. One small onion, grated, two cloves of garlic on the small holes, a big handful of grated hard cheese, a handful of chopped parsley, one and a half teaspoons of salt per pound. That's the shop recipe.
[FIVE] Trick number five. Every one the same size. When some are big and some are small, the small ones dry out before the big ones are cooked in the middle. So we used an ice cream scoop. Two ounces of meat, every time. About the size of a golf ball. Scoop, roll gently with wet hands, set it on the tray. It's faster than doing it by eye, and every meatball cooks the same.
[FIVE] Two ounces, that's eight meatballs to the pound. Three pounds of meat, twenty-four meatballs. On a Friday morning, I could roll a thousand of them before the doors opened. My wife used to say I had meatball hands.
[FIVE] And one more thing before they go in the oven. Put the whole tray of rolled meatballs in the refrigerator for twenty minutes. Just twenty. The fat gets firm again after all that handling, the bread settles, and the meatballs hold their shape in the heat instead of slumping into little flat pancakes. In the shop, the trays went in the walk-in cooler while we washed the grinder. By the time the grinder was clean, the meatballs were ready.
[SIX] Trick number six. Don't fry them. I know, your grandmother fried them. Mine too. But when you're cooking two hundred pounds, you don't stand at a frying pan all day. So we baked them, and the customers said they were better. A sheet pan, a little oil, the meatballs in rows with a finger of space between them. A hot oven, four hundred twenty-five degrees, for about fifteen minutes.
[SIX] They come out brown on the outside, and still a little pink in the middle. That's what you want. They don't fall apart, because you never had to turn them with a fork. They keep their round shape. And you didn't splash grease all over the stove. Fifteen minutes, one pan, two dozen meatballs, all the same.
[SIX] Now, if you only make a dozen and you love the frying pan, go ahead and fry them. But do it right. A heavy iron skillet, a thin layer of oil, medium heat, not high. Don't crowd the pan, leave a finger of space, and turn them only when they let go of the pan by themselves. If they stick, they're not ready. Brown them on three sides, not all the way through, and then into the sauce they go, exactly like the baked ones.
[SEVEN] Trick number seven. The last step, the one my customers didn't believe. You take those meatballs, still pink in the middle, and you drop them into the sauce, and you let them finish cooking in there, slow. A low simmer, little bubbles, never a hard boil. Thirty minutes. The meatball gives its flavor to the sauce, and the sauce gives its flavor to the meatball.
[SEVEN] A hard boil makes them tough and it breaks them apart. A low simmer makes them soft all the way through. And here's the part they never believed. Turn off the heat and let them sit in the sauce for another twenty minutes before you serve them. With the lid on. They soak up the sauce like that bread soaked up the milk. And if you can wait until tomorrow, they're even better.
[SEVEN] And the sauce doesn't need to be anything special. Mrs. Lombardi's was one big can of crushed tomatoes, a little olive oil, two cloves of garlic, a pinch of salt, and a few leaves of basil if she had them. She said, the meatballs make the sauce, Stanley, not the other way around. Thirty minutes with the meatballs in it, and that plain can of tomatoes tastes like it cooked all day.
[TROUBLE] Now, if your meatballs go wrong, here's what it was, and it isn't you. If they came out hard and bouncy, you squeezed them, you mixed them too long, or you used meat that was too lean. Fingertips, one minute, eighty twenty. If they fell apart in the sauce, the sauce was boiling, or there was no egg, or the meat was ground twice and turned to paste.
[TROUBLE] If they were dry, you used dry breadcrumbs instead of wet bread, or you cooked the small ones as long as the big ones. Use the scoop. If they tasted flat, you skipped the test meatball. And if they cracked open, you chopped the onion instead of grating it. Every one of those, I've seen across my counter, and every one has the same easy fix.
