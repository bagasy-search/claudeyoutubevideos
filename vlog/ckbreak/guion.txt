[P6] Number six. Bake Sale Banana Bread, page fifty-eight. This was always the first thing gone at our bake sale table, before the pies, before the cookies. Bernice used to hide one loaf under the table for the pastor, because he never got there in time.
[P7] Here's what you need. Three very ripe bananas, mashed, about a cup and a half. A third of a cup of butter, melted. Three quarters of a cup of brown sugar, packed. One egg and a teaspoon of vanilla. A teaspoon of baking soda, a quarter teaspoon of salt and half a teaspoon of cinnamon. A cup and a half of flour, half a cup of chopped walnuts if you like them, and one tablespoon of sugar for the top.
[P8] Heat your oven to three fifty, and butter and flour a loaf pan. Mash the bananas right in the bowl with a fork, and stir in the melted butter. Then the brown sugar, the egg and the vanilla. Sprinkle the soda, the salt and the cinnamon over the top and stir, then fold in the flour just until the streaks are gone. Pour it in the pan, smooth it, and sprinkle that tablespoon of sugar on top. That's what gives you the crackly crust. Bake it fifty-five to sixty-five minutes, until a toothpick in the middle comes out clean.
[P9] And here's the trick. Wait until the bananas are black. Not spotted, honey. Black. My mother used to say, yellow bananas make bread, black bananas make banana bread. The sweetness is all in there when they go dark, and the loaf comes out sweet and moist.
[P10] The mistake I see the most is stirring it too much. If you beat it like a cake, you get a tough loaf with tunnels all through it. Fold it just until it comes together. And if the top is getting dark at forty minutes and the middle is still raw, lay a piece of foil over it and let it finish.
[P11] If it's just you, slice the whole loaf when it's cool and freeze the slices. In the morning you put one slice straight in the toaster, frozen, and it tastes like you baked it that day.
[P12] Number five. Irene's Potato-Water Dinner Rolls, page fifty-six. Irene made rolls in our church kitchen two hundred at a time. For two hundred rolls we used a dishpan, and three women stood side by side kneading. I was the youngest, so I got the end of the table and the sore arms.
[P13] Irene's secret was the water she boiled her potatoes in. Everybody else poured it down the sink. She saved it. Here's how. Boil one medium russet potato, peeled and cubed, in about two cups of water until it's soft, fifteen to twenty minutes. Save that water. Mash the potato smooth and measure half a cup. Then cool half a cup of the potato water to a hundred and ten degrees. Warm, like a baby's bath.
[P14] Sprinkle a packet of yeast, that's two and a quarter teaspoons, and a pinch of sugar over the warm potato water, and wait ten minutes. It should foam up like the head on a root beer. If it doesn't, your yeast is old. Get new yeast. Warm a cup of milk with a third of a cup of butter and a third of a cup of sugar until the butter melts, then let it cool to warm.
[P15] In a big bowl, stir the yeast, the milk, one egg, the mashed potato and a teaspoon and a half of salt. Then add the flour a cup at a time, four and a half to five cups, until a soft dough pulls away from the bowl. Knead it eight minutes, cover it, and let it rise until it doubles, about an hour. Punch it down, cut it in twenty-four pieces, and roll each one into a smooth ball.
[P16] Now set them touching in two buttered nine by thirteen pans. Touching, honey. If you space them apart, you get a hard crust all the way around. Let them rise forty-five minutes, and bake at three seventy-five for eighteen to twenty minutes, then brush them with butter while they're hot. The potato water and that little bit of mashed potato feed the yeast and hold the moisture, so the rolls come out pillowy, and they stay soft for two days. And the number one reason rolls never rise? The liquid was too hot, and it killed the yeast. A hundred and ten. Warm, not hot.
[P17] Living alone? Bake all twenty-four, let them cool, and freeze them in bags of four. Wrap a few in foil and warm them at three hundred for ten minutes. Supper rolls all month from one afternoon.
[P18] Number four. Fellowship Hour Cinnamon Rolls, page fifty-seven. These came out of the oven during the last hymn, and you could smell them clear up in the sanctuary. More than one sermon got shorter because of these rolls, I'll tell you that.
[P19] You start with one batch of Irene's dough, right after the first rise. Roll it out on a floured board to eighteen by twelve inches. Spread half a cup of very soft butter all the way to the edges. Mix a cup of packed brown sugar with two tablespoons of cinnamon, sprinkle it evenly, and press it in. Roll it up tight from the long side, and pinch the seam.
[P20] Now cut fifteen slices, and use a piece of thread, or dental floss, not a knife. Slide it under, cross it over the top, and pull. A knife squashes them flat. Set the slices in a buttered nine by thirteen, and let them rise until they're puffy and touching, forty-five minutes to an hour.
[P21] Here is the trick, and it's a good one. Right before they go in the oven, pour half a cup of warm heavy cream over the risen rolls. It soaks into the bottoms and makes them gooey, without a sticky pan to scrub. Bake at three fifty for twenty-two to twenty-five minutes, until golden. Pull them at golden. If you wait for brown, they're dry.
[P22] For the glaze, beat four ounces of soft cream cheese, two tablespoons of soft butter, a cup and a half of powdered sugar, a teaspoon of vanilla and two or three tablespoons of milk, and spread it on while they're warm. For the church, we made them the night before and let them rise on the counter in the morning. For one or two, bake them, cool them, and freeze them without the glaze. Warm one up and glaze it fresh.

[P26] Number three. Overnight Church Breakfast Egg Bake, page sixty. You put it together Saturday night, and Sunday morning it goes in the oven while you get dressed for church. For the men's breakfast we made two pans for twenty-four.
[P27] Brown a pound of pork sausage and drain it. Butter a nine by thirteen. Cube eight slices of bread, about six cups, and spread them in the pan. Then the sausage, half a cup of sliced green onion, and two cups of shredded cheddar. [P28] Now press the bread down so every piece gets wet, cover it, and put it in the refrigerator overnight. That's the trick. The overnight soak. The bread drinks up that custard, so it bakes up light, like a soufflé, instead of wet bread on the bottom and dry bread on top.
[P29] In the morning, set it on the counter thirty minutes while your oven heats to three fifty. Don't skip that. If it goes in cold from the fridge, the middle stays runny. [P39] In the hard years, my mother could feed the six of us for a whole week on one sack of cornmeal. We just knew it smelled good in that kitchen on a cold morning.
[P40] You need one cup of yellow cornmeal, one cup of cold water, three cups of boiling water, a teaspoon of salt and a tablespoon of butter. Then a quarter cup of flour for dredging, three tablespoons of butter or bacon fat for frying, and maple syrup or warm sorghum to serve.
[P41] Here's the trick, and it's my mother's. Stir the cornmeal into the cold water first, until it's smooth. Then whisk it slowly into the boiling salted water. If you dump dry cornmeal into boiling water, you get lumps, every time. Start it cold, and you never have a lump again.
[P42] Cook it on low, stirring often, twenty to twenty-five minutes, until it's very thick. Stir in the butter. Pour it into a buttered loaf pan, let it cool, cover it, and put it in the refrigerator overnight until it's firm. If you rush it, it falls apart in the pan. Overnight.
[P43] In the morning, turn it out and cut half-inch slices. Dredge them lightly in flour. Heat the butter or bacon fat in a cast iron skillet until it shimmers, and fry the slices four to five minutes on each side, until they're deep golden and crisp. If the fat isn't hot, they soak it up and get greasy. Serve them hot with syrup, or with eggs and sausage, like the men did.
