I cooked those suppers, ya know, forty men at a time. So pull up a chair, friend. Here are thirty logging camp suppers, counting down to the one every man fought over. And that number one? Well, it cost next to nothing, and it took all day.
[INTRO|] Now, I'm Ole. I started as a cookee at fifteen, peeling potatoes before the sun came up, and I stayed in those kitchens for forty years. A logger burned something like five thousand calories a day, and it was the cook's job to put them all back. The cook's day started at four in the morning. His crew was three or four cookees, and a wood range the size of a small truck. The men came in off the job at dusk, hung their wet wool over the stove, and sat down at long tables, about twenty to a table, on benches with no backs.
[INTRO|] And here's the rule that surprises everybody. Nobody talked. No talking at the table. You could ask for the beans, or the bread, or the potatoes, and that was all. The old cooks said a man with his mouth open to talk was a man not eating, and there was a lot of work to do tomorrow. The historical societies say the same thing, so it isn't just an old cook's story.

[INTRO|] And there was coffee. Always coffee. The old boys said a good cup of camp coffee was one that could float a horseshoe, and I never once saw a cook argue with them. The pot sat at the back of the range from four in the morning until the last man went to bed. Boil the grounds, drop in a splash of cold water to settle them, and pour. That's it. That was the whole trick, and every man who ever sat at that table remembers that smell.

[Nthirty|] Alright. Number thirty. Fried cornmeal mush with molasses. This was the bottom of the menu, and every man knew it. The cook boiled coarse cornmeal in salted water until a spoon could stand up in it, poured it into a pan, and let it set overnight. At supper it came back sliced thick, fried in pork fat till the edges crackled, and drowned in molasses. Cheap, hot, and it filled a corner. Now, I'll tell you what the cookees did with the leftover mush. They fried it again for breakfast. Nothing on that table ever got thrown out, ya know, not a crumb, and the cook knew the price of every pound of meal in the barrel.
[Ntwenty-nine|] Number twenty-nine. Salt pork and boiled potatoes in cream gravy. Salt pork was the meat that never spoiled. It sat in a barrel in the cold shed all winter. You'd slice it, fry it slow until it gave up its fat, and then you'd stir flour into that fat, and pour in milk. That's the gravy. Poured over boiled potatoes. That's a whole supper for two cents, ya know. And there was a second job for the fat left in the pan. The cook saved every spoon of it in a crock by the stove, and it went into the next day's biscuits and the next night's fried potatoes. In a camp kitchen, fat was money, and a good cook counted it like a banker.
[Ntwenty-eight|] Number twenty-eight. Skillet cabbage with bacon. Cabbage kept in a root cellar when nothing else did. The trick is to let the bacon get crisp first, then throw in the cabbage in a hot pan and leave it alone, so the edges brown. Stir it too much and it steams and goes gray. Nobody wants gray cabbage. The old fellows liked it with a splash of vinegar at the very end, right in the pan. Sharp, and salty, and a little sweet from the bacon. It cut through all that rich food the way a cold wind cuts through a coat.
[Ntwenty-seven|] Number twenty-seven. A big bowl of oatmeal with dried fruit, and yes, at supper. Oatmeal porridge shows up in the old camp records at breakfast, but a cook with dried apples or raisins on the shelf would stew them right into the pot on a slow night. Sweet, warm, and it stuck to a man's ribs. A camp cook always figured a man would want something in his belly that would still be there at midnight, and oatmeal with fruit did that better than almost anything, for almost nothing.
[Ntwenty-six|] Number twenty-six. Fried potatoes and onions. Here's one small thing. Rinse the sliced potatoes in cold water and pat them dry. Do that, and they turn crisp. Skip it, and you get glue. Hot fat, big pan, and don't crowd it. And salt them near the end, not at the start. Salt at the start draws the water out, and you're steaming your potatoes instead of frying them. Little things like that. A cook knew a hundred little things.
[Ntwenty-five|] Number twenty-five. Molasses cookies. The camp cook baked them by the sheet pan, and the cookee carried them out to the men on the job in a sack. Big, soft, and spiced with ginger. A man could put three in his coat pocket and forget he was cold for a while. And the cookies were the one thing a cookee could sneak. Every cookee I ever knew had a cookie in his apron pocket, and the cook always knew, and the cook never said a word. He'd done the same at fifteen.
[Ntwenty-four|] Number twenty-four. Vinegar pie. Now this sounds strange, but when the apples ran out and the lemons never came, the cook made a pie with vinegar, sugar, eggs and butter. It tastes like a lemon custard, if you close your eyes. That is the whole idea of camp cooking. You make do, and you make it good. Lemon extract, when the cook could get it, but most of the time it was just vinegar, and cider vinegar at that. Bake it in a lard crust till the top is a little brown and the middle still wobbles like a custard should.
[Ntwenty-three|] Number twenty-three. Liver and onions. When a beef was butchered for the camp, the liver went on the table that same night, because it doesn't keep. Slice it thin, fry it hot and fast, and never, ever cook it long. Overcooked liver is a boot. And sweet onions in the same pan, well, those are half the reason men ate it. One more thing about liver. If you soak it in milk for half an hour, it loses that hard edge some men can't stand. I learned that from a cook who'd been feeding loggers since before the first world war, and he said his liver never came back to the kitchen on a plate.
[Ntwenty-two|] Number twenty-two. Rice pudding. Rice, milk, sugar, a little cinnamon, and a slow oven. It was the softest thing on the table, and I'll tell you a secret. The old teamsters, the men who drove the horses, loved it more than the young fellows did. Their teeth weren't what they used to be. Grate a little nutmeg on the top just before it goes in. Not too much. A cook who used too much nutmeg heard about it for a week.
[Ntwenty-one|] Number twenty-one. Red flannel hash. Leftover corned beef, potatoes, and beets, chopped together and fried in one pan. The beets turn the whole pan the color of a red flannel shirt, which is exactly how it got the name. The whole thing was made from Sunday's leftovers, so it cost the camp almost nothing. And a word about that dish, friend, because you'll hear it called something else too. The men had a name for creamed chipped beef on toast that I won't say on the air. Let's just say it involved a shingle. But that's a different supper, and a different kitchen.
[Ntwenty|] Number twenty. Sauerkraut and sausage, in a skillet. Sauerkraut was cabbage put up in a crock in the fall, and it was one of the few things in the woods that had vitamin C in the dead of winter, though the men just called it good. Brown the sausage first, then let the kraut cook down in the fat. That's the supper. Some folks rinse the kraut first, if it's too sharp. I don't. Sharp is the point, and the fat from the sausage takes care of the rest. Serve it with boiled potatoes and a piece of dark bread, and you have a supper that would carry a man through a Minnesota night.
[Nnineteen|] Number nineteen. Doughnuts. The cook fried them in lard by the hundred. The historical records say doughnuts, pies, cakes and cookies were always on hand in the camps. A cook could not let the cake box go empty. And here's the thing, a doughnut in a coat pocket at five in the morning, at twenty below, is the best thing in the world. There's a reason the lard has to be hot. If the fat is too cool, the doughnut soaks it up and turns heavy as a stone. Too hot, and the outside burns before the inside is done. A good cook tested it with a scrap of dough. If it sizzled and floated right up, it was ready. No thermometer in those days. Just your eyes, and forty years of practice.
[Nnineteen|] Now, before we go on, I want to stop a minute. You're at number nineteen, and you have eighteen more coming. But the one at number four, well, that's the one they fought over, and I promise you'll want to see how they got five hundred of them out of that kitchen. So stay with me.
[Neighteen|] Number eighteen. Fish chowder with salt pork. In the camps near a lake, a fisherman would bring in the catch, and the cook would render salt pork, add onions, potatoes, and milk, and lay the fish on top for the last few minutes. Don't stir it much. The fish falls apart on its own. The cook wouldn't put in a drop of water. Just the milk and the fat, which is why it tastes so rich. And crackers on the side, always crackers, crushed right over the bowl.
[Nseventeen|] Number seventeen. Bread pudding with molasses sauce. Stale bread was never thrown away in a camp kitchen. Never. It went into a pan with milk, eggs, and a handful of raisins, and came out of the oven like something worth saving. The molasses sauce goes over it hot. And the raisins should soak in hot water first, so they plump up, instead of stealing the moisture from your pudding. Little thing. Big difference.
[Nsixteen|] Number sixteen. Corned beef hash. Get the potatoes cooked first, chop the beef fine, and press the whole thing down flat in a hot skillet. Then leave it. Don't touch it. When the bottom is dark brown and crisp, flip it in sections. Patience is the whole secret. A cook I knew in Wisconsin, a big quiet fellow, used to say that hash is a dish you can't hurry, and neither can you hurry a man who's worth his salt. I thought about that for years.
[Nfifteen|] Number fifteen. Prune pie. Sounds funny to you, I know. But dried prunes were a staple, and a pie made from them with a little sugar and lemon was a treat that men talked about for a week. Dried fruit was how a camp got through a winter, ya know. Simmer them soft with a strip of lemon peel, if you have any, and then pit them by hand. Nobody wants a tooth cracked on the last bite of pie.
[Nfourteen|] Number fourteen. Boiled dinner. Now we're at the first big one, so I'll slow down. Sunday was boiled dinner in a lot of the camps I knew. A whole corned beef went into the biggest kettle at first light, with water to cover and nothing else. About three hours in, the cook added cabbage, potatoes, carrots, turnips. Everything cooked together in that one salty broth. The vegetables get their flavor from the beef, and the beef gets sweetness from the vegetables. And then here's what the cook would do with the leftovers. Tomorrow's red flannel hash. So nothing in that kitchen ever went to waste.
[Nfourteen|] The trick for boiled dinner is to never let it boil hard. A hard boil toughens the beef and turns the vegetables to mush. You want the water just trembling. Like a whisper. And you add the vegetables in order of how long they take, so they all come out right at the same minute. Carrots and potatoes early, cabbage last. Twenty minutes for the cabbage, no more.
[Nthirteen|] Number thirteen. Tourtiere. This is a French Canadian meat pie, pork and onion and spices, and it was a favorite in the Maine and Quebec camps, a traditional Christmas dish, and the whole camp wanted a slice. A little clove, a little cinnamon, and a flaky lard crust. Lard makes the crust, and nothing else does it quite the same. Butter is lovely, but lard gives you those flakes that shatter when you cut in with a fork. And cold. Everything cold. The lard, the water, even the flour if you can manage it.
[Ntwelve|] Number twelve. Sourdough biscuits. This one is a hero. Every camp cook had a crock of sourdough starter, and he guarded it like it was money. The starter lived by the stove where it stayed warm, and if the fire died overnight and the crock froze, well, that cook had a very bad morning. You mix the starter with flour, a little baking soda, and a little sugar, and you roll it thick. Bake it hot, in a heavy pan, with the biscuits touching. They rise up together. The recipe for a camp sourdough starter and a loaf is in the book, page twenty-six. And here's something the old cooks knew. The sour in the starter makes the biscuit taste like it has butter in it, even when there wasn't any butter.
[Neleven|] Number eleven. Pot roast with root vegetables. A chuck roast, browned hard in a big pot, with onions, carrots, and turnips packed around it. Then a cup of black coffee goes in the pot, and I'll tell you, the gravy comes out dark and rich, and you will never taste the coffee. That trick came from an old cook, and I've used it ever since.
[Nten|] Number ten. Dried apple pie. Dried apples were soaked and simmered until soft, then sweetened with molasses or sugar and spiced with cinnamon, and baked under a lard crust. When fresh apples were gone, this was the pie. In the winter you didn't get fresh anything, so you learned to love what dried well.
[Nnine|] Number nine. Ham hock and navy bean soup. The day after the beans, or the day after the ham, the cook made soup from the bones. A ham hock in a pot of water, beans added, and left to simmer half the afternoon. Nothing wasted. The next day, the soup had more flavor than the first.
[Nnine|] Stay with me, friend, we're past the halfway mark now. And coming up at number five is the stew I'd cook on the coldest night of the year, the one the men would ask for by name. But first.
[Neight|] Number eight. Roast pork with mashed turnips. A camp menu from Maine, from nineteen twenty-three, lists roast pork loin with mashed turnips, green peas, gravy, baked beans, and yeast bread, all on one dinner. Now that's a lot of plate. Roast the pork with the fat side up so the fat bastes the meat, and mash the turnips with butter, salt and pepper.
[Nseven|] Number seven. Skillet cornbread. Cornbread was the quick bread. Biscuits had to rise, but cornbread just needed a hot cast iron pan with fat smoking in it, and batter poured in so it sizzled. That sizzle is your crust. It comes out in about twenty minutes, and it's good with beans, or with milk poured over it.
[Nsix|] Number six. The stock kettle. This is the pot that never stopped. Every bone from every meal went into it, with onion skins and a bay leaf, and it simmered at the back of the range from Monday to Saturday. Whatever the cook needed for soup, gravy or stew, he dipped from that kettle. Best pot in the kitchen.
[Nfive|] Number five. Now we're into the heavy hitters. Lumberjack beef and barley stew. This is the supper I'd cook on the coldest night of the year. Beef chuck, cut in big chunks and browned a few pieces at a time, because if you crowd the pot it steams instead of browning. Then onions, carrots, and pearl barley, cooked in beef broth for two hours or more. The barley thickens the whole pot until it eats like a meal, not a soup. It's on page twenty-nine. And ya know what makes it special? The men didn't get bored of it, because the cook changed it a little every time. Some nights a turnip. Some nights a parsnip. The bones stayed the same.
[Nfour|] Number four. Flapjacks at supper. I promised you. In a camp of eighty men, the cook might make four or five hundred pancakes in one meal. That's right, four hundred to five hundred. One griddle wasn't enough. The cook had a griddle the size of a door, and the cookees flipped them with long flat paddles. Buttermilk batter, a little baking soda, and a lot of patience. The batter has to rest ten minutes so the bubbles come up. And, ya know, a stack of them, with salt pork and molasses at eight o'clock at night, was a supper a man never forgot.

[Nthree|] Number three. Split pea soup with smoked ham. Peas and beans were what the camp owners stocked, along with flour, sugar and salt. Green peas simmered with a ham bone, onion, carrot, and a pinch of thyme, and it turned thick enough to hold a spoon standing up. It's a cheap soup. The dried peas cost nothing. The ham bone gave it taste. You stir it now and then so the bottom doesn't burn, because the pea soup burns on the bottom before you know it.
[Ntwo|] Number two. Pot beans and salt pork. The old records say pork and beans were the staple, and sometimes they were served three times a day. Three times! No wonder the cookees learned to cook beans by the barrel. Beans, a big chunk of salt pork, an onion, salt from the very start, and a boil for the first ten minutes, and then down to a whisper for hours. That method is on page seven of the book. I could cook you a good bean in my sleep, friend.
[Ntwo|] And now, the thing you've waited for. Number one. Beanhole beans.
[Ntwo|] Here's how it worked. The cook dug a hole in the ground, deep enough to swallow a big iron pot. He built a fire in it, and let it burn down to hot coals, a bed of them. Then a big cast iron pot, with a lid, full of beans that had been parboiled first, with salt pork, onion, molasses, and a little mustard, got set right down on those coals. The cook covered it with more coals, and then dirt, and he left it there. That was that. No stirring. No checking. You left it, the whole night, or the whole day, and you came back and dug it up.
[Ntwo|] And what came out of that hole, friend, was the best thing a man ever put in his mouth. Beans so tender they melted, in a sauce dark as tar, with the salt pork gone soft and sweet. The camp cooks I knew never wrote down a recipe. They just knew the beans by the smell. And those beans are the reason men came back to a camp, year after year, because a man will forgive a lot of things, but he won't forgive a bad bean.
[Ntwo|] You can make them in your oven. Simmer the beans first, until they're nearly tender. Then stir in the molasses, the brown sugar, the mustard and a little vinegar, and bake them covered in a Dutch oven at three hundred degrees. About four hours all told, checking every forty-five minutes so they don't dry out. The recipe for camp baked beans is on page thirteen of the book. But I'll tell you the most important part. The sweet and the sour go in only after the beans are nearly tender. Acid too early keeps the beans hard.

And one night, my old boss said the only speech I ever heard him make. A cook doesn't get thanked. A cook gets empty plates. And he looked down that table, and every plate was empty.
