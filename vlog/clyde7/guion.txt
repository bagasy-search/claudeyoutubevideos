[STORY] In nineteen fifty-eight, when I was five years old, brisket was not fancy. Nobody in this county ever asked for it. When my granddaddy broke down a steer, the brisket went in the grinder for hamburger, and the shoulder, the chuck, that went in the smokehouse on Saturday night. He would rub it with salt and black pepper and nothing else, set it on the rack over a little hickory fire, and go to bed. Sunday after church the whole family ate it on white bread with onions, and it was the best thing I ate all week.
[STORY_B] Then sometime in the nineteen nineties, brisket got famous. Television, magazines, contests. And the price went up and up. Today a whole brisket can cost you more than a car payment. But the chuck stayed cheap, because folks think of it as pot roast. And every butcher behind every counter knows the secret. The chuck has more fat running through it than the brisket flat. More fat means more juice and more forgiveness. That is why we take it home.
[TRICK1] Trick number one. Ask for the right chuck, with the right words. Most people grab the first chuck roast in the case, the thin one, about an inch and a half thick, wrapped flat on a foam tray. That one is for the slow cooker. It will dry out in a smoker before it ever gets tender. You want a thick one. At least three inches thick, three to four pounds.
[TRICK1_B] So walk up to the counter and say these exact words. I want a chuck roast at least three inches thick, from the end closest to the rib, with the most white streaks you have got. That is all. Any butcher worth his apron knows what you mean. The end closest to the rib has the chuck eye in it, the same muscle that runs into the ribeye. That is the tender part. And the white streaks are the fat inside the meat. Not the fat on the outside. The fat inside.
[TRICK1_C] Here is how you check it yourself. Hold the package up to the light and look at the cut side. If it looks like a solid red brick, put it back. If it looks like a map, with white lines running all through it like little rivers, that is your roast. On a good one you can see three or four different muscles with seams of fat between them. Those seams are going to melt in the smoker and baste the meat from the inside for six hours. A brisket flat has none of that. Remember this one. Thick, near the rib, and it looks like a map.
[TRICK2] Trick number two. Trim the outside, never the inside. Now I know what some of you are going to do. You get that roast home and you see those seams of fat in the middle, and you think, I will cut that out, that is the healthy thing to do. Do not touch it, friend. You cut those seams open and the roast falls into four little pieces that dry out like jerky.
[TRICK2_B] Here is what you do instead. Lay it on the board. With the tip of your knife, take off only the hard, white, waxy fat on the outside, the kind that feels like a candle when you press it. That fat never melts, it just turns into a rubbery skin. Trim it down to about the thickness of a nickel. Then look for any thin silver skin, that shiny sheet on the outside, and slide your knife under it and peel it off. That is it. Five minutes. Every bit of fat that is soft, leave it. Soft fat melts, hard fat stays.
[TRICK2_C] And if your roast is loose, if it is already trying to come apart at the seams, take a piece of butcher twine and tie it around the middle, two or three times, like a little package. My granddaddy tied every one of them. A tight roast cooks even. A loose roast cooks the thin parts first and you end up with a dry edge and a raw middle.
[TRICK3] Trick number three. Salt it the night before, and leave it naked in the icebox. This is the one that makes the biggest difference and costs you nothing. Most folks rub the salt on right before it goes in the smoker. The salt just sits on top, the outside tastes salty, and the inside tastes like nothing.
[TRICK3_B] So the night before, take kosher salt, half a teaspoon for every pound of meat. A four pound roast gets two teaspoons. Sprinkle it on all sides from about a foot up, so it falls even, like a light snow. Then set the roast on a wire rack over a plate, and put it in the icebox with no cover on it. No plastic wrap. Nothing.
[TRICK3_C] Here is what actually happens. In the first hour the salt pulls a little water out of the meat, and you will see it bead up on top. Then over the night that salty water goes right back in, all the way to the middle. And because there is no cover, the outside dries out like a piece of leather. A dry outside is what grabs the smoke and makes that dark crust. A wet outside just steams. So salt early, keep it naked, and let the icebox do the work while you sleep.
[TRICK4] Trick number four. The rub is two things, and only two things. In the morning, take the roast out, and while the fire is coming up, mix your rub. Coarse black pepper and kosher salt, two parts pepper to one part salt. That is it. Down here we call it the dalmatian rub, because it looks like the dog. You do not need a store rub with twenty ingredients and sugar in it. Sugar burns at these temperatures and it turns your crust bitter and black.
[TRICK4_B] And here is the part people get wrong. They pat the rub on so thick you cannot see the meat. Too much pepper and the crust tastes like a mouthful of sand. You want to see red meat between the specks. Sprinkle it on from high up, then press it in lightly with your flat hand. Do not rub it back and forth or you will just push it off. A light coat, pressed in. Because you already salted it last night, this little bit of salt is just for the crust.
[TRICK5] Trick number five. A small clean fire, two hundred and fifty degrees, and thin blue smoke. Folks build a big fire, choke it down, and pour white smoke over the meat for six hours. That thick white smoke tastes like an ashtray. It is the number one reason home barbecue tastes bitter. You want smoke you can barely see. Thin, almost blue, coming out the top like a whisper.
[TRICK5_B] So get your smoker to two hundred and fifty degrees and hold it right there. Hickory or oak. Down here we use hickory, the same wood my granddaddy cut off this land. One or two chunks at a time, about the size of your fist, never a whole pile. Let the fire breathe. Keep the vents open. If the smoke comes out thick and white, you have too much wood or not enough air. Open it up and wait until it thins out before the meat goes in.
[TRICK5_C] Put the roast on the grate, fat side up, the thickest end toward the fire. And then here is the hardest part of the whole video. Shut the lid and leave it alone. Every time you open it you let the heat out and add twenty minutes. For the first three hours, do not touch it. If you want, after those three hours, spray it once an hour with half apple cider vinegar and half water. Just a light mist, enough to keep the edges from drying. That is all the attention it needs.
[TRICK6] Trick number six. When it stalls, wrap it in paper, never foil. Somewhere around three or four hours in, when the inside reads about one hundred and sixty-five degrees, the temperature is going to stop. It will sit there for an hour, sometimes two, and not move one degree. That is the stall. The meat is sweating and the sweat is cooling it down. Folks panic, they crank the fire up, and they burn it.
[TRICK6_B] Do not panic. First look at the crust. It should be dark mahogany, almost black at the edges, and when you touch it, it should feel dry and set, not sticky. If it is, it is time to wrap. Tear off a big sheet of pink butcher paper, about three feet long. Put one spoon of beef tallow or the drippings in the middle of the paper, set the roast on top, and fold it up tight like you are wrapping a present. Seam side down, back on the smoker.
[TRICK6_C] Why paper and not foil? Foil holds every drop of steam in there, and that steam turns your beautiful crust into mush, like a pot roast. Paper lets a little of the steam breathe out, so the meat pushes through the stall faster, it stays juicy, and the crust stays a crust. That one sheet of paper costs you about a dime, and it is the difference between barbecue and pot roast.
[TRICK7] Trick number seven. You know it is done by feel, not by the number. Everybody wants a number, so here it is. Somewhere between two hundred and two, and two hundred and seven degrees. But the number is not the rule, the feel is. Take your thermometer and push it into the thickest part through the paper. If it slides in like it is going into a stick of soft butter, with no push back at all, it is done. If you feel it grab anywhere, give it another thirty minutes and check again.
[TRICK7_B] And then the step everybody skips. The rest. Pull it off, leave it wrapped in the paper, wrap an old towel around it, and set it in an empty cooler with the lid shut. One hour. Two is better. While it rests the juice that got pushed to the outside goes back into the middle. Cut it right away and all that juice runs out on the board, and you will watch your dinner soak into the wood. A roast that rests stays juicy for an hour on the table.
[TRICK8] Trick number eight, the one that fooled my family. How you slice it. A brisket has one grain running one way. A chuck roast has three or four muscles and every one of them has its grain going a different direction. If you just slice straight across the whole thing, half your slices are with the grain, and they chew like rubber bands.
[TRICK8_B] So unwrap it on the block, and look for those seams of fat we left in. Take your knife and gently pull the roast apart right along those seams, into two or three big pieces. They come apart by themselves if it is done right. Now look at each piece. You will see little lines running across the meat. That is the grain. Turn each piece so those lines run left to right, and slice straight down across them, about as thick as a pencil.
[TRICK8_C] And the small, fatty pieces, the ones that do not slice pretty, cut them into cubes about one inch, toss them in a pan with a spoon of the drippings and a little of your barbecue sauce, and put them back on the smoker for thirty minutes. Those are burnt ends. In town they sell those as a special, by the half pound, for the price of a whole roast. When my grandson took one bite of those, he put his fork down and looked at me like I had been hiding something from him for twenty years. I had.
[FIX] Now, if it did not come out right, here is what happened, and none of it is your fault. If it is tough and chewy, it was not done. Wrap it back up and give it another hour. Chuck almost never overcooks, it undercooks. If it is dry, the roast was too thin, or you sliced it with the grain, or you skipped the rest. If the crust is bitter, your smoke was white. And if it fell apart into strings when you tried to slice it, congratulations, you just made pulled beef. Put it on a bun with pickles and onion, and nobody will complain.
[FIX_B] And if your roast was a thin one, because that is all the store had, here is the fix. Smoke it the same way, but wrap it sooner, at about one hundred and fifty degrees, and pull it out about two hours earlier. It will not be quite as good as a thick one, but it will still beat any dry brisket you ever paid for.
