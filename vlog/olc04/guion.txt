[HOOK] And I know what happens in most houses. Somebody cooks a big pot of beans on Sunday, everybody eats it Sunday night, they eat it again Monday night, and by Tuesday nobody wants to look at it. The rest goes in the back of the refrigerator, and on Saturday it goes in the trash. And the bag of beans sits in the cupboard for a year after that, because beans are boring.
[HOOK] Beans are not boring. Eating the same thing three nights in a row is boring. And that's not the bean's fault, and it isn't yours either. Nobody ever showed you that one pot of beans is not one supper. It's the start of five.

[STORY] Let me tell you where this started. My first winter as head cook, the boss came into the cook shack with the ledger under his arm and told me I was spending too much on meat. Cut it by a third, Ole, or he'd find a cook who could. Forty men, and a third less meat. I went out behind the cook shack and stood in the snow for a while, I'll tell you that.
[STORY] And then the old cook from the camp across the lake, a Finn named Eino who'd been cooking since before I was born, came over on snowshoes to borrow some yeast. I told him my trouble. He laughed at me. He said, Ole, you're cooking beans like a farmer. One pot, one supper. A camp cook cooks one pot and gets three suppers out of it. Then he showed me. And I've done it his way ever since.
[WHY] First, why beans in a logging camp? Because they came in by the hundred pound sack in the fall, they never spoiled, they didn't need ice, and they didn't freeze on the sled. Fresh meat was expensive and half the winter we didn't have any. Beans and a little salt pork, that was the backbone of every camp kitchen I ever worked in.
[WHY] And here's the arithmetic most people never do. A one pound bag of dry pintos at the store is about a dollar eighty. Cooked, it makes about six cups. A can of beans, one and a half cups, is about a dollar and a quarter. So the bag gives you four cans worth of beans for the price of a can and a half. Cook a bag every week for a year and that's hundreds of dollars left in your pocket, from one thing.
[POT] Now, the pot. Part one is picking. Pour your beans out on a sheet pan and look at them. You'll find little stones, a clod of dirt, a few shriveled or broken beans. Pick them out. In the camp the cookee did this every morning, and once Lars found a stone the size of a pea that would have broken somebody's tooth.
[POT] Then rinse them in a colander. And now here's the thing everybody argues about. You do not need to soak them overnight. Soaking saves you twenty or thirty minutes of simmer, that's all. If you forgot to soak them, you lost nothing. And if you did soak them, pour off that water and use fresh.
[POT] Part two. Salt goes in at the start. Your grandmother probably told you salt makes beans hard. It doesn't. That's an old tale that got passed down. One tablespoon of kosher salt for a pound of beans, right in the pot at the beginning. The salt seasons them all the way to the middle and keeps the skins tender.
[POT] Now the pork. Cut a quarter pound of salt pork, or three or four strips of thick bacon, into pieces and put them in with the beans. Add an onion cut in half and a bay leaf if you have one. Cover everything with water, about two inches over the top of the beans.
[POT] Part three. A hard boil, ten full minutes. Bring the pot up to a rolling boil and hold it there. Don't skip this. It's the safety step for red kidney beans especially, and it's good practice for any dry bean. A slow cooker alone never gets hot enough to do it, so even if you finish in a slow cooker, boil them on the stove first.
[POT] And part four, the whisper. After those ten minutes, turn the heat down until only a bubble or two breaks the surface, and crack the lid. That's what I mean by a whisper. A hard boil after the first ten minutes splits the skins and turns the beans to mush. A whisper cooks them creamy and whole.
[POT] Pintos take about an hour and a half to two hours like that. And here's how you know they're done, the old camp test. Take a spoonful, blow on it. If the skins wrinkle and peel back, they're close. Then bite five beans. All five should be creamy all the way through. If one is chalky, give it fifteen more minutes.
[POT] One more rule. Vinegar, tomatoes, molasses, anything sour or sweet, goes in last, only when the beans are already soft. Acid keeps beans hard. That's why some people cook baked beans for six hours and they're still crunchy. They put the tomato in too early.
[SUNDAY] So that's your pot. On the first night, you serve them just like that, pot beans with the salt pork, in a bowl with the broth, and a piece of cornbread on the side. And here's the first rule of the three-day pot. Don't serve the whole thing. Take out what you need for supper, and put the rest away right.
[SUNDAY] Cool it fast. Never leave the big pot sitting on the stove overnight. Spread the beans out in shallow containers so they cool within two hours, then into the refrigerator, in their broth. That broth is gold, friend. Don't you dare pour it down the drain. Cooked beans keep four or five days that way.
[ONE] Now, day one, Monday. Soup. And this one takes five minutes. Two cups of leftover beans, a cup and a half of the bean broth, two slices of bacon, half an onion, half a teaspoon of paprika. Salt, pepper, and a splash of vinegar.
[ONE] Fry the bacon in a small pot until it's crisp. Take half of it out for the top. Cook the onion in that fat four minutes, add the paprika. Then the beans and the broth go in, bring it to a simmer, and mash it right in the pot with a potato masher until it's thick.
[ONE] Then the vinegar. Just a splash, at the end. That's the secret. Bean soup without a little sour at the end tastes flat, like something's missing, and nobody can tell you what. A splash of vinegar and suddenly it tastes finished. About fifty cents a bowl.
[ONE] Toivo didn't like beans. He told me that every single week. And every Monday he had two bowls of this soup, because it didn't look like beans and it didn't taste like Sunday. That's the whole trick of the three-day pot. Change the shape and change the taste, and it's a new supper.
[TWO] Day two, Tuesday. Beans on toast with bean broth gravy. And this is a camp cook's secret. That broth you saved? It makes a gravy that tastes like it took all afternoon.
[TWO] Three tablespoons of bacon fat or butter in a skillet. Three tablespoons of flour. Stir it two or three minutes until it smells a little nutty and turns light brown. Then whisk in two cups of the bean broth, slowly, a little at a time so it doesn't lump. Simmer five minutes until it coats a spoon. Salt, and a lot of black pepper.
[TWO] Now here's why it works. The bean broth already has body from the starch of the beans, so it needs less flour than any other gravy, and it never tastes like paste. Put a couple cups of beans into that gravy to warm through, and ladle it over thick slices of toast. Or over biscuits, if you're feeling generous.
[TWO] In the camp we used yesterday's bread, toasted on top of the wood range, right on the iron. Halvor used to ask for the burned pieces. He said the black parts were the best. That gravy costs about ten cents a serving, friend. The toast costs more than the gravy.
[THREE] And day three, Wednesday. The bean patties. Logger's hamburgers. Two cups of beans, half a cup of bread crumbs or crushed crackers, one egg, half an onion grated, a teaspoon of cumin if you have it, half a teaspoon of salt. A handful of grated cheese if there is some. And a few tablespoons of fat for frying.
[THREE] Now, the reason most bean patties fall apart in the pan is water. So here's the trick. Drain the beans well and pat them dry on a towel before you mash them. Wet beans are why bean cakes fall apart. Dry beans hold.
[THREE] Mash half of the beans to a paste and leave the other half whole. That way they hold together and still have something to bite. Mix in the crumbs, the egg, the onion, the cumin, the salt. Then let it sit ten minutes, so the crumbs soak up what's left of the moisture.
[THREE] Shape them into patties about half an inch thick, and fry them in hot fat over medium, four minutes a side, until a dark crust forms. Don't move them before then. Put a fried egg on top, or put them in a bun with a slice of onion. About forty cents a serving.
[THREE] The first time I made these, Big Swede picked one up with his fingers, looked at it, bit it, and said, Ole, this is a hamburger. I said, no, it's beans. He said, don't tell anybody. And for the rest of that winter, Wednesday was hamburger night, and nobody told anybody.
[MORE] And if there's still some left on Thursday, and with a big pot there often is, you have more choices. Fry the beans with leftover potatoes and an onion in a skillet, press it flat, let it get a crust, and crack eggs on top. That's bean and potato hash. Or mash a cup of beans smooth into your cornbread batter. Nobody will know, and the cornbread stays moist for three days.
[MORE] And one more, for the lunch pail. A cup of cold beans, a little chopped onion, a spoon of vinegar and a spoon of oil, salt and pepper. That's a bean salad, and it's better the next day. The men carried it out to the cut in their tin pails with a piece of bread, and it never froze solid the way the meat sandwiches did. A lunch for pennies.
[MORE] So you see, friend, the pot is not the supper. The pot is the pantry. You cook it once on Sunday, and then all week the pot cooks for you. That's what Eino meant. And it's the reason a camp cook could feed forty men on what a farmer spent feeding his family.
[MORE] And one last thing. If you have a little bean broth left at the very end of the week, and no beans, don't pour it out. Warm it in a cup with a pinch of salt and drink it. The men in my camp did that in the morning before they went out in the cold, a tin cup of hot bean broth. It warms you right through. Waste nothing, friend. Not even the water the beans were cooked in.

[TROUBLE] Now, if your beans come out wrong, it isn't you. If they're still hard after two hours, they're old beans, more than a year on the shelf, or something sour went in too early.
