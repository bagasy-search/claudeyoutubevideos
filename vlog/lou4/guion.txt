[HOOK] Meatloaf with brown gravy, mashed potatoes, peas, a dinner roll with butter, and a cup of coffee. Ninety-nine cents. That was a blue plate special on my board in nineteen seventy. Fifteen dinners like that, one for every day of the week, and most of them you can't find on any menu today. Tonight I'm going to give you all fifteen, and you can still make every one of them at home for about two dollars a plate.
[HOOK] Last Sunday my daughter took us out for dinner. A big family place by the highway, a menu with pictures. I ordered the meatloaf, because I always order the meatloaf. Twenty-two dollars. It came out on a long square plate, two thin slices standing up against a little tower of potatoes, and a drizzle of something red on top. No peas. No roll. I ate it, and I thought about my blue plate, the one that cost ninety-nine cents and filled up a man who had poured concrete all day.
[HOOK] Maybe you remember those plates too. A heavy white plate with three sections, the gravy running into the potatoes, the vegetables in the little corner. Your grandmother made some of these on a weeknight without a recipe. And when you try them now, the meatloaf falls apart, the liver is tough, the gravy is lumpy, and you figure those old recipes just don't work anymore. Listen to me. It was never you. Nobody wrote down the little things the cooks did. That's what I'm here for.
[PROMISE] So here's what I'm going to do. Fifteen blue plate specials, counting down to number one. What it was, what day we served it, what it cost, and the one trick that made it work. Number ten is a chicken dinner that doesn't have any chicken in it. And number one is the plate that kept my diner open for fifty years. Some of these, your doctor is going to raise an eyebrow at. That's fine. We're not eating them every day.
[INTRO] You know me, I'm Lou. Fifty years behind a griddle. In the evenings the diner changed. The breakfast crowd was gone, the lights over the counter looked yellow, and the big pots came out on the back stove. You could smell the gravy from the parking lot. Grab a cup. Let's go.
[STORY] First, why blue plate. The story the old cooks told me goes back to the Depression. Diners bought cheap plates with three sections, and a lot of them had that blue pattern on them. The special of the day came on that plate, meat, two sides, bread, all in one price, no substitutions. The cook decided, not you. That's how a diner fed a working man for under a dollar. One big pot, one price, and nothing wasted.
[N15] Number fifteen. Liver and onions. Tuesday night. Calf's liver, sliced thin, dredged in flour, and fried fast in bacon fat with a big pile of onions cooked down soft and sweet. Mashed potatoes and green beans on the side. Eighty-five cents. Half the counter loved it and the other half wouldn't sit near it. Here's the trick. Soak the liver in milk for an hour before you cook it. It takes out the bitter taste. And cook it fast, two minutes a side on high heat, with a little pink in the middle. Cook it gray all the way through and it's like chewing a boot.
[N14] Number fourteen. Ham and beans with cornbread. This was Monday. In the old days Monday was wash day, and the women didn't have time to cook, so a pot of beans went on the back of the stove with Sunday's ham bone and sat there all day. White navy beans, soaked the night before, the ham bone, an onion, a bay leaf, and water. Four hours, low. A square of skillet cornbread on the side. Sixty-five cents. The trick is no salt until the end. The ham bone salts it, and salt at the start can keep the beans tough.
[N13] Number thirteen. Goulash. Not the Hungarian kind. The American one, some folks called it American chop suey. A pound of ground beef browned with an onion and a green pepper, a big can of tomatoes, a little tomato paste, salt, pepper and a spoon of sugar, and then elbow macaroni cooked right in the sauce. One pot. Seventy-five cents with a slice of bread and butter. The kids from the high school lived on it. Cook the macaroni in the sauce, not in water. It drinks up the tomato and the whole thing gets thick.
[N12] Number twelve. Stuffed green peppers. Big green peppers with the tops cut off and the seeds out, stuffed with ground beef, cooked rice, onion and a little tomato sauce, and baked standing up in a pan with more sauce poured over. One hour at three hundred and fifty. Two peppers, mashed potatoes, eighty cents. The mistake is raw peppers. Drop them in boiling water for three minutes first. Otherwise the meat is done and the pepper is still hard and bitter.
[N11] Number eleven. The Friday fish fry. Every Friday, for the Catholic families and everybody else who wanted fish. Cod, cut in thick pieces, dipped in a batter of flour and cold beer, or cold soda water if the kids are eating, and fried at three hundred and seventy-five degrees until it's golden. Coleslaw, french fries, a lemon wedge and tartar sauce. Ninety cents, and Friday night the line went out the door. The trick is cold. Ice cold batter and dry fish. The cold hits the hot oil and puffs up crisp.
[N10] Number ten. City chicken. Here's the chicken dinner with no chicken in it. During the hard years, in the cities, chicken cost more than pork, believe it or not. So the cooks cut pork into cubes, pushed them on a wooden skewer so it looked like a drumstick, breaded it and fried it, and then baked it covered with a little broth until it was tender. Mashed potatoes and gravy. Eighty cents. People in Pittsburgh and Cleveland still know it. The trick is the bake after the fry. Fried alone, it's tough. Covered in the oven for forty-five minutes, it falls apart.
[N9] Number nine. Swiss steak. Nothing to do with Switzerland. It's a tough round steak, the cheapest cut, pounded with flour until it's thin, browned in a hot pan, and then cooked slow in tomatoes and onions until a fork goes right through it. Ninety-five cents with mashed potatoes. The name comes from swissing, an old word for pounding. The trick is the pounding with the flour. Use the edge of a heavy saucer. You beat the flour right into the meat, and later that flour thickens the gravy by itself.
[N8] Number eight. Beef tips over egg noodles. The ends and scraps from the roasts, cut in cubes, browned hard, and simmered with onions and beef broth for an hour and a half until they're soft. A little flour and water at the end to make it a gravy. Spooned over a pile of buttered egg noodles. Eighty-five cents. Brown the meat in small batches. If you crowd the pan, it steams and turns gray, and you lose the brown. The brown is the gravy.
[N7] Number seven. Chicken and dumplings. Wednesday, and when it was cold out, a lot of Wednesdays. A whole chicken stewed with celery and onion, the meat pulled off the bones, and the broth thickened a little. Then the dumplings. Two cups of flour, a tablespoon of baking powder, salt, a little butter and three quarters of a cup of milk. Spoon them on top of the simmering stew, put the lid on, and don't lift it for fifteen minutes. Ninety cents. If you peek, the steam escapes, and the dumplings come out heavy and raw inside. Lid on, hands off.
[N6] Number six. The hot roast beef sandwich. Two slices of white bread on a plate, thin slices of roast beef on top, a scoop of mashed potatoes right next to it, and then brown gravy poured over the whole thing until you can't see the bread. You eat it with a knife and fork. Eighty cents. The truckers ordered it because it filled you up for the night. At home, use leftover pot roast. The secret is warming the beef in the gravy, not in the microwave. A minute in hot gravy, and the dry slices drink it right back in.
[N5] Number five. Salisbury steak with mushroom gravy. Ground beef mixed with bread crumbs, an egg, a little minced onion and a dash of the dark steak sauce, shaped into oval patties and browned on the griddle. Then into a pan of brown gravy with sliced mushrooms and simmered for twenty minutes. Mashed potatoes and peas. Eighty-five cents. Most people only know it from the frozen dinners. Made fresh, it's one of the best things on the menu. The trick is simmering in the gravy. The patty stays juicy and the gravy tastes like beef.
[N4] Number four. Pot roast. Sunday, after church, the place full of families in their good clothes. A chuck roast, seasoned and browned on every side in a heavy pot until it's dark, then an onion, a cup of coffee and a cup of water, lid on, into a low oven, three hundred degrees, for three and a half hours. The last hour, carrots and potatoes go in around it. A dollar fifteen, the expensive special. And yes, the coffee. My first boss put a cup in every pot roast. It makes the gravy dark and deep, and you can't taste coffee at all.
[N3] Number three. The hot turkey sandwich. Thursday. We roasted whole turkeys every Thursday, not just at Thanksgiving. Sliced white meat on white bread, mashed potatoes, a spoon of cranberry sauce on the side, and turkey gravy over all of it. Ninety-five cents. Old folks who lived alone came in every Thursday just for this, because nobody roasts a whole turkey for one. At home, buy a turkey breast, roast it, and slice it thin against the grain. And make the gravy from the drippings, never from a packet.
[N2] Number two. Fried chicken. Sunday night, and it went fast. Chicken pieces soaked overnight in buttermilk with salt and pepper, then shaken in a paper bag with flour, and fried in a heavy iron skillet with the lid on for the first half, lid off for the second. Mashed potatoes, gravy, biscuits. A dollar ten. The trick is the lid. Covered, the chicken cooks through to the bone. Then lid off, and the crust gets crisp. Check the thickest piece at one hundred and sixty-five degrees by the bone.
[N1] Number one. Meatloaf. The king of the blue plate. Two pounds of ground chuck, a cup of crackers soaked in a half cup of milk, two eggs, a chopped onion cooked soft first, salt and pepper. Mix it with your hands, gently, and shape it into a loaf on a sheet pan, not in a loaf pan, so the outside browns. Ketchup with a spoon of brown sugar on top. One hour at three hundred and fifty. Let it rest fifteen minutes before you slice it, or it falls apart. Mashed potatoes, brown gravy, peas, a roll. Ninety-nine cents in nineteen seventy. I sold more meatloaf than anything else on the board in fifty years. And I'll tell you the real secret. The crackers in the milk. That's what keeps it soft.
[MEMORY] I'll tell you about one plate. There was a widow, Mrs. Novak, who came in every Thursday at five o'clock for the hot turkey, for eleven years. Always the same stool by the pie case. One Thursday she didn't come. Then the next one. The third Thursday her son came in, a grown man in a suit, and sat on her stool and ordered the hot turkey. He told me she used to say my gravy tasted like her mother's. I gave him extra gravy, and I didn't charge him. That's what a blue plate was. It was supper for people who didn't have anybody to make it for them.
[MONEY] Now let's talk money. That restaurant meatloaf was twenty-two dollars. Two pounds of ground chuck is about twelve dollars, a sleeve of crackers, two eggs, an onion, and that's a meatloaf that feeds six people with leftovers. Add a bag of potatoes and a bag of frozen peas, and you're at about two dollars and fifty cents a plate. The beans and cornbread, the goulash, the city chicken, those are under two dollars.
[MONEY] And here's how the old diner did it, and how you can do it at home. Cook one big thing on Sunday, the pot roast or a turkey breast. Monday it's hot roast beef sandwiches. Tuesday, beef tips. The ham bone goes in the beans. Nothing goes in the garbage. One price, one pot, and nothing wasted. That's the whole blue plate.
