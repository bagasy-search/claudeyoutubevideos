[HOOK] Never cook a burger in a frying pan again. That gray, shrunken hockey puck sitting in a puddle of its own juice? That's not a burger, that's a steamed meatball. The diner burger, the one with the dark brown crust and the crispy edges like lace, takes four minutes, one heavy pan, and a trick we did ten thousand times a week on my griddle. Tonight you're going to make it better than any burger place in town.
[HOOK] A couple of weeks ago I went to one of those new burger joints with my grandson. Chalkboard menu, the music too loud, a kid with a beard flipping on a little flat-top. Sixteen dollars for a double smash burger. Fries were six more. And you know what, it was pretty good. But I sat there and I watched him, and I thought, son, I was doing that in nineteen sixty-nine for forty-five cents, and nobody called it anything. We just called it a hamburger.
[HOOK] And here's what happens at your house. You buy a pack of ground beef, you make nice thick patties, round, pretty, like the picture on the package. You put them in a pan, and in two minutes there's water everywhere. The burger shrinks, it puffs up in the middle like a baseball, it turns gray, and when you bite it, it's tough and dry and somehow still pink inside. The smoke alarm goes off, the kids complain, and you figure you're just not a burger person. Listen to me. It was never you. You were taught the steakhouse way, and a burger is not a steak.
[PROMISE] So here's what I'm going to give you. Seven old diner tricks, in the order you do them. Which meat, and why the lean stuff is ruining your burger. Why you never make a patty. The heat. The smash, and the exact second you do it. When the salt goes on. The scrape. And the cheese and the bun, the way we did it at the counter. Nothing to buy except maybe a stiff spatula. [INTRO] You know me, I'm Lou. Fifty years behind a griddle. And I'll tell you what my griddle smelled like at eleven thirty on a weekday. Onions, beef fat and toasted buns, all at once, and a line of men from the plant standing behind every stool, waiting. We had forty minutes to feed a hundred people, and every one of them got a burger with a crust. Grab a cup. Let's go.
[STORY] The first day I worked that griddle, I was seventeen, and the cook was a big man named Gus, an ex-Navy cook with arms like hams. I made my burgers the way my mother did, thick and round and pretty. Gus watched me for about a minute. Then he took my patty, rolled it back into a ball, slapped it on the iron and smashed it flat with the bottom of a coffee can. It hissed so loud the waitress jumped. Two minutes later he slid it on a bun, and it had a crust I had never seen on a burger in my life.
[STORY] He said, kid, a burger is not a steak. You don't want it thick and juicy in the middle. You want as much of that meat touching the hot iron as you can get, because the brown is where the flavor lives. Brown is flavor. I've been repeating that for fifty-seven years. Now let me show you, one step at a time.
[ONE] Trick number one. The meat. Most people grab the leanest package in the case, ninety percent, ninety-three percent, because it sounds healthy. For a smash burger, that's the first mistake. Lean beef has nothing to fry in. The fat is what melts on the hot iron and fries the edges into that brown lace. Without it, the meat just sticks and dries out.
[ONE] Here's what you do. Buy ground chuck, eighty percent meat and twenty percent fat. It says it right on the label. If you can, ask the meat counter to grind it fresh for you, a pound is plenty. And keep it cold. Cold meat holds together, warm meat smears and falls apart. Buy it fat, keep it cold.
[TWO] Trick number two. Never make a patty. I know, your mother shaped them in her hands, pressed them flat, put a thumb print in the middle. All that handling packs the meat tight, and a tight burger is a tough burger. And a thick patty in a pan has no chance. By the time the middle is done, the outside is gray and the juice is gone.
[TWO] So here's what we did. You weigh out balls, about three ounces each, the size of a golf ball, a little bigger. Roll them loose, just enough to hold together, don't squeeze. Two balls make one double burger. Put them on a plate and back in the icebox until the very second the pan is ready. The ball is the secret. The spatula makes the patty, not your hands.
[THREE] Trick number three. The heat. And this is where the frying pan loses. A thin pan with high walls traps the steam, and steam is the enemy of a crust. You need a heavy flat surface that holds a lot of heat. A cast iron skillet, the heavier the better, or a flat cast iron griddle that goes across two burners.
[THREE] Put it on the stove on high, dry, no oil, and leave it there a good five minutes. You want it hot, hotter than you think. Here's the old test. Flick a few drops of water on it. If they sizzle and disappear, it's not ready. If they roll around in little balls and dance across the iron, now it's ready. And open a window, because there's going to be some smoke. That's how you know you're doing it right.
[FOUR] Trick number four. The smash. This is the whole show, and timing is everything. You drop the cold ball on the hot iron. Then, right away, within the first thirty seconds, before the meat warms up, you smash it. Hard. One time. Put a square of parchment paper on top so it doesn't stick, and press down with a big stiff spatula, and use your other hand on top of the spatula to push. Hold it there for ten seconds.
[FOUR] You want it thin, about a quarter of an inch, wider than the bun, with ragged edges. Those ragged edges are where the lace comes from. Peel the paper off. And here's what you never do. You never press it again. Not after a minute, not after you flip it. The first smash puts the meat against the iron. Every smash after that just squeezes the juice out into the pan. Smash early, smash once.
[FIVE] Trick number five. The salt. Most people mix the salt into the meat before they cook it. Don't. Salt mixed in ahead of time pulls out the proteins and turns the beef bouncy and tight, like a sausage. A diner burger is tender and loose inside.
[FIVE] So the salt goes on top, right after the smash, on the raw side facing you. A good pinch of kosher salt and some black pepper on each one. That's it. No garlic powder, no sauce mixed in, no egg, no breadcrumbs. Salt after the smash, never in the meat.
[SIX] Trick number six. The scrape. Now leave it alone. Don't poke it, don't peek. About two minutes, until you see the edges turn dark brown and crispy and little beads of juice come up on top. That's when you flip. And this is where the frying pan people lose their crust, because they slide the spatula under and lift, and the crust stays stuck on the pan.
[SIX] On the diner griddle, we scraped. You hold the spatula almost flat, the edge sharp, and you push it under the burger hard, scraping the iron, like you're cleaning it. The crust comes up with the meat. A dull, flimsy spatula won't do it. Get a stiff steel one with a sharp straight edge. That's the one thing worth buying. Scrape, don't lift.
[SEVEN] Trick number seven. The cheese and the bun. Right after you flip, a slice of American cheese goes on top, right away, while the meat is still hot. It's thin, so the second side only needs about thirty seconds. If you're doing a double, stack the second patty on top of the first with the cheese in between.
[SEVEN] And the bun goes face down in that same pan, in the beef fat, for about thirty seconds, while the cheese melts. Plain soft potato bun or a cheap white one. Not a fancy one. The fancy ones are too thick, and they eat the burger. Toast it in the fat, and you've got a crispy edge on the bread too. On my counter it was mustard, pickles and raw onion. That's a diner burger.
[ONION] Now, one more, because I promised the old timers. The onion burger. During the hard times of the thirties, out in Oklahoma, meat was expensive and onions were cheap. So the cooks sliced an onion paper thin, threw a big handful on top of the ball, and smashed the onions right into the meat. The onions fry in the beef fat underneath, they turn sweet and brown, and you stretch the beef.
[ONION] I made them every Friday. A whole onion for six burgers. Slice it as thin as you can, a knife or one of those slicers. Pile them on the ball, smash through them, salt, and when you flip, the onions are on the bottom, cooking in the fat. Some folks never go back.
[MELT] And while the iron is hot, here's what the night cooks ate. The patty melt. Same smash burger, but instead of a bun, two slices of rye bread, buttered on the outside. A smash patty, a slice of Swiss cheese, a spoon of onions you fried soft in the beef fat, and the second patty if you're hungry. Close it up, press it with the spatula, a minute and a half on each side until the rye is golden and the cheese runs out the edges. Cut it corner to corner. I've seen grown men go quiet over a patty melt at two in the morning.
[TOOLS] And if you don't have a big spatula or a griddle, don't worry about it. I've done this at my daughter's house with what she had. The bottom of a second heavy pan works to smash, or a bacon press, or a flat-bottom pot. Wrap the bottom in foil if you're worried. The cast iron skillet you probably have in a cupboard somewhere, the one your grandmother left you, that's the best burger pan in the house. Two burgers at a time in a ten inch skillet, no more. Crowd the pan and the meat steams, and we're right back to the gray puck.
[TOOLS] One more thing about cast iron. Don't wash it with a lot of soap while it's hot and don't let it soak. Scrape it, wipe it, a few drops of oil, and back on the burner for a minute. The more burgers you make in it, the better the next one comes out. That's how a diner griddle gets black and shiny over fifty years.
[TROUBLE] Now, if it goes wrong, here's why. If it sticks and tears when you flip, your pan wasn't hot enough or your spatula is dull. Give the pan more time and scrape harder. If the burger falls apart when you smash it, the meat was too warm or too lean. Keep it in the icebox until the last second, and buy the eighty-twenty.
[TROUBLE] If it comes out dry, you smashed it late or you pressed it after the flip. Smash in the first thirty seconds, then hands off. If your kitchen fills with smoke, wipe the extra fat out with a paper towel held in tongs between batches. And about safety. The government says ground beef should reach one hundred and sixty degrees. A smash burger is so thin it gets there in about three minutes. If you want to be sure, check the thickest one with a thermometer.
