[HOOK] It's the middle of July, friend. Ninety degrees in the shade, the deer flies so thick the horses are stamping, and a hundred hungry men are coming in off the cutting for supper. And I walk out behind my cook shack, open a plain board door, dig my hands into a pile of sawdust, and pull out a block of ice as clear as window glass. We cut that ice off the lake in January. Six months it sat there. And it was still hard.
[HOOK] No electricity. No ice truck. No machine of any kind. Just a lake, a saw, a shed, and a pile of the cheapest thing a logging camp had, which was sawdust. And that one shed is what kept the meat sweet, the milk cold and the butter firm for a hundred men all summer long.
[PROMISE] So today I'm going to take you through the whole thing, start to finish. How we cut the ice in the dead of winter, how we got two hundred pound blocks out of the water without losing a man, how the ice house was built so it wouldn't melt, and how the cook used it every single day from May until the frost came back.
[PROMISE] And at the end I'll show you the same five rules working in your own house today, with a picnic cooler and a few jugs of water. Because the rules that kept a ton of ice alive all summer will keep your food cold for days when the power goes out. And there's one mistake, a very common one, that will melt your ice twice as fast. Most people make it without thinking.

[WHY] First you have to understand why a camp needed ice at all. In the early days, most logging was a winter job. The ground froze, the horses hauled the logs on sleighs over iced roads, and the whole camp was a big icebox by itself. Meat hung outside frozen like cordwood. Nobody worried about it.
[WHY] But then the railroads came into the woods, and the big outfits started running camps right through the summer. And summer in the north woods is hot, friend. Hot and wet. A side of beef will turn on you in two days. Milk sours by noon. Butter runs off the plate. Flies get into everything. A cook with a hundred men and no cold place to keep the meat was a cook who would be poisoning his crew by August.
[WHY] So the answer was simple, and it was old. Your grandpa's grandpa did it on the farm. You take the winter and you save it. You cut the cold out of the lake in January, you bury it, and you dig it back up a little at a time all summer long.
[HARVEST] Ice cutting time was usually January, sometimes into February. You waited for the ice to get thick. Twelve inches was the least we wanted. Sixteen to twenty was better. Thin ice melts too fast in the house, and thin ice won't hold a team of horses either, and you need the horses.
[HARVEST] The first thing the men did was shovel the snow off a big square of the lake. Snow on top makes cloudy ice, and cloudy ice melts faster than clear ice. Then a man walked a straight line with a marker, a long board with iron teeth, pulled by a horse, and he scratched a grid into the ice like a big checkerboard. Every block the same size. Usually about twenty two inches on a side.
[HARVEST] Then came the ice plow. It looked like a big iron comb with a handle on it, and the horse dragged it down each line, again and again, cutting a groove deeper every pass. You didn't cut all the way through with the plow. You went about two thirds down. If you cut all the way through, the whole field would float loose with men and horses standing on it.
[HARVEST] The last part was by hand, with ice saws. An ice saw is a long saw, five feet maybe, with big teeth and a handle on the top, and one man works it straight up and down. Hard work. Cold work. Your mittens freeze to the handle. Big Swede could saw a row faster than any two men in camp, and he sang the whole time, mostly hymns, mostly in Swedish, and mostly wrong.
[HAUL] Once you had a channel open in the water, you broke the blocks off with a heavy iron bar called a spud. One good hit in the groove and a block would crack loose. Then men with long pike poles floated the blocks down the open channel toward the shore, one after another, like logs on a river drive.
[HAUL] The ice house had to be filled fast, in a week or two of good cold weather. So every man who could be spared off the cutting got sent to the lake. The cook sent coffee out in big pots wrapped in burlap, and doughnuts by the bushel basket, because a man cutting ice at twenty below burns through his breakfast before ten in the morning. I carried those pots out myself, and by the time I got to the end of the line the coffee had a skin of ice on it.
[HAUL] At the shore there was a wooden ramp going down into the water. A man hooked a block with the ice tongs, a team of horses pulled on a rope, and up the ramp it came, onto the sleigh. A block twenty two inches square and sixteen inches thick weighs about two hundred pounds. Picture forty of those on one sleigh.
[HAUL] And I'll tell you the rule every crew boss shouted all day long. Nobody walks backward on the ice. Not ever. Because the man who walks backward is the man who steps into the open channel. The water under that ice is just above freezing, and a man in wool clothes goes down fast. Halvor went in once, to his chest. They had him out in seconds and stripped by the cook stove in two minutes, and he was back on the ice by noon, but he never walked backward again.
[HOUSE] Now, the ice house. This is where the magic is, and it's not really magic. It's just sense. The ice house in our camp was a plain board shed. No fancy walls. No insulation like you'd buy today. Maybe sixteen feet by sixteen, and ten or twelve feet high. Built in the shade if you could manage it, on the north side of the hill, out of the afternoon sun.
[HOUSE] And here is the first secret, and the one nobody believes. The building doesn't keep the ice cold. The sawdust does. The walls just hold the sawdust in. A foot of dry sawdust all the way around the ice is better than any wall you could build out of boards. And in a logging camp, sawdust was the cheapest thing in the world. The mill made mountains of it. We hauled it by the wagon load.
[HOUSE] The second secret is under your feet. Ice melts. A little every day, all summer, no matter what you do. And that water has to go somewhere. If it pools on the floor, the bottom of your ice sits in water, and water eats ice faster than warm air does. So the floor was gravel or rough poles laid with gaps, and the ground under it sloped away so every drop ran out and away from the house.
[HOUSE] And the third secret is up top. Up under the roof, at each end, there was an open vent, a gap or a little louvered window. Warm air rises. In the summer the air under a roof gets hot as an oven. If you seal that heat in, it sits right on top of your sawdust and melts your ice from the top down. With the vents open, the heat just walks out the ends.
[PACK] Packing the house was its own job, and the cook watched it like a hawk, because it was his ice. First a foot of sawdust on the floor. Then the first layer of blocks, set tight together like bricks on a hearth, but never touching the walls. You leave a gap, twelve inches or more, all the way around, and that gap gets packed full of sawdust.
[PACK] Every crack between the blocks got filled with crushed ice, chipped off with an axe and stamped in. You want the whole stack to freeze together into one big solid lump. One big lump melts much slower than a pile of separate pieces, because it has less outside for the warm air to work on. Then the next layer, staggered, then the next, right up to the rafters.
[PACK] And on the very top went the thickest blanket of all. Two feet of sawdust, sometimes more. Because the top is where the heat comes from. Arvid used to say a good ice house looks like a pile of sawdust that just happens to have a door. He wasn't wrong.
[PACK] And no sawdust, no problem, if you were on a farm instead of a camp. The farmers used marsh hay, or straw, or even dry leaves raked up in the fall. Anything that's dry and full of little air pockets will do the same job. Lars grew up on a farm near Lake Superior, and he said his father packed their ice house with straw and chaff from the threshing, and they had ice for the cream until September. The rule is always the same. Dry, thick, and all the way around.
[PACK] How much did you lose? With a good house and good sawdust, maybe a quarter of it, maybe a third, between January and the fall. A bad house, wet sawdust, no drainage, and you could lose it all by the middle of July. And then the cook was in real trouble.
[SUMMER] So how did the cook use it? Right next to the ice house, sharing a wall, was the meat house. Some camps had the ice right up in a loft above the cold room. Cold air is heavy, friend. It falls. So the ice sat high, the cold rolled down over the hanging meat and the shelves of butter and milk below it, and the warm air went out the top.
[SUMMER] Every morning before light, the cookee went in with a lantern and the tongs. Dig down, pull a block, cover the hole right back up with sawdust. That last part matters more than anything. Every minute that hole sits open, you're letting the summer in. My first cook made me count to twenty. If I wasn't covered up again by twenty, I'd hear about it all day.
[SUMMER] One block went into the meat house. One block went into the big wooden icebox in the cook shack, for the milk and the butter and the lard. And on the hottest days, a chunk went into the water barrel the men drank from out on the cutting. A hundred men working in the heat, and a dipper of ice water at noon. You'd be surprised how much a crew will put up with for a cold drink in July.
[SUMMER] Fresh meat came in on the railroad, a quarter of beef or a couple of hogs at a time, and the cook planned the whole week around it. The freshest cuts went first, roasts and steaks for the first few days. Then the tougher cuts for stew and boiled dinner. And whatever was left by the end of the week got salted down in a barrel, just in case. With the ice house full, you could keep a quarter of beef sweet for a week or more in July. Without it, you'd be lucky to get two days.
[SUMMER] One more thing, and it's important. We used the lake ice to keep things cold, not usually to eat. Lake ice is only as clean as the lake. If we chipped it into the drinking water, it came from a clear lake far from the camp, cut in the middle where the water was cleanest. You use your head with ice the same as you do with meat.
[MISTAKE] Now, I promised you the mistake that melts ice twice as fast. And it's the one I see people make with a cooler every summer. They let the melt water sit. They think the cold water is helping. It isn't. Ice sitting in water melts much faster than ice sitting dry, because water carries the heat into the ice better than air does. In the ice house, drainage was everything. In your cooler, it's the same rule.
[MISTAKE] The second mistake is wet sawdust. Dry sawdust is full of tiny pockets of still air, and still air is what keeps the heat out. Wet sawdust is just mud. Toivo once packed a house with sawdust that sat out in the rain all fall, and we were out of ice in June. The whole crew was eating salt pork and beans in July, and they knew exactly whose fault it was.
[MISTAKE] And the third is opening the door. Every time warm air rolls in, it does a little damage. The cook planned his trips. Get everything you need in one go, in the cool of the morning, and close it up.
[TODAY] So, here's how you use the same rules today, in your own kitchen, with no ice house at all. The five rules of the ice house, in a picnic cooler. Rule one, big ice beats little ice. One big block lasts much longer than a bag of cubes, for the same reason the camp froze the whole stack into one lump. Freeze water in clean milk jugs or loaf pans in your freezer. Those are your blocks.
[TODAY] Rule two, cold before you start. Put the food in the cooler cold, and cool the cooler itself first with a sacrificial jug of ice for an hour. Rule three, ice on top and on the sides, food in the middle. Cold falls, the same as it did over the meat. Rule four, drain it. Every morning, tip out the melt water. Keep the jugs sealed and the food in tight containers so nothing sits in it.
[TODAY] And rule five, insulate the outside. Set the cooler in the shade, on the north side of the house, up off the hot ground on a couple of boards. Throw an old wool blanket or a sleeping bag over the top, the same way we threw two feet of sawdust over the ice. And open it as little as you can. Plan your trips, the same way the cook did. Do that, and a cooler that usually goes warm in two days can hold for four or five, even in summer.
