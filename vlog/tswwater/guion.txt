[HOOK] This blue flame is burning old motor oil and plain tap water. Yes, water. A few drops of it, falling on a red hot plate, and the same liter of oil that used to last me three hours now runs this stove for almost six.
[HOOK] Most people think water and oil are enemies. And in the fuel tank, they are. Water in your oil makes a burner spit, smoke and die. But put the water in the right place, at the right moment, and it does the opposite. It turns a lazy, smoking, orange fire into this.
[HOOK] Today I am going to build the whole stove in front of you, out of scrap. A brake drum, a piece of pipe, two old jugs and two little valves. Every measurement, every hole. Then we light it, we add the water, and I will show you my numbers. Hours per liter, and what the thermometer on my garage wall did.
[HOOK] And stay for the part about the cold plate. There is one moment when adding water makes this stove pop and spit oil at you, and almost everybody who tries this trick gets it wrong the first time.
[INTRO] If you are new here, this is the Scrap Workshop. I build heat out of things people throw away. Old oil, old metal, old parts. Winter is coming in, the nights are already cold out here, and the cheapest heat there is, is heat you make from something that was going in the trash.
[INTRO] This water trick is old. Mechanics have been doing it in shop stoves for a long time, long before anybody put it on the internet. The old timers I learned from never explained it. They just had a little can of water with a tiny drip over the stove, and their fire burned clean while everybody else had soot on the ceiling.
[SAFETY] One quick word before the grinder comes out. This stove goes in a garage or a shop with the chimney going outside, never in a bedroom, never in a closed room. Keep a carbon monoxide alarm on the wall, a fire extinguisher by the door, and never, ever pour water into hot oil. The water only drips onto hot steel. You will see exactly how.
[MATERIALS] Here is everything on the bench. One old brake drum, the big kind off a truck, about twelve inches across. Any shop has a pile of them out back. A piece of steel pipe, three inches across and sixteen inches long. A steel plate a quarter inch thick, for the bottom. A small thick steel disc, about five inches, for the splash plate. That is the heart of the whole stove.
[MATERIALS] Then the plumbing. Two plastic jugs, one for oil and one for water. About six feet of quarter inch copper line, the kind that comes off an old refrigerator. Two small brass needle valves, I pulled mine off a dead air compressor. A short piece of half inch pipe for the feed tube. Some bolts, a tube of stove cement, and a few lengths of five inch stove pipe for the chimney.
[MATERIALS] Tools are simple. An angle grinder, a drill with a quarter inch bit, a step bit, a wrench and a tape measure. No welding. If you can drill a hole, you can build this.
[STEP1] Step one, the burn pot. The brake drum lays flat on its face, the open side up. The center hole in the bottom is too big, so we close it. I cut a square of the quarter inch plate, seven by seven inches, and drill four holes in the corners.
[STEP1] Put the plate under the drum, mark through the holes, drill the drum, and bolt it tight with four bolts. Then a thick bead of stove cement all the way around the seam, inside and out. That seam has to be sealed. Any gap down there pulls cold air in the wrong place and the fire burns crooked.
[STEP1] The drum sits up on three bricks, so you have air under it and nothing hot touches the concrete. Iron that thick holds heat like a skillet. That is why we use a brake drum and not a thin bucket. A thin can burns through in two weeks.
[STEP2] Step two, the splash plate. This is the secret, so I will go slow. The thick little disc sits in the center of the drum, raised up half an inch on three bolts, like a tiny table. Drill three holes near its edge, put a bolt down through each one with two nuts, and set it on the bottom.
[STEP2] Why raised? Because the fire has to get under it and all around it. In ten minutes this plate glows dull red. Every drop of oil that lands on it flashes into vapor in a second. And later every drop of water that lands on it does the same thing, only much more violently. That is the whole trick, right there. A small, thick, red hot plate.
[STEP3] Step three, the burner tube. This three inch pipe stands straight up in the middle of the drum, and it is where the real flame lives. Mark five rings around it, one inch apart, starting two inches from the bottom. On each ring, eight holes, evenly spaced. That is forty holes.
[STEP3] Quarter inch bit, a drop of oil on the tip, slow speed. Do not rush this. Each one of those holes is a little air jet. When the stove is running, the chimney pulls air in through all forty of them, and that air mixes into the oil vapor rising out of the drum. That is what gives you the roaring blue ring of flame instead of a smoky campfire.
[STEP4] Step four, the lid. I cut a lid out of the rest of the quarter inch plate, round, the size of the drum, and drill a three inch hole dead center with the step bit and the grinder. The burner tube drops through that hole and sits on the lid, with about four inches sticking up above it.
[STEP4] Next to the tube I drill one more hole, an inch across. That is where the feed tube goes in, so the drops fall straight down onto the splash plate. Then the top of the burner tube goes into the five inch stove pipe, and the stove pipe goes out through the wall, up at least ten feet. A tall chimney means strong draft, and draft is your blower. This stove has no fan at all.
[STEP5] Step five, the oil line. The oil jug goes on a shelf about four feet above the stove. Gravity does the pushing. Drill the cap, push the copper line through, and seal it with a bit of epoxy. Then the needle valve, right where you can reach it standing next to the stove, and then the copper runs down into the feed tube.
[STEP5] Keep that valve far from the heat and at the height of your hand. You will be touching it a lot in the first hour. The copper line should drop into the feed tube and stop two inches above the splash plate, so every drop lands right on the hot center.
[STEP6] Step six, the water line. Exactly the same, but smaller and separate. Second jug, second valve, second copper line, into the same feed tube next to the oil line. Two lines, two valves, never mixed. The oil and the water only meet on the hot plate, never in a jug and never in a line.
[STEP6] I put a strip of red tape on the water valve and leave the oil valve plain. In the dark, with cold hands, you want to know which one you are turning without thinking about it.
[STEP7] Step seven, the oil itself. Used motor oil has water and dirt in it from the engine. Let it sit in a bucket for a week. The water and sludge sink to the bottom. Pour off the top, then strain it through an old cotton shirt into the jug. Clean oil means the needle valve does not clog in the middle of the night.
[STEP7] And here is the funny part. We just worked hard to get the water out of the oil, and now we are going to drip water right back in. But not mixed. Water in the oil makes the drip uneven, it sputters and it can put the fire out. Water on its own line, landing on red hot steel, is a completely different animal.
[STEP8] Step eight, a dry run before any fire. Fill both jugs, put a coffee can under the feed tube, and open each valve on its own. Count the drops for ten seconds. Find the spot on the oil valve that gives you one drop a second, and the spot on the water valve that gives you one drop every three seconds.
[STEP8] Mark both spots with a paint pen, a little line on the valve and a line on the body. Now you are not guessing in the dark with a hot stove in front of you. You just turn each valve to its line. Check every fitting while you are at it. A copper line that weeps oil onto a hot drum is a fire you did not plan for.
[FIRE] Time to light it. Lid off. I put a handful of torn cardboard on the splash plate, and a rag with a little of the oil on it. Never gasoline, never anything that flashes. Light it, drop the lid on, and open the oil valve just a little. One drop every second or two.
[FIRE] For the first ten minutes, it looks like this. Orange flame, lazy, a little smoke out of the top. The drum is still cold, the plate is still cold, and the oil is not vaporizing. It is just burning in a puddle. This is how most homemade oil stoves run all the time.
[FIRE] Give it time. Open the oil to about two drops a second as it heats up. Listen. The sound starts to change from a crackle to a low rumble. Look in the feed hole. When the splash plate is glowing dull red, now, and only now, it is ready for water.
[WATER] I open the water valve until I get one drop every three or four seconds. That is it. Roughly one part water to eight or ten parts oil. Watch the flame.
[WATER] Hear that? It goes from a rumble to a roar. The flame jumps up the burner tube, and through those forty holes you start to see blue. A few more minutes and it is blue and white, the whole ring. The smoke at the top of the chimney is gone. Just a heat shimmer.
[WATER] Now the magic part for your wallet. Because the fire is burning so much cleaner, I can turn the oil down. Back to one drop a second, maybe a little less, and the drum is still too hot to stand close to. Same heat, less oil. That is where the longer burn comes from.
[WHY] So why does a little water make a fire burn better? Water does not burn. It actually costs a little heat to boil it. Here is what it does. When a drop of water hits steel that hot, it turns to steam instantly, and steam takes up around sixteen hundred times more room than the water did.
[WHY] That little burst happens right in the middle of the oil. It blows the oil apart into a fine mist, tiny droplets, and tiny droplets burn completely. Big drops and puddles burn on the outside and leave soot and smoke. A mist burns all the way through. That is why the flame turns blue, and why the soot disappears.
[WHY] People argue about the chemistry, and I am not going to pretend I have a lab out here. What I can show you is what the flame does, and what the oil jug does. So let us measure it.
[OILS] What can you burn in it? Used motor oil is the main one. Old transmission fluid and hydraulic oil burn fine too. Old cooking oil works, strained the same way, and your garage will smell like a diner. What you never put in it is gasoline, paint thinner, antifreeze or brake fluid. None of those belong anywhere near this stove.
[OILS] On a really cold night, used motor oil gets thick and the drip slows down by itself. The fix is simple. Mix in a little diesel, about one part diesel to five parts oil, and keep the oil jug inside where it stays warmer until you need it. Thin oil drips steady, and a steady drip is a steady flame.
[TEST] Here is how I tested it. Same stove, same garage, same chimney, two different nights with the same temperature outside, right around thirty two degrees. My garage is about twenty four by twenty four feet, not insulated, with an old roll up door that leaks air on both sides.
[TEST] First night, oil only. To hold the garage at sixty two degrees, I had to run the oil at about two drops a second. One liter lasted me three hours and ten minutes. And the inside of the burner tube was black with soot the next morning.
[TEST] Second night, with the water drip. I brought the garage from thirty eight degrees up to sixty four in about fifty minutes, then turned the oil down until the thermometer just held there. One liter of oil lasted five hours and forty minutes. And I used about one cup of water per hour.
[TEST] Three hours ten, against five hours forty. That is almost double from the same liter of oil. And the burner tube was gray inside, not black. Less soot means less cleaning, and a chimney that keeps pulling strong all winter.
[TEST] Your numbers will be different. Your garage, your weather, your oil. But if you build it right and you get that plate red before the water goes in, you will see the difference with your own eyes in the first hour.
[MISTAKES] Now the mistakes. The big one is the cold plate. If you open the water before the splash plate is red, the water does not flash. It puddles. Then the plate heats up under the puddle, and the whole puddle turns to steam at once. Pop. It throws hot oil out of the feed hole. Wait for red. Every single time you light it.
[MISTAKES] Mistake two, too much water. If the flame turns yellow again and starts to flicker and pulse, you are cooling the fire. Back the water off until you get that steady roar. More water is not more heat. A drop every three or four seconds is plenty.
[MISTAKES] Mistake three, a weak chimney. If smoke rolls out of the burner holes into your garage, you do not have enough draft. Make the chimney taller, get rid of extra elbows, and make sure the top is higher than the roof line. A short chimney is the number one reason these stoves smoke.
[MISTAKES] Mistake four, shutting down wrong. When you are done, close the water first. Let the oil burn for two more minutes to dry everything out, then close the oil. If you close the oil first, the water keeps dripping on a cooling plate, and the next time you light it you start with a wet puddle in the bottom.
[MISTAKES] And one more, the jugs. Keep them high, keep the lids on, and keep the water jug out of a spot where it can freeze. A frozen water line on a cold night just puts you back to an oil only stove. Nothing bad happens, you just lose the blue.
[CLEAN] Keeping it running is easy. Once a week, with the stove cold, lift the lid and scrape the splash plate with an old putty knife. You will find a thin crust of carbon. Push a piece of wire through each of the forty holes in the burner tube, and once a month take the chimney down and knock the soot out over a trash can.
[CLEAN] With the water drip there is so much less to clean that I almost felt cheated. On oil only, I was scraping every three days. With water, once a week, and the crust comes off in flakes instead of tar.
[COOK] And a little bonus. The lid of this stove gets hot enough to cook on. I set an old steel shelf on two bolts next to the burner tube, and a full kettle of water boils there in about twelve minutes. Coffee in the shop on a cold morning, from oil that was headed for the trash. It does not get much better than that.
[CLOSE] So that is the old mechanic's secret. A brake drum on three bricks, a small thick splash plate raised half an inch, a three inch burner tube with forty holes, a tall chimney, and two separate drips, oil and water, that only meet on red hot steel. Oil first, red plate, then one drop of water every few seconds.
[CLOSE] Here is your homework. Before you build anything, go ask the nearest shop for one old brake drum and a bucket of their used oil. Let the oil settle for a week. By the time it is clean, you will have the stove built.
