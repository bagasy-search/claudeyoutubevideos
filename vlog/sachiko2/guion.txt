[STORY] So when I was a girl, in the nineteen fifties, Osaka was growing again. Everything was getting better, a little each year. [STORY] Then came the rainy season, the tsuyu, in June. The air was heavy and wet, and the laundry never dried. The chicken was the hardest thing to keep in those weeks, because the heat came in through the window and the fridge was small. My mother had a rule. She bought the chicken in the morning, she marinated it in the afternoon, and she cooked it before the evening. Nothing waited in the kitchen. I did not know how to cook the American chicken. It came out dry every time, no matter how carefully I watched it. [STORY] Before we start, I want you to understand one thing. The Japanese restaurant cook is not a magician. He is a person who has cooked the same dish ten thousand times, and who knows the small amounts that make the difference. You do not need a restaurant kitchen to do what he does. You need the amounts, the order, and a thermometer that tells you the truth. The rest is patience, and I will help you with that too.
[CH1] Now, let us start. I will show you the seven things, one at a time, in the order my restaurant friends taught me. Get a clean bowl, a pair of tongs, a big plate, and your thermometer. Put the thermometer next to you now, and keep it there. You will need it more than you think.
[BRINE] Number one is the brine. The brine is the reason the chicken stays juicy. For one liter of cold water, which is about four cups, use fifty grams of fine table salt. That is about three level tablespoons. Add twenty grams of sugar, which is about one and a half tablespoons. Stir until both are completely gone. If you use a coarse salt with bigger crystals, weigh it on a kitchen scale, because a spoon can fool you. Fifty grams is the amount that matters.
[BRINE] Why salt? Here is the simple reason. Salt changes the muscle proteins in the meat, so they open up a little and hold on to water. When the meat cooks, those proteins tighten, and the water inside them is pushed out. A meat that was brined has more water to lose, and it still stays wetter at the end. I will explain the science at the end, with a source, so you know I am not guessing.
[BRINE] Put the cut chicken into the brine and put the bowl in the refrigerator. Not on the counter. Never on the counter. For thighs cut into halves or thirds, one hour is enough. For thin pieces of breast, forty minutes is enough. Do not go past two hours, because the meat becomes too soft and the texture turns strange. Cold is what keeps this safe, so the bowl stays in the fridge the whole time.
[BRINE] When the time is done, take the chicken out of the brine and let it drain in a colander. Do not rinse it under the tap. This is important, and I will say it again later. Raw chicken should never be rinsed in the sink, because the water splashes and spreads the germs around your kitchen. Instead, take paper towels and pat each piece dry, all sides, until the surface is no longer shiny. A dry surface is what makes the next step work.
[SEASON] Number two is the seasoning, and this is the flavor my mother used. In a small bowl, put two tablespoons of cooking sake, one tablespoon of soy sauce, one tablespoon of grated fresh ginger, and one small clove of garlic, grated fine. Mix it well. The ginger is the soul of the dish. Japanese cooks always use fresh ginger, not the powder from the jar, because the fresh one is bright and clean, and it takes the smell of the chicken away.
[SEASON] Put the dry chicken into this mixture, toss it with your hands, and cover the bowl. Keep it in the refrigerator for fifteen to twenty minutes. Do not leave it longer than two hours. In my kitchen I use a plastic container with a lid, the same one every time. When you lift the chicken out, it should look a little glossy and smell of ginger and warm soy, but never sour.
[COAT] Number three is the coat, and this is the trick that surprises almost everybody. The Japanese word is katakuriko, which means potato starch. It is a fine white powder that looks like flour, but it is not flour. Flour makes a heavy, soft crust that gets soggy in a few minutes. Potato starch makes a light crust that turns crisp and stays crisp, and inside it, the starch forms a thin layer that holds the juices in, the way my mother always said.
[COAT] For about five hundred grams of chicken, which is about one pound, use four tablespoons of potato starch. Put the seasoned, drained chicken in a bag or a bowl. Add the starch and toss until every piece is lightly and evenly covered. Shake off the extra. You want a thin, dusty coat, not a thick paste. Let the pieces rest for five minutes so the starch becomes a little damp and holds on to the meat.
[COAT] Here is the most important warning for the coat. Do not let the chicken sit in the starch for more than ten minutes before you fry it. If it sits too long, the starch takes in the moisture from the chicken, turns gummy, and falls off in the oil. Coat the chicken right before you heat the oil. Work with one batch at a time. Put the rest of the chicken back in the refrigerator, covered, while the first batch fries.
[THIGH_BREAST] Number four is the choice of cut, because thighs and breast are very different animals, even though they come from the same bird. The thigh has more fat and more connective tissue. That fat melts while it cooks and keeps the meat moist, so the thigh forgives you. If you cook it a little longer than you planned, it still tastes good. This is why, in my mother's kitchen and in every Japanese restaurant I have worked with, the thigh is the king of karaage, the crispy fried chicken.
[THIGH_BREAST] The breast is lean. It has almost no fat to protect it. It goes from juicy to dry in a minute or two. So with the breast, we do two things. First, we make the thickness even. Put the breast on a board, lay your hand flat on top, and cut horizontally through the thickest part, like opening a book, so the piece is about one and a half centimeters thick everywhere. Second, we give it a short cooking time in gentle heat, and a long rest. Thin and even means it cooks at the same speed from edge to edge.
[THIGH_BREAST] For the thighs, cut each one into pieces about the size of a walnut, two or three centimeters. Keep the skin on, because the skin is what makes the crust. The thighs you see in the picture are the cold pan kind, skin side down, which I explain in the bonus tricks. If you buy boneless thighs, that is fine too. The cut size matters, because a big piece takes longer in the middle, and the outside is burned before the inside is ready. Small even pieces, all the same size, is the rule. I make them all the same size even if I have to take a long time to do it.
[HEAT] Number five is the gentle heat, the one that most people skip. Most home cooks hurry. They turn the burner to the highest level, they drop the chicken into oil that is already smoking, and the outside turns brown in thirty seconds while the middle is still raw. That is the worst mistake, and it is also the most dangerous, because you think the chicken is cooked when it is not.
[HEAT] Use a heavy pot or a deep pan. Pour in enough oil to cover the chicken by about five centimeters. Vegetable oil or canola oil is fine. Put the thermometer in the oil, clip it to the side of the pot, and heat the oil to about one hundred sixty to one hundred seventy degrees Celsius, which is about three hundred twenty to three hundred forty Fahrenheit. That is low for frying, and that is the point. Gentle heat lets the inside warm up slowly, while the crust forms without burning.
[HEAT] When the oil is ready, lower the chicken in carefully, one piece at a time, away from your face and your arms. The oil will bubble hard for a moment. Do not crowd the pot. If you put in too many pieces, the oil temperature falls, and the chicken soaks up oil instead of frying. Keep the pieces apart, use chopsticks or tongs, and turn them gently every minute or so. For the first fry, cook for about four to five minutes, until the outside is pale gold and the pieces float a little higher in the oil.
[HEAT] Then take them out and put them on a wire rack. Not on paper towels. A paper towel traps the steam, and the steam softens the crust you just made. A rack lets the steam go. Let the chicken rest on the rack for five minutes. This rest is the first part of the trick. The heat keeps moving through the meat during the rest, and the inside finishes cooking without the outside becoming too dark.
[DOUBLE] Number six is the double fry, the karaage trick that makes a restaurant crust. Karaage means, roughly, fried. The Japanese cooks in the best places fry twice, and the second fry is the one that makes the crust crackle when you bite it. Heat the oil up to about one hundred eighty degrees Celsius, which is about three hundred fifty five Fahrenheit. Now the oil is hot enough to crisp the surface in a short time.
[DOUBLE] Put the rested chicken back into the hot oil for one to two minutes. Only a short time. You will hear the sound change, from a soft sizzle to a sharp crackle. The outside becomes deep golden and very crisp, and the starch layer becomes a light, glassy shell. Take the pieces out and put them on the wire rack again. Let them drain for a minute, then serve them, or let them rest for a few minutes before serving if you want the crust to set.
[DOUBLE] Here is a note for the second fry. Do not do the second fry too early. If you rush it, the first fry has not finished the inside, and the second fry makes the outside too dark. Wait the full five minutes on the rack. If you are cooking many batches, keep the finished pieces in a low oven on a rack, about one hundred degrees Celsius, so they stay hot, but do not stack them, because the stack traps steam again.
[POACH] Number seven is the trick for the breast, and this one is my favorite, because it makes lean chicken soft like a custard, and it is almost impossible to ruin. You do not fry it. You poach it in a covered pot with the heat turned off. Bring two liters of water to a boil in a pot big enough for the chicken. Add one teaspoon of salt, four or five thin slices of fresh ginger, and one tablespoon of sake. Let it boil for one minute.
[POACH] Now lower in two whole breasts, about five hundred grams together. Turn off the heat right away. Put the lid on the pot. Do not lift the lid. Let the chicken stand in the hot water for twenty to twenty five minutes. The water will not boil again, it will stay hot and gentle, and it will cook the meat slowly from the outside to the inside. Your job is to keep the lid on and wait. This is the Japanese patience, and it is a very good kind of patience.
[POACH] After twenty minutes, take out one breast and check it with your thermometer. If it has not reached one hundred sixty five degrees Fahrenheit, which is seventy four Celsius, put the lid back on and wait five more minutes. If the water has cooled a lot, you can turn the heat on for one minute, then turn it off again and cover. Never serve breast that is still pink in the middle. When it reaches the number, lift it out, put it on a plate, and let it rest for ten minutes before you cut it.
[POACH] The poached breast is tender and moist, and it is perfect for a salad, for a bowl of noodles, or for a cold dish in summer. Keep the water in the pot, because it is a light and tasty broth. Let it cool on the counter for no more than an hour, then put it in the refrigerator. Chicken broth is food, and it can go bad quickly if it is left warm for hours in the kitchen.
[THERMO] Now the most important section of this whole video. The thermometer. Chicken must reach one hundred sixty five degrees Fahrenheit, which is seventy four degrees Celsius, inside the thickest part. Not on the surface. Not by color. Color lies. The color of chicken can look white when it is still not safe, and it can look pink when it is safe, because of the juice and the ginger and the salt. Only the thermometer tells the truth.
[THERMO] How do you measure? Push the probe into the thickest part of the piece, from the side, so the tip reaches the middle. Keep the tip away from bone, because bone heats differently and gives a wrong reading. Wait a few seconds until the number stops moving. Check at least two pieces from each batch, and the biggest piece always. If one piece is below the number, put it back in the hot oil or the hot water for a minute or two, and check again. Never guess. Never say, it looks done.
[THERMO] Two more things about safety, and I want you to hear them slowly. First, never wash raw chicken in the sink. The United States food safety service advises against it, because the water spreads bacteria onto your counter, your towel and your hands. Pat it dry instead. Second, keep raw chicken and cooked chicken apart. Use one board and one knife for the raw meat, and a different clean plate for the cooked meat. Wash your hands with soap after you touch raw chicken, before you touch anything else in your kitchen.
[THERMO] I have one more safety rule, and it comes from my own notebook. Some pages say, I was in a hurry, and I served the chicken early. Those pages are the ones I do not like to read. In a hurry, we make the mistake. So write the number on a piece of tape, put it on the thermometer, and let it remind you every time: one sixty five, then serve. That is the whole rule.
[VARIATIONS] Now, the seven things are yours. But my mother also had many small tricks that came out of the same kitchen, and she used them all year long. I will give you eight of them. Each one has an amount, and each one has a reason. Write them in your notebook if you have one. I have a blue notebook, and I still write in it at seventy eight.
[VARIATIONS] Bonus one. My mother also made chicken wings the same way. Use the same brine, but only for thirty minutes, because wings are small and they take the salt quickly. Then the same ginger and sake seasoning, and the same potato starch. The second fry is one minute longer, so the skin becomes a little darker. She called them the wings for the children, and they disappeared before the rice was on the table.
[VARIATIONS] Bonus two. This is the cold pan trick, the one for the thighs in my picture, and it is very gentle. Take a thigh, skin side down, and put it in a cold heavy pan with one tablespoon of oil. Turn the heat to medium low. Now wait. Do not touch it. After about ten to twelve minutes, the skin turns golden and crisp, and the fat slowly melts out. Turn it over, cook two or three minutes more, and check it with your thermometer. The number is still one sixty five. The patience does all the work.
[VARIATIONS] Bonus three. My mother's sweet glaze. Mix two tablespoons of soy sauce, two tablespoons of mirin, which is a sweet cooking wine, and one tablespoon of sugar. Brush it on the thighs in the pan during the last minute of cooking, never before. If you add it too early, the sugar burns, and the chicken tastes bitter. Keep the pan on low, turn the pieces, and let the glaze turn shiny. It should smell like caramel, not smoke.
[VARIATIONS] Bonus four. The lunchbox. In Osaka, my mother packed chicken for my school lunch, and the trick was the cold. She shredded a poached breast by hand, with two fingers, into thin strips. She added cucumber, a little sesame, and a spoon of the cooled poaching broth. Then she put the box inside a bag with a cold pack. Cooked food should not stay warm in a lunch bag for long, so the cold pack is not optional. The lunch is good until the afternoon, and it must be eaten within a few hours.
[VARIATIONS] Bonus five. The broth. Do not throw away the water from the poaching pot. Strain it through a fine sieve, pour it into a shallow container, and let it cool for a short time on the counter. Then put it in the refrigerator, not later than two hours after cooking. A shallow container cools faster than a deep one. Use the broth within three or four days, or freeze it in small jars. The broth is light, with ginger and a little salt, and it is the base for a bowl of soup in winter.
[VARIATIONS] Bonus six. Leftover karaage the next day. Here is the rule. The microwave makes it soft, so do not use it for crispness. Put the pieces on a rack over a tray, and heat them in the oven at two hundred degrees Celsius, about four hundred Fahrenheit, for about eight to ten minutes. The crust comes back to life. Check the middle with your thermometer. It must reach one sixty five. Leftover chicken is safe only when it is reheated all the way, so the number is always the final word.
[VARIATIONS] Bonus seven. Coat now, fry later. My mother prepared chicken in the morning for the evening meal, when the rainy season made the afternoons impossible. She seasoned the chicken, coated it in potato starch, and laid the pieces on a tray, not touching. She covered the tray and put it in the refrigerator. Only for a few hours, never overnight. Then she fried it fresh before dinner. The starch coat stays dry if you keep the pieces apart and cold, and the starch stays dry only in the cold.
[VARIATIONS] Bonus eight. Frozen chicken. Here is the one thing I was taught the hard way. Do not fry chicken from frozen. The outside browns long before the middle is warm, and the thick pieces come out raw in the center. Thaw it in the refrigerator, one day for a large package, a few hours for small pieces. Put it on a plate under the rest, so the juice does not drip on your vegetables. Then season it, coat it and fry it, exactly as I showed you. A thawed piece is a fresh piece.
[MISTAKES] Now, the mistakes. Every one of these I have made myself, in my own kitchen, and I am not ashamed to tell you about them. Almost every bad plate of chicken comes from one of these five or six cases. I will tell you the case, what it looks like, what caused it, and what to do the next time. If it happens to you, it does not mean you are a bad cook. It means the chicken did what chicken does when a small rule is broken.
[MISTAKES] Case one. The chicken comes out dry and chewy, like a rubber ball. This is almost always the breast, cooked too hard and too long, with no brine. If it comes out like that, it was the breast, and it was not you. Next time, brine the breast for forty minutes, cut it to an even thickness, poach it covered with the heat off, and check the number early. The breast gives up its water fast. You must catch it at one sixty five and not one minute later.
[MISTAKES] Case two. The crust is pale and soft, and it falls off in the oil. This is the potato starch coat, and the reason is almost always the wait. The chicken sat in the starch for twenty minutes, it got damp, and the damp starch washed off the meat. Or the oil was not hot enough for the first fry. It was not your hands. Next time, coat just before frying, shake off the extra, and check that the oil reaches the right number before the first piece goes in.
[MISTAKES] Case three. The outside is very dark, but the middle is pink near the bone. This is the classic burn. The oil was too hot, around two hundred degrees, and the outside was finished in a minute while the inside was still raw. It was the heat, not your patience. Next time, lower the oil to the gentle number, use smaller pieces, and always cut the thickest piece in half to look at it before you serve the batch. Never trust the color of the crust alone.
[MISTAKES] Case four. The chicken tastes watery, and the seasoning sits on the surface like a wet sheet. This is what happens when the chicken was rinsed under the tap after the brine, or when the seasoning was too wet to stick. The brine is supposed to be patted dry. The rinse is what washes the salt away and makes the meat bland. Next time, pat it dry with paper towels until the surface feels almost tacky, then season.
[MISTAKES] Case five. The skin is soft and rubbery, and the chicken looks grey. Usually this is a crowded pot. Too many pieces went into the oil at once, the temperature crashed, and the chicken steamed in its own juice. Or the pieces were wrapped in paper towel after frying, and the steam softened the crust. It is not you who failed. It is the pot that got too full. Next time, fewer pieces, the wire rack, and the patience to wait between batches.
[MISTAKES] Case six. The breast is red in the middle, and everyone is hungry. This is the most dangerous case, and I will say it slowly. Do not serve it. Color is not a safety test, and the ginger and the salt can make the meat look cooked when it is not. If it is red in the middle, put it back in hot water or hot oil and check again. A guest who waits five more minutes is much better than a guest who gets sick. Use the thermometer, every single time, and then serve with a happy heart.
[SCIENCE] Now, why does all of this work? I promised you the science, and I like to explain it the way my husband explained his engine to me, slowly and with a picture in the mind. Imagine the meat as a sponge made of thin strings of protein. Inside the sponge there is water. When the chicken heats up, the strings tighten, and like a sponge that is squeezed, they push the water out. That is the dry chicken you know.
[SCIENCE] Salt does something different. The salt from the brine changes those protein strings. They loosen and open a little, and they hold on to more of their own water, even while the meat heats. Researchers have studied this for many years. A well known paper from nineteen eighty eight, by Offer and Knight, explains that the way meat holds water depends on how its proteins are arranged, and that salt changes that arrangement. The brine does not add water, it helps the meat keep its own.
[SCIENCE] The potato starch works in a different way, on the outside. When the starch touches hot oil, its grains swell with the moisture from the chicken surface and form a thin, glassy layer. That layer dries fast in the oil and turns crisp. It is also a barrier. It slows the escape of the juices from the meat, so less of the good water leaves the chicken while it fries. It is the same reason a light coat helps a crust stay crisp, instead of soggy.
[SCIENCE] And the gentle heat gives the middle time to warm up. Heat moves slowly into the middle of a thick piece. If the outside is very hot, the outside cooks quickly and the middle is still far from the number. A lower temperature lets the heat travel to the center while the outside gets golden. Then the short hot second fry finishes the crust without going back into the danger zone for long. The double fry, the rest, and the low poach are all a way of giving the middle time to catch up.
[SCIENCE] Finally, the number itself. One sixty five Fahrenheit is the temperature that the United States food safety service sets for poultry, because at that temperature the harmful germs that can live in raw chicken are destroyed quickly. It is not a number for the taste. It is a number for your family. A piece cooked to the number is safe, and it can still be juicy, because the brine and the starch and the rest all protect the water inside it.
[MONEY] Let us talk about money, because I am a practical woman and so was my mother. A whole chicken from the big store in America is cheap, but the pieces that the restaurant uses, the boneless thighs and the breasts, cost more per pound when you buy them already cut. My old way costs less. I buy the thighs with the bone and skin, which are the cheapest part, and I cut them myself in ten minutes. A pack of six bone in thighs costs about six dollars in my town.
[MONEY] The potato starch is about four dollars for a large bag, and a bag lasts me all winter. The sugar, the salt, the sake, the soy sauce and the ginger are the things that are already in most kitchens. Let us say the whole dinner for four people costs about twelve dollars with the oil. The store version, the chicken that comes ready coated in a box, costs about twice that for half the flavor. You are paying for the box, and the box is not your mother's kitchen.

On Tuesday, make the poached breast in the covered pot, and keep the broth. Use the breast on Wednesday for the cold salad, and the broth for a light soup. On Friday, make the karaage for the family, with the double fry and the thermometer on the table. One chicken, four dinners, and almost no waste. Mottainai. Nothing thrown away.

[RECAP] So, let us remember the whole method. One: a brine of one liter of cold water, fifty grams of fine salt and twenty grams of sugar, one hour in the refrigerator for thighs, forty minutes for breast. Pat it dry, never rinse it.
