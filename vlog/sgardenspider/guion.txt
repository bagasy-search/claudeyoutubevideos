[HOOK] A small brown bottle of peppermint oil, a jug of white vinegar and a bowl of lemon peels. That is what my mother used to send the spiders out of her house and her little shed in Osaka every fall. No spray. No poison. Never once. And last week a young man with a tank on his back walked around my neighbor's garage for ten minutes and asked her for four hundred dollars. Four hundred. Come, sit with me on the back step. I will show you what my mother did instead, and why it still works in an old American garage, fifty years later.
[PAIN] You know the feeling. The first cold night of the fall, you open the garage door, and there it is. A big web across the corner of the window, with a fat brown spider sitting in the middle like she pays the rent. Webs on the shelves, webs on the rake, webs on the garden boots you left by the door. Little white egg sacks up in the corners of the shed. You sweep them down on Saturday, and on Monday they are back. And you start to walk into your own garage with your arms up in front of your face.
[NOTYOU] I want to tell you something, and please listen. It is not because your house is dirty. It was never you. A spider does not come because your garage is messy. It comes because the nights are getting cold, and your garage is warm and dark and full of little bugs to eat. So the answer is not to fight it with a spray can. My mother had a simpler answer. Make the house smell wrong to a spider, take away its dinner, and close the door behind it.
[PROMISE] So today I will show you all three things, one by one. What each one does, exactly how to mix it, and where it goes. I will show you the four places in every garage and shed where spiders come in, because most people spray the middle of the room, where no spider ever walks. The one mistake that makes everything useless. And at the end, how to keep them out all winter with ten minutes a week. The first thing is the one that surprises people most, because it smells like a candy cane.
[INTRO] You know me, I am Sachiko. On my kitchen channel I cook the old Japanese way, the way my mother cooked. But my mother's house was more than her kitchen. Behind our little wooden house in Osaka there was a small shed made of cedar boards, where she kept her bamboo brooms, her clay pots, her bags of rice straw and my father's bicycle. Every fall, the spiders moved into that shed, and every fall my mother moved them out again, politely.
[CTA1] Quick, while I have you. If you want to keep these three things, I wrote all of them in the description, with every measure, so you can find them again next fall. And if you like the old ways, please subscribe. This is my garden channel, and here we go outside, to the yard, the garage and the shed. Now, back to the spiders.
[STORY] In Japan, the spider is called kumo, and the old people have a saying about it. A spider you see in the morning brings good luck, so you must never kill it. A spider you see at night is a little different, but still, you carry it outside. My mother believed this all her life. She would not step on a spider, not even a big one. She would put a teacup over it, slide a postcard under the cup, and carry it out to the persimmon tree. Then she would say, go eat the bugs out there, where you belong.
[STORY_B] But my mother did not want a house full of webs either. So she learned from her own mother, in the countryside, before the war, how to make a house a place where spiders do not want to stay. My grandmother used three things from the kitchen. When I came to America in nineteen seventy, with my husband, our first little house had a garage full of webs, and a spider as big as my hand behind the water heater. I wrote to my mother. Her letter came back three weeks later. Three lines. Mint oil in the water. Vinegar in the corners. Lemon peel on the sills. I still have that letter.
[ONE] Number one, peppermint oil. This is the most important one, so I start with it. Peppermint oil is the strong oil pressed from the mint plant, and you find it in a small brown bottle with the baking things, next to the vanilla. My mother used the mint that grew wild along the river, crushed in a jar of water. Here, I use the little bottle. Spiders do not smell with a nose like we do. They taste and smell the world through the tiny hairs on their legs. And peppermint, to those little legs, is like walking on a hot stove. The spider does not get hurt. It just turns around and goes somewhere else.
[ONE_B] Here is how my mother mixed it, and how I do it. In an old spray bottle, I pour two cups of water. Then about twenty drops of peppermint oil and one small squeeze of plain dish soap. The soap is very important. Without the soap, the oil floats on top of the water like a little island, and you spray only water. With the soap, the oil mixes in and goes everywhere the water goes. Shake it well before every use, until the water looks a little cloudy. That is your mint water. Make it fresh every two weeks, because the smell gets weak after that.
[ONE_3] Now, where it goes. Not in the middle of the room. Spiders walk along the edges, always the edges. So you spray the four places where they come in. One, around every door, the bottom and both sides, where the weather strip is old. Two, around every window frame, especially in the corners. Three, the gap where the garage door meets the floor. And four, any little hole where a pipe or a wire comes through the wall. Spray a thin line, like you are drawing a border. Do not soak it. A fine mist is enough.
[ONE_4] Do it once a week in the fall, when the nights get cold, and once every two weeks in the winter. In my garage, the new webs stopped after the second week. The first time I did it, my husband said the garage smelled like a toothpaste factory. He walked around with his nose up, complaining. Two weeks later, he could reach his fishing rods without a broom in his other hand, and he stopped complaining. He even asked me to do his little workbench too.
[ONE_5] One more thing about number one. My mother also kept little cotton balls with three drops of mint oil on each one, tucked into the corners of the shed, behind the clay pots, and on the high shelf where the spiders like to hide their egg sacks. Every two weeks she added three more drops. I do the same in my garage, and in the shed behind the tomatoes. I put one in each corner, one behind the water heater, and one in the box where I keep my garden gloves. Nobody has found a spider in a garden glove in this house since.
[TWO] Number two, white vinegar. This is my grandmother's favorite, and the second thing in your pantry. Plain white vinegar, the cheap kind for pickles and for cleaning. Spiders do not like the sour smell, and they do not like walking on it. But the real reason my grandmother loved vinegar is different. It cleans away the silk lines that spiders leave behind. Every spider leaves a little thread wherever it walks, like a road sign for the next spider. Vinegar wipes the road signs away.
[TWO_B] Here is what my grandmother did. She mixed one cup of white vinegar with one cup of water in a bucket. Then, with an old cotton rag, she wiped every windowsill, every door frame and the bottom of every wall in the shed. Slowly, the whole edge of the room, like she was writing a long line around it. Then she took her broom, the soft one, and swept down every old web and every egg sack, and carried them outside in a paper bag to the far end of the garden, far from the house.
[TWO_3] Then she did one more thing, the clever thing. She poured a little of the vinegar water into a small dish and put it on the windowsill of the shed, in the sun. The sour smell sat there all day, quietly, and the spiders did not build on that window. My mother did the same in our kitchen window, every summer, with a little blue dish that had a chip on the edge. That dish is on my own windowsill now, here in America, full of vinegar water and one slice of lemon, and the window above my sink has no webs. Nothing in that house was ever only one thing. The vinegar cleaned the glass, kept the spiders away, and in the evening my mother used the rest to pickle a few cucumbers.
[TWO_4] A small warning. Vinegar can make a dull spot on stone and on some wood floors, so test a little corner first, and do not use it on marble. And open the garage door for an hour after you wipe, because the smell is strong for a while. My husband said our garage smelled like a salad. My mother said that every time too, and every time she laughed, and then she made a salad.
[MID] Before I show you number three, tell me in the comments. Where are the spiders in your house? In the garage, in the shed, in the basement, or in the corners of the windows? I read every comment, and I want to know. And if you have a friend who is walking into webs right now, send this to her. It may save her four hundred dollars.
[THREE] Number three, lemon peels. In Osaka my mother used the peels of yuzu, a small, bumpy Japanese citrus with a very strong smell. Here in America, I use lemon, and sometimes orange. Spiders do not like the smell of citrus at all. And the peels are something you were going to throw away anyway, after you squeeze the lemon for your tea or your fish.
[THREE_B] Here is how I do it. I keep a jar with a lid next to the sink. Every time I use a lemon or an orange, the peel goes in. Once a week, I take the peels and rub the white side, the side with all the oil, along the windowsills and the bottom of the garage door. Just a quick rub, like you are polishing a shoe. Then I lay the old peels in the corners of the shed, on the high shelves, and behind the boxes. When they get dry and hard, after about a week, they go in the compost, and new ones go in their place.
[THREE_3] My mother also made a lemon water for the spray bottle, when the mint was finished. She put the peels of four lemons in a pot with two cups of water, boiled it for ten minutes, and let it sit overnight with the lid on. In the morning, she poured it through a cloth into the spray bottle with a little vinegar. That one smells the nicest of all three. My neighbor started asking me for my lemon peels, and now she keeps a jar by her sink too. We trade, a little. She gives me lemons from her tree, I give her pickles from my kitchen, and we both have clean garages and a reason to talk over the fence every Saturday.
[THREE_4] My mother's old trick with the light is my little extra for you. In the countryside, the farmers knew that spiders go where the bugs go, and bugs go to the light. So in the fall my grandmother kept the lamp by the shed door turned off at night, and she hung a paper lantern far away, at the end of the garden, on the persimmon tree. The bugs went to the lantern, and the spiders followed them there. Here, I changed the bulb over my garage door to a soft yellow one, the kind made for porches, and I turn it off when I go to bed. The bugs go to my neighbor's bright white light instead. I feel a little bad about that.
[MISTAKE] The one mistake that makes everything useless. Doing only the webs. Most people sweep down the webs, spray the middle of the room, and stop there. But the web is only where the spider eats. The spider is not the problem. The open door and the dinner are the problem. So first close the gaps, then clean the silk lines with vinegar, then the mint water on the edges, and the lemon peels in the corners. And clear the clutter on the floor. A pile of old boxes and newspapers in the corner of a garage is a hotel for spiders, with room service.
[CHECK] How do you know it worked? Use my mother's little test. Sweep every web down on Sunday, every single one, so the garage is clean. Then look again on Wednesday. If there are no new webs, the spiders have moved out. If one corner has a new web, that corner has a door. Look for the little gap there, close it, and give it some extra mint water and a fresh peel, right there.
[TROUBLE] If the webs keep coming back after a month, look outside. Bushes and vines that touch the walls of the house are like bridges for spiders. A pile of firewood stacked against the garage is like a city. My mother kept everything one big step away from the walls, her flower pots, her straw, her firewood. I cut back my bushes in October so nothing touches the house. And remember, the worst week is always the first cold week of the fall, so start before that. My mother kept a little calendar by the back door, and on the first cool weekend of September she wrote one word on it, kumo, so she would not forget. I do the same thing on my kitchen calendar now, in pencil, and every year it reminds me of her.
[FIX] Now, the keeping. Once the spiders are out, ten minutes a week keeps them out. Mint water on the doors and the windows on Sunday. Fresh lemon peels in the corners. The vinegar wipe once a month. And a soft broom along the ceiling of the garage every Saturday, before any spider can finish a new web. Fix the old weather strip at the bottom of the doors, it is cheaper than you think, and put a little screen over the small air holes of the shed. A garage with no open doors and no dinner is a garage with no guests.
[MONEY] Let me do the arithmetic, because this house does not do villains, it does arithmetic. The man with the tank, four hundred dollars, and next fall maybe again. My way. A small bottle of peppermint oil, a few dollars, and it lasts the whole season. A jug of white vinegar, a few dollars. A little dish soap you already have. Lemon peels, nothing at all, you already throw them away. A soft yellow bulb, a few dollars. Less than twenty dollars for the whole fall and winter.
[MEMORY] I remember one autumn evening in Osaka. I was maybe eight. My mother and I were in her little cedar shed, and there was a big yellow and black spider in the corner, in a web as wide as an umbrella. I was afraid. My mother did not say anything. She took a teacup and a postcard, caught the spider very gently, and carried it out to the persimmon tree. Then she wiped the corner with her vinegar rag, tucked a sprig of mint into the crack in the wall, and patted the wood with her hand, the way you pat a child on the head. She said, there. Now you live outside, little weaver. And it did.
[RECAP] So, here is everything one more time. Number one, peppermint oil with a little dish soap in a spray bottle of water, sprayed on the four doors where spiders come in, every week in the fall. Number two, white vinegar and water, wiped along the edges to clean away the silk roads, and a little dish of it on the windowsill. Number three, lemon peels, rubbed on the sills and laid in the corners. And my grandmother's trick, the soft yellow light by the door.
