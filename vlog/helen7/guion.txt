[HOOK] Never boil your potatoes in a pot of plain water again. That little jar of bacon fat you scrape into the trash every Sunday morning is the best thing that ever happened to a potato, and it costs you nothing. You already paid for it when you bought the bacon. Last winter a man named Marvin was standing right here at my counter with a bag of frozen seasoned breakfast potatoes in his basket. Four dollars and ninety-nine cents, for a pound and a half.
[HOOK_B] He said, Helen, when I make them myself I boil them, and they come out gray and watery and they fall apart in the pan. So I buy these. I asked him one question. Marvin, what do you do with your bacon grease? He said, I throw it out. I told him, Marvin, you are throwing out the trick and paying five dollars for the bag. He went home with a ten pound bag of russets, six dollars and forty-nine cents, and he came back the next week and told me they were the best potatoes he had made since his wife passed.
[PROMISE] So today I am going to show you exactly what I told Marvin. Twelve tricks, in the order you do them, from the bag at the store to the plate. Number four is how you save the fat so it keeps for months and never tastes old. Number six is the whole title of this video, the pan trick that cooks a potato without a pot of water, crispy outside and soft as butter inside. And number nine is why your mashed potatoes turn to glue. The people who sell frozen potatoes are not going to love this one. That is all right.
[INTRO] You know me, I am Helen. Forty-one years behind this counter, in a little meat market in Iowa, and my father cut meat here before me. Most of my customers are retired, cooking for one or two, on a fixed income. And potatoes cooked in a spoonful of good fat are the cheapest real supper I know. So get your heaviest skillet out, honey. Let us cook.
[STORY] My mother Pearl kept a coffee can on the back of the stove her whole life. Every morning my father had bacon, and every morning she poured the drippings into that can through a little piece of cloth. Nobody in our house ever bought cooking oil. She fried the potatoes in it, she greased the pans with it, she put a spoon of it in the green beans. When people came in the market and asked her how she made her potatoes taste like that, she would just point at the can.
[STORY_B] When the doctors started telling everybody that fat was the enemy, a lot of people threw their cans out. And then they started boiling everything in water, and they wondered why supper tasted like nothing. I am not telling you to eat a cup of grease, honey. One tablespoon is enough for a whole skillet of potatoes for two people. Everything I am about to tell you, I learned from my mother, and from forty years of customers coming back to tell me what went wrong.
[T01] Number one is picking the right potato, because they are not all the same. A russet is the brown one, dry and floury inside. That is the one for baking and for mashing, because it turns light and fluffy. A gold potato is in the middle. It holds its shape and it tastes like it already has butter in it, and it is the best one for the skillet. A red potato is waxy. It holds together no matter what, so it is good for potato salad and for a pot of soup.
[T01_B] If you only buy one bag, buy gold potatoes. They do everything pretty well. And look at the bag before you put it in your cart. You want them firm, with smooth skin. If you see a lot of sprouts, soft spots, or skin that is turning green, leave that bag right there. A ten pound bag of russets or golds is usually somewhere around six or seven dollars, and that is about thirty potatoes. That is twenty-some cents a potato.
[T02] Number two is how you keep them at home, because most people ruin their potatoes in the first week. Do not put them in the refrigerator. The cold turns the starch to sugar, and then they taste sweet and they burn dark in the pan before they are cooked. Keep them somewhere cool and dark, like a cupboard away from the stove, or a basket in the basement. And keep them away from your onions. Onions and potatoes side by side both go bad faster.
[T02_B] Take them out of the plastic bag and put them in a paper sack or a cardboard box with a few holes. Plastic holds the wet in and they rot from the bottom. And here is the green thing. If a potato has a green patch on the skin, that is from sitting in the light, and it tastes bitter. Cut the green part off, deep, with a good chunk around it. If the whole potato is green, throw that one away. It is not worth it.
[T03] Number three is how you get the fat in the first place. Most people cook bacon too hot and too fast. It smokes, it burns, and half the fat turns black in the pan. Put your bacon in a cold skillet. Cold, not hot. Then turn the burner to medium low and let it warm up slowly. The fat melts out of the bacon a little at a time, and the bacon gets crispy all the way across instead of burnt on the edges and chewy in the middle.
[T03_B] One pound of bacon cooked that way gives you about half a cup of clear fat, sometimes a little more if it is a fatty bacon. Half a cup is eight tablespoons. That is eight skillets of potatoes. So every pound of bacon you buy, you are getting eight suppers of cooking fat thrown in with it, and you never paid an extra penny for it.
[T04] Number four is saving it so it keeps. This is where people go wrong and then they say bacon fat tastes old. Let the fat cool for a few minutes, until it is warm but not smoking hot. Then set a coffee filter or a folded paper towel in a little strainer over a clean glass jar, and pour the fat through it. All those little burnt bits stay in the filter. Those bits are what go bad and make the fat taste sour.
[T04_B] Put the lid on and keep the jar in the refrigerator. Strained like that, it keeps about three months in the refrigerator, and much longer in the freezer. Every time you cook bacon, strain the new fat right into the same jar. And never pour it down the sink, honey. It turns hard in the pipe and the plumber is going to cost you more than all the bacon you ever bought. If it ever smells sharp or like old crayons, it is time for a new jar.
[T05] Number five is cutting them the right size. Every piece in the pan has to be the same size, or the little ones burn while the big ones are still raw in the middle. For the skillet, cut them in pieces about as big as the end of your thumb, about three quarters of an inch. Leave the skin on. The skin is where a lot of the flavor is, and it gets crispy. Just scrub them under the faucet with a brush first.
[T05_B] Now dry them. Put the cut pieces on a clean kitchen towel and pat them all over. Wet potatoes in hot fat spit at you and they steam instead of browning. Dry potatoes go brown and crispy. That one little minute with a towel is the difference between a potato that sticks to the pan and a potato that slides around like it is on ice.
[T06] Number six is the one in the title. This is how you cook a potato all the way through without a pot of water. Put your skillet on medium heat with one tablespoon of bacon fat, two if you are cooking for a family. When the fat is melted and shimmering, put the potatoes in, one layer, and sprinkle about half a teaspoon of salt over them. Stir them once so every piece gets a little shine of fat on it.
[T06_B] Now here is the trick. Pour in just a quarter cup of water, and put the lid on right away. It is going to hiss. Turn the heat down to medium low and leave the lid on for eight minutes. That little bit of water turns to steam under the lid and cooks the potatoes all the way to the middle, soft, the way boiling would. But they are not sitting in a pot of water losing all their flavor.
[T06_C] After eight minutes, take the lid off. Most of the water is gone. Turn the heat back up to medium and let the last of it cook away. Now the potatoes are frying in the bacon fat. Leave them alone. Do not stir them for four or five minutes. When the bottoms are deep golden brown, turn them over with a flat spatula and give them another four or five minutes. Twenty minutes, one skillet, one spoon of fat, and they taste like a diner on a Sunday morning.
[T07] Number seven is for when you are in a hurry or cooking for one. Take one big potato, poke it four or five times with a fork, and put it in the microwave for about four minutes, until a knife goes in with a little push. Let it cool enough to hold, cut it in pieces, and put it straight into a hot skillet with a spoon of bacon fat. It is already cooked inside, so all it needs is about eight minutes to get brown and crispy on the outside.
[T07_B] That is supper for one person, and it costs about twenty-five cents for the potato. Throw half an onion in the pan with it. If you have a little leftover ham, or one chop from yesterday, cut it up small and put it in for the last two minutes. My widowers love this one. One skillet, one fork, nothing to wash but the pan.
[T08] Number eight is the baked potato, and this is where bacon fat really shows off. Most people wrap a baked potato in foil. Do not do that. The foil keeps the steam in, and you get a soft, wet, wrinkled skin. Scrub a russet, dry it, and poke it a few times with a fork. Then rub a little bacon fat all over the skin with your fingers, just a thin coat, and roll it in coarse salt.
[T08_B] Put it right on the rack of the oven at four hundred degrees, with a pan on the rack under it to catch any drips. A medium russet takes about an hour. You know it is done when you squeeze it with a towel and it gives. The skin comes out crackly, salty, and tasting like bacon. A lot of people throw the skin away. With this one, the skin is the best part. Four hundred, an hour, no foil.
[T09] Number nine is mashed potatoes. If yours come out gluey, like wallpaper paste, it is not you. It is the water. When you boil cut-up potatoes, they soak up water like a sponge, and when you mash a wet potato, the starch breaks and turns to glue. So bake the russets instead, the same way as number eight but without the salt crust. When they are soft, cut them in half and scoop the inside out with a spoon.
[T09_B] Push the inside through a potato ricer, or mash it gently with a fork or a hand masher. Never an electric mixer. That beats it to glue in ten seconds. For two big potatoes, warm about half a cup of milk, and stir in one tablespoon of butter and one teaspoon of bacon fat. Warm milk, not cold out of the refrigerator. Cold milk makes them pasty. Salt to taste. Those are the lightest mashed potatoes you ever made, and they taste like something.
[T10] Number ten is the roasted potatoes for the oven, when you are feeding more than two. Cut golds or reds into pieces about an inch and a half, and dry them on a towel. Put your sheet pan in the oven empty while it heats up to four hundred degrees. That is the secret. A hot pan. Then toss the potatoes in a bowl with two tablespoons of melted bacon fat and a teaspoon of salt.
[T10_B] Take the hot pan out, spread the potatoes in one layer with the cut side down, and leave space between them. If they are touching, they steam each other and they come out soft. Use two pans if you need to. Roast them twenty minutes, then turn them and give them fifteen or twenty more, until they are brown and they crunch when you tap them with a spoon. A pinch of black pepper and a little dried rosemary at the end, if you like it.
[T11] Number eleven is hash browns, the real kind, and these are the ones Marvin was trying to buy in the bag. Grate one big russet on the big holes of a box grater, skin and all. Now put the grated potato in the middle of a clean kitchen towel, twist it up, and squeeze it over the sink as hard as you can. You will not believe how much water comes out. Squeeze it twice.
[T11_B] Heat one tablespoon of bacon fat in the skillet on medium until it shimmers. Spread the potato out flat and thin, sprinkle a pinch of salt, and press it down with the spatula. Then do not touch it. Six minutes. Lift one corner and peek. When it is deep brown underneath, flip it over in big pieces and give it five minutes on the other side. One potato is a big plate of hash browns for one person, about twenty-five cents.
[T12] Number twelve is cooking ahead, because I know a lot of you do not want to cook every day. Bake four or five potatoes on Sunday, all at once, while the oven is already hot. Let them cool, then keep them in the refrigerator in a covered dish. They are good for four days. Any night that week, cut one up and fry it in a spoon of bacon fat for ten minutes. It is supper faster than you can heat up a frozen bag.
[T12_B] And if you made too much mash, it freezes better than people think. Put it in a freezer bag in one cup portions, squeeze the air out, and lay it flat. To use it, let it thaw in the refrigerator overnight, then warm it in a pan with a splash of milk, stirring. Or make potato cakes. Mix one cup of cold mash with one egg and a spoon of flour, make little patties, and fry them in bacon fat until brown on both sides.
[FIX] Now, here is what goes wrong, so you know it was not you. If the potatoes stick to the pan, the pan was not hot enough when they went in, or you turned them too soon. Wait until they let go on their own. If they come out soft and pale, the pan was too crowded or you left the lid on too long. If they are brown outside and hard inside, the heat was too high and you skipped the lid and the quarter cup of water.
[FIX_B] If the bacon fat smokes in the pan, turn it down. Bacon fat does not like to be pushed as hot as oil, so keep it at medium, and keep the oven at four hundred, not higher. If your potatoes taste sweet and turn dark too fast, they were kept in the refrigerator. And if your doctor has you watching your salt, go easy on the salt, because the bacon fat already has a little in it. Taste first, then salt.
