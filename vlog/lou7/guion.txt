[N25] Number twenty-five. Save the bacon grease. Every diner had a coffee can next to the griddle. When the bacon was done, the grease got poured through a strainer into the can, and that grease cooked the eggs, the hash browns and the grilled cheese all day. A pound of butter costs you five dollars now. The grease from a pound of bacon is free, and it tastes better. Keep it in a jar in the refrigerator, it lasts for months.
[N24] Number twenty-four. Stale bread is not garbage. In my diner it became French toast in the morning, bread pudding at lunch, and bread crumbs for the meatloaf at night. Day old bread actually makes better French toast, because it soaks up the egg without falling apart. A family that throws out one loaf a week throws out about two hundred dollars a year.
[N23] Number twenty-three. Buy the potatoes in the big bag and store them right. Cool, dark, dry, and never next to the onions. Onions make potatoes sprout. In the diner the potatoes lived in a wooden bin in the back, the onions in a basket across the room. A ten pound bag kept for a month.
[N22] Number twenty-two. Cook the hash browns from yesterday's baked potatoes. Every night we baked extra potatoes and put them in the walk-in. In the morning they got grated, cold, straight onto the griddle. Cold cooked potatoes don't get gummy and they don't need to be rinsed and squeezed. Crisp every time, and nothing wasted.
[N21] Number twenty-one. The water trick for fried eggs. A diner cook cooks eggs over easy for a hundred people a morning, and he doesn't flip half of them. A tablespoon of water on the hot pan next to the eggs, and a lid on top. Thirty seconds, and the steam cooks the top. You use half the butter and you never break a yolk.
[N20] Number twenty. Wrap the bananas at the stem. Bananas go brown from the top. A little piece of plastic wrap or foil around the stems, and they last three or four extra days. And when they do go brown, they go in the freezer, peel and all, for banana bread. We never threw away a banana.
[N19] Number nineteen. Put the herbs in a glass of water. Parsley, the green onions, the cilantro. A short glass with an inch of water, like flowers, in the refrigerator with a bag loose over the top. They last two weeks instead of three days. A bunch of herbs costs two dollars. Throwing away half of every bunch adds up.
[N18] Number eighteen. Make your own breakfast sausage. A pound of ground pork, salt, black pepper, a spoon of dried sage, a pinch of red pepper flakes and a little brown sugar. Mix it, roll it into a log, and slice patties off. Ten minutes of work, half the price of the store kind, and you know what's in it.
[N17] Number seventeen. Grate your own cheese. The bag of shredded cheese costs more per pound than the block, and it has powder on it so it doesn't stick together, which is why it doesn't melt right. A block and a box grater. Cheaper, and your grilled cheese will be better.
[N16] Number sixteen. Use the whole onion. The ends and the skins go into a bag in the freezer with carrot peels and celery tops. When the bag is full, it goes into a pot with water for two hours. That's soup stock. In the diner, every soup started that way. A carton of stock at the store is three or four dollars. Yours costs nothing.
[N15] Number fifteen. Revive stale crackers and chips. Ten minutes in the oven at three hundred degrees, on a sheet pan. They come out crisp like you just opened them. Every diner did this with the saltines at the end of the week.
[N14] Number fourteen. Make the pancake batter the night before. Flour, sugar, baking powder, salt in one bowl, the eggs, milk and melted butter in another, in the refrigerator. In the morning you mix them together. Nothing from a box, and it costs you about thirty cents a batch. Rested batter makes fluffier pancakes too.
[N13] Number thirteen. Buy the whole chicken. A diner never bought chicken in pieces. A whole chicken costs about half what you pay per pound for the parts. Learn to cut it in eight pieces, it takes five minutes, and the back and the wing tips go into the stock bag. That one trick alone saves a family more than a hundred dollars a year.
[N12] Number twelve. And here's the egg trick. Crack the eggs on the flat counter, not on the edge of the bowl. On the edge, the shell pushes into the egg and you get little pieces in everything, and you crack the yolk. On a flat surface, it breaks clean. A diner cook cracks eggs with one hand, two at a time, on the flat of the griddle. Nobody ever fishes shell out of his omelet.
[N11] Number eleven. Keep the coffee grounds working. At the diner we didn't throw them out until the end of the day. They went in the garden, they took the smell out of the refrigerator in a little dish, and they scrubbed the grease off the pots. The coffee you drink is the most expensive thing you throw away every morning.
[N10] Number ten. Freeze your butter. Butter goes on sale twice a year, before the holidays. Buy six pounds and freeze them. Butter keeps in the freezer for six months and comes out like new. The diner bought it by the case when the price was low. Same trick, smaller freezer.
[N9] Number nine. The milk trick. When milk is about to turn, you don't pour it out. It goes into pancakes, biscuits or cornbread. A little sour milk makes them rise higher, it's basically buttermilk. And if you need buttermilk for a recipe, a tablespoon of vinegar in a cup of milk, five minutes, and you have it. Don't buy a quart for one recipe.
[N8] Number eight. Season your cast iron and never buy nonstick again. The diner griddle was seasoned steel, fifty years old, and nothing stuck to it. A cast iron skillet costs twenty dollars and it'll outlive you. Nonstick pans wear out every two or three years. Over a lifetime, that's hundreds of dollars.
[N7] Number seven. Use the whole loaf of meatloaf. Monday's meatloaf is Tuesday's meatloaf sandwich, fried on the griddle, and Wednesday's chili or spaghetti sauce, crumbled. That's how a diner turned one pan of ground beef into three dinners.
[N6] Number six. Cook a big pot on Sunday. Beans, chili, soup, stew. The diner had a big pot going on the back of the stove all day. One pot, one afternoon, and you have lunch for the whole week in the refrigerator. A soup lunch at home costs about a dollar. The same lunch out is twelve.
[N5] Number five. The lemon trick. Roll the lemon hard under your palm on the counter before you cut it, or warm it for ten seconds. You get almost twice the juice. A diner went through a crate of lemons a week for the iced tea and the fish fry. Half the juice wasted is half the money wasted.
[N4] Number four. Save the pasta water. That starchy water makes the sauce stick to the noodles, and it thickens gravy. We kept a pot of it on the back of the stove. Same with the potato water. It makes the best bread you ever baked.
[N3] Number three. Make your own gravy, every time. A packet of gravy mix is a dollar fifty for salt and powder. Diner gravy was drippings from the meat, two spoons of flour cooked in it until it smells like toast, and stock or milk, slowly. Five minutes. It costs about twenty cents, and nobody will ever buy the packet again in your house.
[N2] Number two. Keep a leftovers night. Every Thursday at my house was clean out the refrigerator night. Everything left from the week came out, got warmed up, and went on the table. Whatever was left after that went into an omelet on Friday morning. The average family throws away about a third of the food they buy. That night alone fixes most of it.
[N1] And number one, the big one. Cook breakfast at home. That's it. A diner breakfast, two eggs, hash browns, toast, bacon and coffee, costs you fourteen or fifteen dollars out today with the tip. Do that three mornings a week instead of going out, and you're keeping more than a thousand dollars a year in your pocket. And it'll taste like my diner did.
