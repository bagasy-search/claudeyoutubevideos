[STORY] Thirty cents a man, the boss figured in his ledger. He came into the cook shack one January with the book under his arm and said, Ole, how do you feed them for thirty cents on Thursday? [STORY] And let me tell you about the cold that January. It went to forty below one week, and stayed there. [ONE] Rule number one. Keep the bone. Whenever you have a ham, at Easter, at Christmas, a Sunday dinner, don't throw the bone away. Wrap it and freeze it. Even with all the meat carved off, there's enough left on it and inside it to flavor a whole pot of soup for eight.
[ONE] If you don't have a ham bone, a smoked ham hock works just as well. One pound. They sell them by the meat counter, and they're cheap, because most people don't know what to do with them. Or two cups of diced smoked ham, or a piece of smoked sausage. Something smoky, that's the point.
[ONE] And here's something a lot of people don't know. You can make a ham bone go twice. After the first pot, if there's still a little meat and the bone is still whole, wrap it up again and use it for a pot of beans the next week. The second pot is a little lighter, but it still tastes smoky. In the camp a ham bone did two or three pots before I gave it to the camp dog.
[TWO] Rule number two. The peas. One pound of dried green split peas. Pour them out on a sheet pan and look them over, because now and then there's a little stone in a bag. Pick it out. Then rinse them in a colander until the water runs clear.
[TWO] And here's the good news. Split peas need no soak. None. Unlike whole beans, they don't even need the ten minute hard boil. They come split in half already, and they dissolve on their own in the pot. You can decide at three in the afternoon to have pea soup for supper, and you'll have it.
[TWO] But here's the one thing to watch. Old peas. If a bag of split peas has been in your cupboard for two or three years, they may never go soft. They'll stay gritty no matter what you do. So buy them fresh, from a store that sells a lot of them, and use them within a year.
[THREE] Rule number three. The pot. Everything goes in together. The peas, the ham bone, eight cups of water, one big onion diced, two carrots diced, two stalks of celery if you have them, a bay leaf, half a teaspoon of dried thyme, and one teaspoon of kosher salt.
[THREE] Now, use the heaviest pot you have. A thin pot will scorch pea soup no matter how careful you are, because the peas sink to the bottom as they cook. In the camp it was a big iron kettle with walls as thick as my thumb. At home a Dutch oven is perfect. A thin aluminum pot is the worst.
[THREE] Bring it up to a boil, and you'll see a gray foam on top. Skim that off with a big spoon. Then turn it down to a gentle simmer, the lid cracked a little, so just a few bubbles break the surface.
[THREE] And if you don't have celery, don't go to the store just for celery. The soup is fine without it. The old camp soup was peas, ham, onion and maybe a carrot, because celery didn't keep in the cellar. Use what you have. A potato cut in small cubes is good in it too. It thickens it and it stretches it further.
[FOUR] Rule number four. And this is the one that ruins more pea soup than anything else. Stir it. Every ten minutes, for the whole hour. And not just stir it. Take a flat-edged wooden spoon and scrape the bottom of the pot, all the way across, every single time.
[FOUR] Split peas love to stick. They sink, they settle on the bottom, and if you leave them alone twenty minutes, one thin layer scorches. And one scorched layer flavors the whole pot. You can't take it back. Every bowl tastes burned.
[FOUR] In the camp, the cookee had one job on Thursday afternoon. Stir the pea soup. Every ten minutes I'd holler, stir, and he'd come running. A boy named Arne, fifteen years old, skinny as a broom handle. By the end of that winter he could stir a pot and peel potatoes at the same time. He became a fine cook himself later on.
[FIVE] Rule number five. Let it fall apart. After about an hour at that gentle simmer, the peas break down completely. The soup turns thick, and olive green, and smooth. You don't need a blender. The peas do it on their own. If you want it smoother, give it a few pushes with a potato masher.
[FIVE] Then lift the ham bone out. Let it cool a few minutes on a plate. Pull off every bit of meat you can find, and you'll find more than you think, down in the joints. Chop it small, throw away the skin, the bone and the bay leaf, and put the meat back in the pot. Ten more minutes of simmer.
[FIVE] If it's too thick, and it will get thick fast, add hot water a splash at a time until it's how you like it. Some men in my camp wanted it so thick the spoon stood up. Halvor wanted it thin enough to drink from the bowl. I made it in the middle and let them argue.
[FIVE] And the color. People worry when their pea soup comes out a dull olive green instead of bright green, like the picture on a can. That dull green is right. That's what real pea soup looks like when it's cooked slow with a ham bone. The bright green ones in the pictures are the ones that never cooked long enough to taste like anything.
[SIX] Rule number six. The finish. And this is the one nobody's mother told them. A splash of vinegar at the very end. Two teaspoons of cider vinegar, or lemon juice. Stir it in, then taste it, and add salt and pepper.
[SIX] You won't taste the vinegar. What you'll taste is the soup, suddenly brighter and more like itself. A pot of peas without a little sour at the end tastes flat and heavy, like something's missing. Two teaspoons, and it's finished. That's the difference between a pea soup people eat and a pea soup people ask for.
[SIX] And pepper at the table. Every table in my cook shack had a big tin pepper shaker, and on pea soup night those shakers got passed up and down the table until they were empty. Pea soup wants a lot of black pepper. Put it in the bowl, not the pot, so everybody can have it the way they like it.
[CORNBREAD] Now the cornbread. Because in the camp, pea soup went out with a big square of skillet cornbread, hot from the oven.
