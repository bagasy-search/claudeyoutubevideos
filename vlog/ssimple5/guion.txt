[INTRO] You know me, I'm Sachiko. I was born in Osaka in nineteen forty-eight, the fourth of five children, and I came here in nineteen seventy with my husband. [STORY] When my father retired, my mother sat down at the low table with her kakeibo, her little household money book, and a pencil. Kakeibo, it means the household account book, where every yen that comes in and every yen that goes out is written by hand. She looked at a whole year of pages. Then she drew a line under nine things. She told me, Sachiko, these nine things are taking the money and giving me nothing back. From now on, they are finished. And she kept that promise until the end.
[ONE] Number one, she stopped buying drinks in bottles. No juice in cartons, no tea in plastic bottles, no fizzy drinks for guests. Instead, every morning she boiled a big kettle of mugicha. Mugicha, it means barley tea, made from roasted barley seeds that cost almost nothing. She dropped a handful into the boiling water, let it cool on the stove, and poured it into a big glass jug in the cupboard. In summer it was cold, in winter it was warm. That jug was on her table for sixty years.
[ONE_B] Most people carry heavy bottles home from the store every week and then carry the empty bottles out again. My mother carried nothing. I make mugicha too, every morning, in the same kind of kettle. A bag of roasted barley lasts me a whole month. My grandchildren drink it when they visit, and they say it tastes like toast. In the hot months my mother also kept a second jug of plain cold water with a slice of cucumber in it, for the neighbors who came to sit on her step. Nobody ever left her house thirsty, and she never paid for a single bottle.

[TWO] Number two, she stopped buying paper towels and paper napkins. In my mother's kitchen there was always a stack of fukin. Fukin, it means a small kitchen cloth, a square of soft cotton for wiping the table, drying the cups, covering the rice. When my father's work shirts got too old, my mother cut them into squares and stitched the edges by hand. She had about twenty of them. Every night she washed them in the sink, and every morning they were dry on the line by the window.
[TWO_B] My mother said, paper is for writing letters, not for wiping tables. A roll of paper towels is gone in a week. A good cotton fukin lasts for years and gets softer every time you wash it. My mother marked her cloths with colored thread in one corner, blue for the dishes, red for the table, white for the rice, so they never got mixed up. She said a kitchen with good cloths is a kitchen with good manners.
[THREE] Number three, she stopped buying bags and wrapping paper. This is the furoshiki I showed you at the beginning. Furoshiki, it means a square wrapping cloth. In old Japan, everybody carried one. My mother could tie a furoshiki around anything. A bottle of sake, two melons, a box of sweets, her books for the calligraphy class. She tied it with two knots in ten seconds, and it became a bag with a handle.
[THREE_B] When she gave a present, she wrapped it in a furoshiki, and the person gave the cloth back the next time they met. Sometimes the same cloth went around the neighborhood for years. My mother had six of them in different colors, and the blue one was her favorite, with small white waves printed along the edge. I have it now. I take it to the store every week, and the young cashier asked me last month how to tie it. I showed her, right there at the register. Two corners tied together, then the other two, and the cloth becomes a bag. A long line of people waited behind us, and an old man said it was the most useful minute of his week.
[FOUR] Number four, she stopped buying gifts that stay. In Japan we have a word, kiemono. It means a thing that disappears. When my mother gave a present after sixty, it was always a kiemono. A box of good tea, a jar of her pickled plums, a small cake from the old shop by the station, a nice hand towel. Something the person could eat, drink or use up. Never a vase, never a figure, never a picture frame that somebody would feel bad to throw away.
[FOUR_B] My mother said, a good gift leaves the house happy, and leaves no dust. Most of us have a cupboard full of things people gave us that we never wanted and cannot throw away, because the person might visit. My mother did not want to give anybody that cupboard. Next birthday, give a jar of jam, a bag of good coffee, a pretty candle. It is a kindness twice. One year my mother gave her sister a box of twelve small soaps, each wrapped in a different paper. Her sister used one every month and wrote to her each time a new one was opened.
[FIVE] Number five, she stopped buying new clothes when the old ones could be mended. In Japan there is an old way of mending called sashiko. Sashiko, it means little stabs, small running stitches in white thread that make the cloth strong again. When the elbow of my father's work jacket wore thin, my mother put a patch of dark cotton behind it and stitched over it in little white lines, like rain. It looked so beautiful that the neighbors asked who made it.
[FIVE_B] My mother mended at night under the lamp, while the radio played. A sweater with a hole, a skirt with a loose hem, a pair of socks. My mother kept a tin of buttons from every shirt that was ever cut into cloths, hundreds of them, sorted by color in little paper twists. When a button fell off, she never bought a new one. She opened the tin, and there was always one that matched.

[SIX] Number six, she stopped buying books. My mother loved to read. Novels, history, old poems, a little magazine about flowers. But after sixty, she stopped buying them. Every Friday afternoon she walked to the little library near the temple, returned three books and took three new ones. The lady at the library knew her taste, and she kept the new books on a little shelf behind the desk with a little paper note that had my mother's name written on it in pencil.
[SIX_B] My mother said, a book you buy reads once and then sits on a shelf for thirty years. A library book reads once and then goes to the next person. [SEVEN] Number seven, she stopped buying the big bargain packs. You know them. Twenty-four rolls, the giant bag of rice, three jars for the price of two. [SEVEN_B] At the end of each month, she opened her cupboard and looked at every shelf. If something was still there from last month, she did not buy more of it until it was finished. [EIGHT] Number eight, she stopped buying things she could borrow. A ladder, a big pot for New Year, a drill to hang a picture, a folding table for guests. [EIGHT_B] My mother said, a thing you use once a year should live at the neighbor's house. When you borrow, you knock on a door, you say thank you, and you bring a little something back. Nothing was ever lost in forty years, and every borrowed thing came back with a small gift, a few rice crackers, a pear, a little bunch of flowers from somebody's yard.
[NINE] Number nine, she stopped buying anything that wanted money every month. This is the one I promised you at the beginning. After sixty, my mother looked at her kakeibo and saw small payments, every month, for things she did not need anymore. A magazine she never read, a club she never went to, an insurance for a television that was ten years old, a little monthly plan the salesman had talked her into. Each one was small. Together, they were a big bite of her pension. She added them up on one page, and she sat very still for a minute, and then she laughed at herself.
[NINE_B] So my mother made a rule. If a thing wants money from me every month, it must give me something every month. If not, it is finished. She cancelled them one by one, with a letter and a stamp. When an envelope was empty, the month was finished for that thing. She said, a thing you pay for every month is not a thing you own. It is a thing that owns you.
[MISTAKES] Now, two mistakes I see people make when they try this. The first is stopping everything at once and feeling poor. My mother never felt poor. She stopped one thing at a time, and every time, she used the money for something she loved, a new koto string, a train ticket to visit her sister. The second mistake is thinking it is about the money. It was not. It was about a lighter house and a quieter mind. She told me once, Sachiko, every thing you do not buy is one less thing to dust, one less thing to carry, and one less thing your children must decide about one day.
