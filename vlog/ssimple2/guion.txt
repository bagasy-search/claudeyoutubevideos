[INTRO] You know me, I'm Sachiko. I was born in Osaka in nineteen forty-eight, the fourth of five children, and I came to America in nineteen seventy with my husband. My mother was born in nineteen fifteen. She lived through the war and the hungry years after it, and she outlived almost everybody she knew. Come, sit with me, and I will tell you about her.
[STORY] When my father died, my mother was sixty-one. My brothers and sisters and I thought she would get small and quiet, the way some widows do. We talked on the telephone about who would take her in. Then I got a letter from her, three pages, in her beautiful handwriting. It said, do not worry about me. I have decided to stop doing some things. And she did. She stopped them one by one, over the next few years, like taking stones out of her pockets. She lived forty more years in that house. Every summer when I visited, she was a little lighter.
[ONE] Number one, she stopped rushing. My mother said, isogaba maware. It means, if you are in a hurry, take the long way around. When you rush, you drop the eggs, you miss the step, you say the wrong thing. So she left the house fifteen minutes early for everything. The market, the post office, the temple, the bus. She never ran for a bus in forty years. She said, the next bus is also a bus. When I was young I thought she was just slow. Now I understand. She was never late, and she never fell. She did the same with her words. When somebody asked her a hard question, she counted to three in her head before she answered. One, two, three. In those three seconds, she said, most foolish answers walk away by themselves.
[ONE_B] I do the same here. If my appointment is at ten, I leave at nine fifteen. If I am early, I sit in the car and I listen to the radio, or I look at the trees. Nobody ever got hurt sitting in a car looking at a tree. Rushing is what old people do when they forget they are old.

[TWO] Number two, she stopped buying things on the first day. When my mother saw something she wanted in a shop window, a new teapot, a shawl, a radio, she did not buy it that day. She went home and wrote it in her kakeibo. Kakeibo, it means the household money book. It is a little notebook where every yen that comes in and every yen that goes out is written down by hand, every night. Japanese housewives have kept them for more than a hundred years. If she still wanted the thing after three days, and it fit in the book, she went back and bought it. Most of the time, she did not want it anymore. She said, the shop will still be there. The money might not.
[TWO_B] I keep a kakeibo too, a plain notebook from the drugstore. Every night after supper, I write what I spent that day. It takes three minutes. At the end of the month I look at it with a cup of tea, and every month I find something I do not need to pay for anymore. Writing it by hand is the trick. When you write it, you feel it. When you tap a card, you feel nothing. On the first page of every month, she wrote four questions, the same four that every kakeibo asks. How much money do I have? How much do I want to save? How much am I spending? And how can I do better? She answered them in pencil, in two or three words each. That was her whole money plan for sixty years.
[THREE] Number three, she stopped sitting still in the morning. At six thirty every morning, the radio in the park played the same music, and everyone in the neighborhood came out to do rajio taiso. Rajio taiso, it means radio exercises. It is a few minutes of simple stretching and arm swinging to music, and Japan has done it on the radio since nineteen twenty-eight. Children, mothers, old men in their undershirts, everyone. My mother went every morning until she was ninety-six. She stood in the second row, always the same place, next to the lady from the tofu shop. In summer the children got a stamp on a little card for every morning they came, and at the end of August, a pencil or a notebook as a prize. We children stopped going when school started again. My mother never stopped.
[THREE_B] When she could not go to the park anymore, she did it in her kitchen, holding the edge of the table. I do it here too, every morning, in my living room, with the music on my grandson's tablet. It is gentle, nothing fancy, and you can do most of it holding a chair. Ten minutes in the morning, and the whole day starts moving.
[FOUR] Number four, she stopped trying to win. My mother said, makeru ga kachi. It means, to lose is to win. When my brother argued with her about politics, or my sister told her she was doing the laundry wrong, she just smiled and said, maybe you are right. Then she went on doing the laundry her way. She told me once, when you are sixty, you have already won enough arguments. Now you can let the young ones be right. It costs nothing, and the house stays quiet. And she noticed something. When she stopped fighting, people started asking for her opinion. My brother, the same one who argued about politics, came to her twenty years later to ask what he should do about his own son.
[FOUR_B] I have practiced this one for years, and it is the hardest one on the list. When my son tells me how to use my telephone, I say, maybe you are right. When my neighbor tells me my roses need more sun, I say, maybe you are right. Then I go inside and make tea, and I feel lighter than if I had won.
[FIVE] Number five, she stopped going to sleep with worries. Next to her pillow she kept a small notebook and a pencil. Every night, before she turned off the light, she wrote down whatever was worrying her. The rent, my brother's job, the roof, a letter she had not answered. Then she closed the notebook and said out loud, tomorrow. The worries were on the paper now, not in her head, and the paper could wait until morning. She kept that notebook by her pillow for almost forty years. When we cleaned out her house, we found a whole box of them. I read some on the airplane home. Most of the worries I did not even remember. The roof was fixed in nineteen seventy-eight. My brother found a job. The letter got answered. Forty years of worries, and almost all of them came out fine.

[SIX] Number six, she stopped saying, I am too old. At seventy, my mother started shodo, Japanese calligraphy, with a brush and black ink, at the community center. At eighty, she started writing haiku, little poems of seventeen sounds, and she sent one to me in every letter. At ninety, she learned to make origami cranes for the children at the temple school. She was never very good at any of them. That was not the point. We have a word for it, ikigai. It means, the reason you get up in the morning. [SIX_B] I am seventy-nine, and last year I started learning to play the ukulele. My grandson laughs at me, and my fingers hurt, and I play very badly. But every morning, I want to get up and try again. That is ikigai.
[SIX_C] One of the haiku my mother sent me, I still have on my refrigerator. She wrote it in Japanese, and I put it in English for you. Old woman's teacup. Steam rises in winter light. [SEVEN] Number seven, she stopped staying home alone all day. Every morning after rajio taiso, my mother walked to the market, even if she only needed one onion. She said good morning to the fish man, the tofu lady, the boy at the rice shop. She had one rule: one real conversation every day, with someone who is not family. In the afternoon she sat on the bench outside her house, and the neighbors stopped to talk. When she was ninety-nine, the mailman still stopped his bicycle to ask her about her haiku. [SEVEN_B] Here it is harder, because everybody drives. So I go to the same little grocery store at the same time every Tuesday and Friday, and I know the cashiers by name. [EIGHT] Number eight, she stopped doing everything alone. When she was younger, my mother never asked anybody for anything. After seventy, she changed. A high school girl from next door started carrying her rice home from the market. In return, my mother taught the girl calligraphy every Sunday. We have a word for this too, otagai sama. It means, we are all in this together. You help me, I help you, and nobody owes anybody. My mother said, letting someone help you is also a gift to them. It makes them feel needed, and everybody wants to be needed, at any age. So she learned to say one sentence without being ashamed: would you help me with this, please? Then she always found something to give back.
[EIGHT_B] I learned this one late. For years after my husband passed, I climbed the ladder to change the light bulbs myself. Then a young couple moved in next door. Now the husband does my light bulbs, and I watch their little girl after school on Thursdays. Otagai sama. Nobody is keeping score.
[NINE] Number nine is the one sitting in your cupboard. My mother had a set of good teacups, thin white porcelain with blue flowers, a wedding present from nineteen thirty-nine.
