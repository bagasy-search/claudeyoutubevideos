[WHY] Now, why potatoes? Because in a logging camp the potato was the one thing you never ran out of. They came in by the wagon load in the fall, a hundred pound sacks, and they went down into the root cellar dug into the hill behind the cook shack. Dark, cool, never froze. Those potatoes lasted us until the ice went out in the spring.
[WHY] And a potato is a remarkable thing, friend. It's cheap, it fills you up, it has more in it than people think, and it takes on the flavor of whatever you cook it with. A cook who knows potatoes can feed a crew through any winter. A cook who doesn't know potatoes is just boiling water.
[ONE] Secret number one. The potato. People think a potato is a potato. It isn't. The waxy little red ones and the yellow ones hold their shape, and that's wonderful in a salad, but in a soup they stay hard and the broth stays thin.
[ONE] You want the big plain brown ones, the baking potatoes, or the all-purpose kind. Those are full of starch, and when they cook down, that starch thickens the soup all by itself. No flour, no cream, no can of anything. The potato does the work.
[ONE] For a pot that feeds six, two pounds of potatoes. That's about four or five big ones. Peel them, cut them in cubes about the size of a sugar cube, a little bigger is fine. In the camp I used a whole sack for one pot, and Lars and I would sit on the bench by the stove and peel for an hour, and he'd tell me about every girl in Duluth.
[ONE] And here's an old trick. Keep the cubes in a bowl of cold water until the onions are ready. They won't turn brown, and you'll see the water go cloudy. That cloudy is starch. Some cooks poured it off. I didn't. It's thickener.
[TWO] Secret number two. The onions. This is where almost every recipe goes wrong. They tell you to cook the onions five minutes until they're soft. Five minutes is not enough. Five minutes gives you a sharp soup that tastes raw.
[TWO] Three big onions, sliced thin. In a heavy pot over medium low. A pinch of salt on them right away, because the salt pulls the water out. And then you leave them alone, friend. Stir them now and then. Fifteen minutes. Twenty minutes. Until they're soft and golden and the whole kitchen smells sweet.
[TWO] Big Swede used to come into the cook shack in the afternoon just to stand by the stove and smell the onions. He'd say, Ole, I don't care what's for supper, just make it smell like this. Those slow onions are the flavor of the whole soup. Everything else just carries it.
[TWO] If they start to stick, don't turn up the heat. Turn it down, add a spoon of water, and scrape the bottom. The brown that comes off the bottom of the pot goes right into the soup. That's flavor you already paid for.
[THREE] Secret number three. The fat. Three tablespoons of butter is fine. But the camp way was bacon drippings, or a piece of salt pork cut in little cubes and fried first, until the cubes were crisp and the fat was all in the pot.
[THREE] And here's why it matters for a working man. Fat is what keeps you full. A soup of only potatoes and water fills you up for an hour, and then you're hungry again. Put a little fat in it and it sits with you until morning. The old cooks didn't know the science, but they knew which soup kept a man quiet at night.
[THREE] You don't need much meat. Remember this, friend. Meat flavors the pot, it doesn't fill the belly. Four ounces of salt pork or two strips of bacon, crisped up, is plenty for the whole pot of six. Fish the crisp bits out, keep them on a plate, and put them back on top of the bowls at the end.
[FOUR] Secret number four. The water and the salt. Six cups of water, or chicken broth if you have it. In the camp it was water, and it was good. If you use water, you'll need more salt, about two teaspoons for the whole pot, and half a teaspoon of black pepper.
[FOUR] Now, most people salt at the end, and the soup tastes flat. Salt the onions at the start, then put the rest in with the potatoes, so the potatoes cook in salty water and get seasoned all the way to the middle. You taste again at the very end, but most of the salt goes in early.
[FOUR] Bring it to a boil, then turn it down to a quiet simmer. A gentle bubble, not a rolling boil. A hard boil beats the potatoes into paste on the outside while the inside is still hard. A gentle simmer cooks them evenly, all the way through.
[FIVE] Secret number five. When it's done, and how to mash it. Twenty minutes or so of simmer. You don't look at the clock. You take one cube out, press it with the back of a spoon against the side of the pot. If it falls apart with no fight, it's done.
[FIVE] Now here's where the glue comes from. People pour the whole pot in a blender and run it for a minute, and the starch goes gummy and the soup turns to wallpaper paste. Don't do that. Take a plain potato masher, right in the pot, and mash it rough. Ten or twelve pushes.
[FIVE] You want half of it broken down into a thick creamy broth, and the other half still in soft chunks you can bite. If you like it smoother, scoop out two ladles, mash those fine, and stir them back in. But leave some texture. A soup with something to chew holds a man longer than a soup he drinks.
[SIX] Secret number six. The milk. One cup of milk, stirred in at the very end. And this is important. Once the milk goes in, the soup must never boil again. Heat it gently until it steams, three to five minutes, and stop right there.
[SIX] If you boil it after the milk, the milk curdles. Little white grains all through the soup, and it looks spoiled even though it isn't. I did that once, my first winter, and Halvor took one look at the pot and asked me if the cow was sick. I never boiled the milk again.
[SIX] In the camp, milk was canned most of the winter, the evaporated kind in the little cans. Half a can and half a can of water, and it worked just the same. If you only have powdered milk, that works too. It's the one thing in this soup you can't skip, because it makes it taste like a soup and not like potato water.
[SEVEN] And secret number seven. The bread. This is the one that kept forty men full until morning. Every bowl went out with a heel of bread beside it. The heel, the end of the loaf, the hard part nobody wants.
[SEVEN] And the men didn't eat it on the side. They tore it up and dropped it in the soup and let it soak. The old bread drinks the soup and swells up, and suddenly that bowl of soup is a meal that sits in you like a supper. Stale bread is better than fresh for this. Fresh bread goes mushy. Stale bread holds.
[SEVEN] So don't throw out the ends of your bread this week. Put them in a paper bag on the counter. By Friday they're perfect for this soup. That's the camp way. Nothing wasted, and the cheapest thing in the house ends up being the best part of the bowl.
[SEVEN] Nels, our teamster, used to save his bread heel from breakfast in his coat pocket all day, out in the cold with the horses, so it would be hard as a board by supper. He said soup bread had to be earned. The cookee laughed at him every night. Then one night the cookee tried it Nels's way, and after that there was always a little row of bread heels drying on the warming shelf above my stove.
[VARIATIONS] Now, three ways to change it once you've got the plain one down. The first is the salt pork version I made at thirty below. Crisp the pork cubes first, cook the onions in that fat, and put the crisp bits on top of each bowl. It's the heartiest of the three.
[VARIATIONS] The second is with leeks. If you can find leeks cheap, use one leek in place of one of the onions. Slice it, wash it well, because leeks hold sand, and cook it slow with the onions. It makes the soup softer and sweeter. Toivo said it tasted like Sunday.
[VARIATIONS] And the third is my mother's thick version, the one she made when there were too many at the table. She grated one raw potato straight into the pot in the last ten minutes. The grated potato melts and thickens the whole pot so much you can almost eat it with a fork. Then a spoon of chopped chives or parsley on top, if you have it.
[SERVE] And let me tell you how it went out to the table, because the serving matters too. In the camp the soup went into big white enamel bowls, the heavy kind with the blue rim, and the bowls sat on the warming shelf for ten minutes before supper so the soup didn't go cold the second it hit them. A hot bowl keeps a soup hot to the last spoon.
[SERVE] At home, run your bowls under hot water from the tap for a minute and dry them before you ladle. It sounds like nothing. But a soup that stays hot tastes better, and people eat it slower, and they feel full sooner. Put the crisp salt pork on top, a little black pepper, and the bread on the side for tearing.
[SERVE] And in the camp there was a rule, no talking at the table. The men ate fast and they ate quiet. But on potato soup nights you'd hear the spoons scraping the bottom of those enamel bowls all down the long table, and that sound, friend, that was the best review a cook ever got.
[TROUBLE] If something goes wrong, it isn't you. If the soup is thin, you used the waxy potatoes, or you didn't mash enough. Mash some more and give it ten minutes. If it's gluey, it was blended too long. If it's sharp, the onions were rushed. And if it curdled, it boiled after the milk went in.
[TROUBLE] And one more thing people ask me. Can you make it ahead? Yes. Make the soup up to the point before the milk, and keep it in the refrigerator. When it's time for supper, warm it gently, stir in the milk, and it's ready in ten minutes. In the camp I'd start the onions after the breakfast dishes and the pot sat on the back of the range all afternoon, barely warm, until the men came in.
