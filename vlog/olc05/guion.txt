[ONE] Step one. Judge the pan before you bring it home. And this is the step that saves you from wasting a whole afternoon. Set the pan on a flat table and try to spin it. If it rocks, the bottom is warped, and a warped pan will never heat evenly. Walk away.
[ONE] Then run your finger across the cooking surface. Orange rust, thick black crust, old sticky grease, those can all be fixed. But if you feel deep pits, real little craters all over where you cook, that pan has been eaten into, and you should skip it.
[ONE] And then the old test. Hold the pan by the handle and tap the rim with your knuckle. A clear ring, like a bell, means the iron is sound. A dull thud can mean there's a crack you can't see. My old skillet rang like a church bell in that barn. Big Swede would have said it was singing.
[ONE] And one more thing to check. Look at the bottom, the outside. Old skillets often have a ring around the bottom edge, or numbers and letters cast into the iron. That's fine, that's just how they were made. What you don't want is a crack running in from the rim. Hold it up to the light and look along the edge. Even a hairline crack will open up when the pan gets hot.
[TWO] Step two. Start gentle. Most people go straight to the strongest thing they can find. Don't. Hot water, a stiff brush or a pad of steel wool, and a little dish soap. That's all. Scrub it in circles. You'll be surprised, most rust and old grease comes off with nothing but elbow work.
[TWO] And yes, I said soap. People will tell you soap ruins cast iron. That rule is left over from the old days, when soap was made with lye and it was harsh enough to strip a pan. A little mild dish soap today won't hurt a thing. Especially not on a pan you're about to strip down anyway.
[THREE] Step three, for the stubborn rust. White vinegar and water, half and half, in a tub or a sink, enough to cover the pan. Lay it in. And now here's the part everybody gets wrong. Thirty minutes to an hour, and no longer. Not overnight.
[THREE] Vinegar is an acid. It eats the rust, and that's what you want. But leave the pan in there overnight and it starts to eat the iron itself, and you'll pull out a pan that's rough and pitted where it used to be smooth. So set a timer. Check it every fifteen minutes. When you see bare gray iron coming through, take it out.
[THREE] Wear gloves for this, and never, ever mix vinegar with bleach. The fumes can hurt you. Just the vinegar and water, a timer, and patience. My skillet sat in the bath forty minutes, and when I lifted it out, the rust came off under the steel wool in long orange streaks, and there was gray iron underneath. Gunnar's pan was still in there.
[FOUR] Step four. Dry it right now. Not in ten minutes. Right now. Because bare iron, with no seasoning on it, will rust again in a few minutes. You'll see a little orange bloom come up while you watch, and you'll have to start again.
[FOUR] Rinse it off, wipe it with a towel, and then put it straight on a burner over low heat, or into a warm oven, until every last drop of water is gone. In the camp I set it on the back of the wood range, and it dried in a minute. When the pan is bare, gray and bone dry, you're ready for the important part.
[FOUR] And here's a thing about bare iron that surprised me the first time. When it's stripped all the way down, it's not black at all. It's gray, like a stone or like an old nail. People think they've ruined it, because they've never seen a skillet that color. That gray is exactly right. That's clean iron, waiting for its first coat.
[FIVE] Step five. The seasoning. And this is the step that makes or breaks the whole job. Seasoning is just oil baked into the iron until it turns hard and black and slick. And the whole trick, friend, is one word. Thin.
[FIVE] Warm the pan a little so the iron opens up. Then put a very small amount of oil on a paper towel, canola or plain vegetable oil is fine, and rub it all over the pan. Inside, outside, the handle, the bottom. And then take a clean towel and wipe it all off again, as if you made a mistake. You want the pan to look nearly dry.
[FIVE] That's where people go wrong. They pour on a big coat of oil, thick and shiny, thinking more is better. And the oil can't harden all the way, and it comes out sticky and gummy, and they think they did it wrong and give up. Thin is the whole secret. Wipe it off like you made a mistake.
[FIVE] Now put the pan upside down in your oven at four hundred fifty to five hundred degrees, with a sheet of foil on the rack below it to catch any drip. One hour. Then turn the oven off and let the pan cool inside. There will be a little smoke. Open a window. If the smoke alarm goes off, your coat was too thick.
[FIVE] And then do it again. Three to five coats, one after another, each one thin. Every coat goes a little darker. After the first one my old skillet was the color of coffee with milk. After the third it was brown. After the fifth it was black and smooth as a church pew.
[FIVE] In the camp, of course, I didn't have an oven with a dial. I seasoned a pan on the wood range. A thin coat of lard, then the pan upside down on the back of the range overnight, where the heat was steady. In the morning it was dark and hard. It's the same idea. Thin coat, steady heat, a long time. A modern oven just makes it easier.
[FIVE] And which oil? Any plain cooking oil with a high smoke point works. Canola, vegetable oil, shortening, even lard. Some people swear by flaxseed oil, and it does make a hard coat, but it costs more. Don't use butter or olive oil for seasoning. They burn before they harden. Save those for cooking.
[SIX] Step six, and this one is the best step. Cook in it. The best seasoning in the world is the one that comes from cooking. Every time you fry something in a little fat, the pan gets another thin coat. That's why a camp skillet that fried a hundred flapjacks a morning was slicker than any new pan in a store.
[SIX] For the first few times, cook something with fat. Fry some bacon, or potatoes in drippings. Not tomato sauce, not anything sour for the first couple of weeks, because the acid can pull off a young seasoning.

[TROUBLE] If something goes wrong, it isn't you. If the pan is sticky, the oil was too thick. Bake it upside down at four fifty for another hour and it usually hardens.
