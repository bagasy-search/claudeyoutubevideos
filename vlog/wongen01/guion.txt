Secret number one. The rice must sleep.

Every afternoon at three o'clock, when the lunch rush was finished, I made the rice for tomorrow. Not for today. For tomorrow. Twenty cups of long grain jasmine rice into the big rice cooker. Then I spread it out on the sheet trays, thin, maybe one inch, and the trays go into the walk-in fridge, no cover for the first hour. Nobody touches it. Mr. Lau used to say, fresh rice is for eating, old rice is for frying.

Here is what actually happens. Fresh cooked rice is full of water, and the starch on the outside of every grain is soft and sticky, like glue. You put that in a hot pan, the water comes out, the rice steams instead of fries, and the glue sticks every grain to its neighbor. That is your mush. But when the rice sleeps one night in the cold, two things happen. The water on the outside dries off. And the starch inside the grain goes firm again. Scientists call it retrogradation. I call it the rice wakes up hard. Now every grain is alone. Separate. It can fry.

So at home, one cup of jasmine rice, rinse it three times until the water is almost clear, and cook it with a little less water than the bag says. One cup rice, one cup water, and a little more, just one tablespoon. Spread it on a plate or a tray, let the steam go off, and in less than one hour it goes into the fridge. Uncovered for one hour, then cover it. Next day, you have fried rice rice.

And this is important, my friend, so I say it slow. Do not leave cooked rice sitting on the counter all afternoon. Warm rice on the counter is a party for bacteria. In the restaurant, the health inspector came every year, and the first thing he looked at was the rice trays. Cool it fast, fridge in one hour, and use it in one or two days.

Now, I know what you are thinking. Uncle Wong, I want fried rice tonight, not tomorrow. Okay. Restaurant trick for the emergency. Cook the rice with less water, spread it very thin on a big tray, put a fan on it for ten minutes, then into the freezer for twenty minutes, no cover. Not perfect. But ninety percent. Mr. Lau did this on Chinese New Year when we ran out. He never told anybody either.

And which rice? Long grain. Jasmine is what we used, a fifty pound bag every three days. Long grain has less of the sticky starch, so the grains stay separate. Short grain rice, sushi rice, the round one, it is made to stick together. Beautiful for sushi. Terrible for fried rice. Basmati works too, a little drier, a little different smell. If you only have short grain at home, rinse it five times instead of three, and use even a little less water.

One more thing before the rice goes in the pan. Wet your fingers with a little water and break every clump with your hands. Squeeze it gently, like this, until it falls like sand. If you put a clump in the wok, the clump stays a clump. The wok will not fix it for you.

Secret number two. Your stove is lying to you.

In the restaurant, under the wok, there is a burner like a jet engine. When you open it, it roars. You hear it from the street. A home stove burner, the big one, is maybe ten thousand, maybe fifteen thousand BTU. The restaurant wok burner is one hundred thousand, sometimes more. Ten times the fire. So when you watch a cook on the internet and you do exactly the same thing at home, it does not come out the same. Your stove cannot do it.

So you have to cheat. Three ways.

First, the pan. If you have a carbon steel wok, good. If you don't, use your cast iron skillet, the heavy one. Heavy iron holds heat like a stone. Thin nonstick pan, when the cold rice hits it, the temperature falls down and never comes back. Then you are boiling rice, not frying.

Second, heat the pan empty. Long time. Five minutes on high, until you see a little smoke coming off the metal. Hold your hand over it, one hand high. If you can keep your hand there for more than two seconds, it is not ready.

Third, and this is the one everybody gets wrong. Small batches. In the restaurant I cooked four orders in one wok, because I had a jet engine. At home, never more than two cups of rice in the pan. Two cups, that's two plates. If you are feeding six people, you cook three times. It takes four minutes each time. It's worth it. When you crowd the pan, everything steams. Steam is the enemy. Remember this: if you hear the pan go quiet, you put too much.

Secret number three. Wok hei. The breath of the wok.

You know the taste. The smoky taste in takeout fried rice, a little bit like barbecue. You can never get it at home and you don't know what it is. In Cantonese we say wok hei. The breath of the wok.

Here is what actually happens. When the pan is very hot and you toss the rice, tiny drops of oil fly up, they touch the flame, and they burn for a split second. That smoke falls back on the rice. At the same time, the sugar and the starch on the outside of the grains toast, like the edge of bread in a toaster. That toasting, the color and the smell, that is the browning. Both together, that's wok hei.

Friday night in the restaurant, two hundred orders. The ticket machine never stops, the fan on the roof is screaming, and the kitchen is so hot the sweat goes in your eyes. You see this mark on my arm? That is the wok, nineteen ninety-one, a Friday. Every old cook has these. But on Friday night, the fried rice was the best of the whole week. Because the wok never had one second to cool down. Hot, hot, hot, all night.

Now, your stove has no big flame coming up the side. So don't toss like a crazy person. I see people on video throwing the rice in the air at home. The rice comes down cold. Every time you lift the pan off the fire, you lose heat. At home, you do the opposite. Spread the rice flat in the pan, press it down gently with the spatula, and leave it alone. Count to twenty. Listen. You hear crackling, little pops, like rain on a tin roof. That is the rice toasting. Then turn it, spread it, press it, wait again. Three, four times. You get the toasted edges, the little crispy grains. That is your wok hei at home. Not a hundred percent of the restaurant. But close enough that your family asks you which restaurant you ordered from.

And the oil. Use peanut oil, or any oil that can take high heat. Peanut oil can go to about four hundred fifty degrees before it burns. Olive oil, extra virgin, no. Butter, no. They burn before your pan is hot enough. Two tablespoons of oil for two cups of rice. Not more. Fried rice should not be greasy. If there is oil at the bottom of the plate when you finish, you used too much.

Secret number four. The egg goes in first, and it comes out first.

In my first week I put the egg on top of the rice. Mr. Lau slapped the spatula out of my hand. Twice. He said, the egg is not a sauce.

Here is how we did it. Hot pan, a little oil, two eggs beaten with a pinch of salt. Pour them in. Count to five. Now push them softly, big soft curds, and when they are still a little wet on top, take them out of the pan and put them on a plate. Seventy percent cooked. They finish cooking later, with the rice. If you leave the egg in the pan the whole time, it becomes little rubber crumbs. If you pour it raw on top of the rice, it coats everything and makes the rice wet again, and you lose all the work you did.

There is another way, the golden fried rice, where you mix the raw yolk into the cold rice before it goes in the pan, and every grain comes out yellow like gold. That one is beautiful. It's a different video. Today we do the takeout way.

Secret number five. The two soy sauces.

This is the one Mr. Lau really did not want people to know. You go home, you buy a bottle of soy sauce, you pour it in, and your rice tastes too salty but it is still pale. Or you keep pouring for the color and now it is wet and salty and brown mush. Sound familiar?

The restaurant uses two. Light soy sauce for the salt and the flavor. Dark soy sauce for the color. Dark soy is thicker, a little sweet, almost like molasses, and very little salt compared to the color it gives. One teaspoon of dark soy makes the whole pan of rice that takeout brown. So for two cups of rice, one tablespoon of light soy sauce and one teaspoon of dark. That's all. That's the color you see in the white box.

And here is the second part of the secret. Where you pour it. Never in the middle, on the rice. You pour it on the hot side of the pan, on the metal, around the edge. It hits the iron, it sizzles, it bubbles, it gets a little burnt, a little caramel. Then you mix it into the rice. That sizzle is flavor. Pour it on the rice in the middle, it just makes the rice wet.

Secret number six. The little white powders.

Every takeout kitchen has a row of containers next to the wok. Salt. Sugar. White pepper. And MSG. People make a face when I say MSG. My friend, MSG is in tomatoes, in parmesan cheese, in mushrooms. It is the same savory taste. In the restaurant we used a small pinch per order. At home, for two cups of rice, a quarter teaspoon of MSG, a quarter teaspoon of sugar, and a good pinch of white pepper. White pepper, not black. White pepper is the smell of a Chinese kitchen. If you don't want MSG, okay, it is your kitchen, a half teaspoon of chicken bouillon powder does almost the same job.

The sugar, you don't taste it as sweet. It just rounds the salt, like a little cushion. And the salt, careful. The soy sauce is already salty. Taste first, then salt.

Secret number seven. The last ten seconds.

The very last thing, after the pan is off the fire. Half a teaspoon of toasted sesame oil, and the green part of the scallions. Off the heat. Sesame oil is for smell, not for cooking. In a hot pan it burns and turns bitter in seconds. And the scallions. You cut them in two parts. The white part goes in early, with the oil, to perfume it. The green part goes in at the very end, so it stays bright green and fresh. In the restaurant, if a box of fried rice went out with brown, sad scallions on top, Mr. Lau opened it, looked at me, and closed it again. He didn't have to say anything.

Now, the three mistakes. I see them every time somebody shows me a picture of their fried rice.

Mistake number one. Too much soy sauce. People think the color comes from more soy sauce, so they pour and pour. The rice gets wet, salty, and the bottom of the pan turns to brown paste. One tablespoon light, one teaspoon dark, for two cups. If you want more color, a few drops more dark soy, not more light.

Mistake number two. Stirring all the time. You are nervous, so you stir and stir and stir. The rice never touches the hot metal long enough to toast. It just goes around and around and gets warm. Press, wait, listen, turn. Patience is the secret ingredient nobody sells you.

Mistake number three. Everything in the pan at the same time. Rice, eggs, peas, sauce, all together, because you are hungry. Now the pan is cold and full of water, and you made a rice stew. Everything goes in its own time. Egg first and out. Aromatics. Meat. Rice. Vegetables. Sauce on the edge. Finish.

And one for free, because I like you. If your rice is sticking to the pan, the pan was not hot enough when the oil went in, or your pan is too thin. Don't add more oil to fix it. Next time, heat the empty pan longer.
