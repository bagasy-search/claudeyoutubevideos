So I built the coop myself. It cost me two hundred eighty dollars. It's still standing. Thirty years of Indiana winters, two tornado warnings, and one very determined raccoon. And it's the coop you see behind me every week.

And what I got wrong, the things that rotted, the things I'd skip, and the one mistake that cost me four hens before I fixed it. If you're thinking about building a coop this spring, write this down before you buy a single board.
[INTRO] I'm Opal. Fifty years with hens on the same little farm in Indiana. Every week I tell you what actually happened in my coop, what I did, what it cost me, and how it turned out. I'm not a vet, and I'll always tell you when it's time to call one.

[STORY] Let me tell you about those eleven chicks. They were straight run from the feed store in town, a dollar and a quarter each. Four turned out to be roosters, which is about what you get. One of the pullets was a Buff Orpington I named Lucy. She was the first one to walk into that coop the day I finished it, and she lived to be twelve. Every time I go out there, I still think of it as Lucy's coop.
[STORY] I built it on weekends, by myself, with a circular saw, a drill, a level, and a book from the library. My husband sat in a lawn chair and told me what I was doing wrong. He was right about half the time. It took me six weekends. The chicks were half grown by the time they moved in, and they'd been in my laundry room so long the whole house smelled like a feed store.
[PLACE] Before you build anything, think about where it goes. I put mine on the highest spot in the yard, so the water runs away from it in a storm, not under it. On the east side of the big walnut tree, so it gets morning sun and afternoon shade. And close enough to the house that I can see it from the kitchen window, and hear it at night. If something's bothering your hens at two in the morning, you want to hear it.
[PLACE] And don't put it right on your property line, next to your neighbor's patio. Mine is a good hundred feet from the road and from anybody's house but mine. Chickens are noisy in the morning, and a coop has a smell, even a clean one, especially in the summer. Your neighbors will like your hens a lot better if they're not right up against the fence.
[SIZE] First thing, the size. My coop is eight feet by six feet inside, and the run is eight by twenty. I built it for eleven hens, and that was too many. The rule I use now is four square feet per hen inside the coop, and ten square feet per hen in the run. So my coop is right for twelve on paper, but in real life, seven or eight hens are happy in there. Crowded hens peck each other, and they get sick more.
[SIZE] And build it tall enough for you. My coop is six feet tall at the low side of the roof, so I can stand up inside to clean it. The first one I ever built, back in the seventies, was four feet tall, and I cleaned it on my knees for six years. Never again. If you can't stand up in it, you won't clean it as often as you should.
[MONEY94] Here's what I spent in nineteen ninety-four, from my notebook. Lumber, about a hundred and thirty dollars. Tin for the roof, forty. Hardware cloth, thirty-five. Hinges, a latch, screws and nails, twenty-five. Paint, thirty. And the window and the nest boxes I made out of scrap from the barn. Two hundred eighty, give or take. Today, the same coop would cost me about nine hundred to eleven hundred dollars in materials. Lumber has gone up that much.
[COPY] Now, what I'd copy. Number one is the floor. My coop sits up on concrete blocks, about a foot and a half off the ground. That does three things. Rats and snakes can't live under it, because there's nothing to hide in. The floor stays dry, because air moves under it. And the hens get a shady spot underneath in the summer, out of the sun and the rain. I would do that again exactly.
[COPY] Number two is the hardware cloth. Not chicken wire. Chicken wire keeps chickens in, and it keeps nothing out. A raccoon can reach right through it, and tear it open with its hands. I used half inch hardware cloth on every window and every vent, screwed down with washers, not stapled. And I buried it along the bottom of the run, a foot deep and a foot out, in an L shape, so nothing can dig under. A roll today is about seventy dollars, and it's the best seventy dollars you'll spend.
[COPY] Number three, the roosts are higher than the nest boxes. Hens want to sleep in the highest place they can find. If your nest boxes are higher than your roost bars, they'll sleep in the nest boxes, and they'll poop in them all night, and you'll have dirty eggs every morning. My roosts are about three feet up, and the nest boxes are about eighteen inches up. Simple, and it works.
[COPY] Number four, the nest boxes open from the outside. I put a hinged lid on the back of the nest boxes, so I can lift it up from outside the coop and pick up the eggs without going in. In the winter, with snow on the ground, that's a gift. And one nest box for every four hens is plenty. I built four for eleven hens, and they all fight over the same one anyway. They always do.
[COPY] Number five, the cleanout door. On the back wall, down at floor level, I put a big door, three feet wide, that opens all the way. When I clean the coop, I rake the old bedding right out that door into the wheelbarrow. Without it, you're carrying shavings out one shovel at a time through the people door. That door saves me an hour every time.
[VENT] Number six, and this one's important. Ventilation. Up high, under the roof, on both sides, I left a gap about six inches tall, the whole length of the coop, covered with hardware cloth. It's open all year, even in January. People think you should close a coop up tight in the winter to keep it warm. That's backwards. Hens breathe out a lot of moisture, and their droppings are wet. If that moisture can't get out, it settles on their combs at night, and that's how you get frostbite. A dry coop is a warm coop. Vents up high, no draft down where they sleep.
[VENT] I lost two hens to frostbite the first winter, before I understood that. I'd stuffed rags in the vents to keep the cold out. Their combs turned black at the tips. I pulled the rags out, and I never had it again. That was a hard lesson.
[SKIP] Now, what I'd skip, or do different. Number one, the plywood floor. I put down plain plywood, and by year eight it was soft in the corner under the waterer. I replaced it, and the second one went the same way. If I did it again, I'd put a sheet of vinyl flooring over the plywood, the cheap kind, and run it a few inches up the walls. Water can't soak in, and it scrapes clean. A piece big enough for my coop is about sixty dollars.
[SKIP] Number two, the waterer inside the coop. I had it inside for years. Hens drink at night only a little, but they knock it over, they splash it, and that's what rotted my floor. Now the water lives outside, in the run, under the overhang. In the winter, on a heated base. I wish I'd done that from day one.
[SKIP] Number three, the window that opened. I put in an old house window that slid up and down, from my mother's house. It looked so nice. It also let in a draft I could never stop, right at roost height. I finally screwed it shut and put hardware cloth over a vent up high instead. If you want light, put the window up high, and don't make it open, or put it on the side away from where they sleep.
[SKIP] Number four, the paint. I painted it barn red, it looks so pretty, and I've repainted it four times. Thirty dollars of paint, every seven or eight years, plus a weekend on a ladder. If I did it again, I'd still paint it. But I'd spend more on a good primer the first time, and I'd put a drip edge on the roof, so the rain doesn't run down the walls.
[BED] Now, the inside. I use pine shavings on the floor, not straw. Straw looks nice and smells nice, but it's hollow, and it holds moisture, and mites love to live in it. Pine shavings are dry, they soak up the droppings, and they're easy to rake. A big bale is about seven dollars and it covers my floor four inches deep. In the nest boxes, I'll use straw, because the hens like to make a little nest. But on the floor, shavings.
[BED] And in the fall I do what my grandmother called deep litter. I stop cleaning it all out, and I just stir it and add a few more inches of shavings every couple of weeks. By January there's eight inches down there, and the bottom of it is breaking down like a compost pile, and it puts off a little warmth. In the spring the whole thing goes on the garden. That's the best tomato fertilizer there is.
[POWER] Here's one people ask about. Do you need electricity out at the coop? I didn't have it for the first twelve years. I ran a heavy outdoor cord out there in the winter for the heated water base, and that's it. No heat lamp, ever. A heat lamp in a coop full of dry shavings is how coops burn down, and I know two people that happened to. A healthy hen in a dry coop does fine at zero degrees. She's wearing a down coat.
[RACCOON] And the mistake that cost me four hens. The pop door, the little door the hens use to go in and out. I made it slide up and down, and I held it open with a wooden peg. A raccoon figured out how to pull the peg. Raccoons have hands, and they're smarter than you think. One night in nineteen ninety-seven, he pulled the peg, slid the door up, and I lost four hens.
[RACCOON] After that, every latch on my coop is the kind that needs two steps to open. A spring hook through a hasp, or a latch with a clip. Something a raccoon can't work out with one hand. And I lock that pop door every single night at dusk. No exceptions. Today you can buy an automatic door that closes on its own at dark, for about a hundred and thirty dollars. If you travel at all, it's worth it.
[ROOF] One more thing I got right by accident. The roof. I put the tin on with an overhang, about a foot on every side. It keeps the rain off the walls, it keeps the sun out of the coop in the summer, and the hens have a dry spot along the side in a storm. The tin's been up thirty years. I replaced the screws once, the kind with the rubber washer, and it's never leaked.
[RUN] And the run. Eight by twenty, with a roof over half of it. That roof was the best change I made, in the year two thousand. Before that, the run was open, and every hawk in the county could look right down into it, and every rain turned it into a mud hole. Now half of it stays dry all year, the hens have shade in the summer, and the hawks can't get in. I put a tin roof over the front half and hardware cloth over the back half. If you only add one thing to an open run, add a roof.
[RUN] And put down sand or wood chips in the run, a few inches deep, not just bare dirt. Bare dirt turns into mud the first wet spring, and a hen standing in mud all day gets sore feet and dirty eggs. I bring in a pickup load of wood chips from the tree service every couple of years. They give it to me for free, they're glad to get rid of it.
[NOTBUY] And here's what I don't buy. I don't buy the little coops in a box from the big stores. They say they hold eight hens, and they really hold two or three. The wood is thin, the wire is chicken wire, and the floor is right on the ground. I've seen three of my neighbors buy them, and none of them lasted three winters. If you can afford a few hundred dollars more, build it, or have somebody build it, with real lumber and hardware cloth.
[VET] And for the health side, the coop is where it starts. A crowded coop, a wet coop, a coop that stinks of ammonia when you walk in, that's where you'll see coughing, runny eyes, and bumblefoot. If you see a hen breathing hard with her mouth open, or sneezing with bubbles in the corner of her eye, separate her, and if it's more than one hen, call a vet. Respiratory sickness spreads fast in a bad coop.
[REPAIR] And here's my repair list, thirty years of it, from the back of my notebook, so you know what to expect. New roost bars three times. New floor once, plus a new corner. Hinges on the nest box lid twice. The cleanout door once, the bottom rotted. New screws in the roof once. Four coats of paint. The hardware cloth on the windows, never. That's the part I'd tell you not to skimp on.
[REPAIR] Add it all up, and over thirty years I've spent maybe another four hundred dollars keeping it up. Two hundred eighty to build it, four hundred to keep it. About twenty-three dollars a year for a coop that's raised I don't know how many hens. That's the cheapest thing on this farm.
