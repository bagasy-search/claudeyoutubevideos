[ONE] Trick number one is knowing what's really in that tray. Stew meat is not a cut. There's no stew muscle on a cow. It's the trimmings. The odds and ends left over after the butcher cuts the roasts and the steaks for the case. A corner off the bottom round. A piece of sirloin tip. Some chuck. A bit of the brisket point. All cut into cubes and mixed together in one tray.
[ONE] And here's the problem. Those muscles don't cook the same. The lean round is done and tender in about an hour and a half, and after that it only gets drier. The chuck needs two and a half hours before it lets go. So you cook it long enough for the chuck, and the round turns to sawdust. You cook it short enough for the round, and the chuck stays rubber. There's no right time for a tray of mixed meat. That's what the fireman ate.
[ONE] And there's one more thing. The pieces in a tray are usually cut small, about an inch, because small cubes look like more meat. Small cubes dry out. In a long stew, an inch of beef shrinks to a little gray pebble. That's two strikes before you even turn on the stove.
[TWO] Trick number two. Buy one cut, and it's the cheapest one. A chuck roast. Look for a piece about a pound and a half, with those white seams running through it. In my shop that's nine dollars. Every piece of your stew comes from one muscle family, so every piece gets tender at the same time. That's the whole secret, and it's been sitting right next to the tray the whole time.
[TWO] If you watched my roast videos, you know why the chuck wins. Those white seams are collagen. Hours of gentle heat turn it into gelatin, and that's what makes stew meat silky instead of dry. [FOUR] Trick number four. Here's the one that sounds wrong. Only brown half the meat. Everybody tells you to brown every single cube. So you stand there for half an hour, batch after batch, the kitchen full of smoke, and the cubes get tight and dry on the outside before they even hit the liquid.
[FOUR] Here's the old butcher way. Heat two tablespoons of oil in your heavy pot until it shimmers. Put in half the cubes, with space between them, and brown them hard on two sides. That's all the browning a stew needs. Those dark bits on the bottom of the pot are where the taste comes from. The other half of the cubes go in raw, later, straight into the liquid.
[FOUR] The browned half gives the stew its deep color and flavor. The raw half stays softer and juicier, because it never got a hard crust. And you saved twenty minutes and a cloud of smoke. My father did it this way his whole life. When I asked him why, he said, Michael, the gravy can't tell the difference, and the meat can.
[FIVE] Trick number five. The flour goes in after, not before. A lot of recipes tell you to roll the raw cubes in flour before you brown them. Don't. The flour browns faster than the meat, it burns on the bottom of the pot, and it turns into a gummy coat around every cube.
[FIVE] Instead, take the browned cubes out, turn the heat down to medium, and put in one big onion, chopped, and two carrots, chopped small. Five minutes, stirring, until the onion is soft. Then sprinkle two tablespoons of flour right over the vegetables and stir for one minute. No more. That's your thickener, cooked, no lumps, no raw taste, and it can't burn on the meat.

[SIX] Now the meat goes back in. The browned half, with its juice, and the raw half. Add a bay leaf and a sprig of thyme if you have it. The liquid should come just to the top of the meat.
