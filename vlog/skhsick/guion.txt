[ONE] Number one is shoes inside the house. Most American homes walk right in, across the carpet, into the kitchen and up the stairs. In Japan, every house has a genkan, a little entry where you step out of your shoes before you step up into the house. The floor of the house is higher than the floor of the genkan, so the street stays down there.
[ONE_B] You do not need to build anything. Put a small mat inside the front door, a low shelf or a basket for shoes, and a row of slippers for the family and for guests. And in Japan we even keep a separate pair of slippers just for the toilet room, so the bathroom floor stays in the bathroom.

[THREE] Number three is the big fluffy towel that never dries. Most American bathrooms have thick towels hanging on one hook, folded over itself, still damp the next morning, and used again. A towel that stays damp starts to smell sour in a few days, and that smell is telling you something. In Japan, many of us use a tenugui, a thin cotton towel, long and flat. It dries in a few hours, even in the rain season. I keep two bath mats and four tenugui, so there is always a dry one waiting on the shelf, and nothing in my bathroom ever stays wet overnight. If you love your thick towels, keep them, but hang them open and flat, never folded on a hook, and wash them every three uses. In summer I hang my towels outside in the sun for an hour. [FOUR] Number four is the bathroom that stays closed after a hot shower. Most people shower, close the door, and walk away, and the mirror is still dripping an hour later. All that warm water in the air lands on the ceiling, the grout and the corners, and that is where the black spots begin. My mother's rule was simple: after the bath, the window opens or the fan runs, for twenty minutes. Then she wiped the walls with one dry cloth, from top to bottom, two minutes, every night. Her bathroom was older than mine and never had a black spot. And pull the plug on the tub the same night. Standing water in a warm closed room is an invitation, my mother said, and she never sent that invitation.
[FIVE] Number five is the old flat pillow. Look at your pillow without the pillowcase. If it is yellow, flat, and it folds in half like a pancake, it has been with you too long. A pillow collects sweat and skin and dust every night for years. Japanese grandmothers used a pillow filled with buckwheat husks, called sobakawa. The husks let the air move through, and once a month the pillow went out in the sun. Whatever pillow you have, put it in the sun on a dry afternoon, turning it over after an hour so both sides get the light, wash it if the label allows, and replace it every two or three years. Change the pillowcase twice a week. My mother laid a small cotton tenugui over her pillow and changed only that every single morning. Her pillowcase stayed fresh all week.
[SIX] Number six is the bed made the minute you get up. I know, your mother taught you to make the bed right away, and so did my neighbors here. But in Japan, we fold the futon back, open the window, and let the bed breathe for an hour before we cover it. You sweat a little every night, dear, even in winter. If you cover the bed right away, that damp is locked in with the dust. So in the morning, throw the blanket back to the foot of the bed, open the window a crack, have your tea, and make it after breakfast. On a sunny morning in Japan, you see futons hanging over the balcony rails of every apartment on the street, like flags. Everybody knows the weather report, and everybody knows when it is a futon day.
[SEVEN] Number seven is the plug-in air freshener, the scented candles, and the spray in the bathroom. Most homes buy them because they want the house to smell clean. But a smell on top of a smell is not clean, it is only covered. And many people with sensitive noses do not like those strong perfumes at all. In Japan, we have kanki, airing the house. Every morning, even in winter, I open two windows on opposite sides of the house for ten minutes. The old air goes out, the fresh air comes in, and the house smells like nothing.
[SEVEN_B] Ten minutes, not more in winter, so the walls do not get cold. If something smells, find it and take it out, do not cover it. [EIGHT] Number eight is the heavy curtains that have never been washed. [TEN] Number ten is the dirty filter in the air conditioner and the furnace. Most people have no idea when it was changed last. All the air in your house goes through that filter, again and again, every day. If it is grey and thick, the dust just goes around and around the house with you. In Japan, families wash the filter of the room air conditioner every two weeks in summer. It is on the calendar on the wall. Here, write the date on the side of the new furnace filter with a marker when you put it in, and look at it every month. When it is grey, it is time. [ELEVEN] Number eleven is the feather duster. Most people swish it over the shelves and the dust goes up in the air and comes down again ten minutes later, a little to the left. In Japan we clean with a damp cloth, a zokin, always from the top to the bottom. The high shelves first, then the tables, then the floor last, because the dust falls down. The damp cloth holds the dust, and you rinse it out in the bucket. My mother used to say, a dry duster moves dust, a damp cloth takes it away. For the ceiling fan, slide an old pillowcase over each blade and pull it back slowly. The dust stays inside the pillowcase, not on your bed.

[THIRTEEN] Number thirteen is behind a door you open every day: the closet packed tight against an outside wall.
