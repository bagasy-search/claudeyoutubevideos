[SHOP] Before we start, three rules a diner cook uses in any store. First, look at the little price per pound or per ounce on the shelf tag, not the big price. That's where they hide it. Second, the cheap food is on the edges of the store and on the bottom shelf. The middle of the store and the shelf at your eye level is where the expensive stuff lives. And third, if somebody already cut it, cooked it, mixed it or seasoned it, you're paying them to do it. Sometimes that's worth it. Most of the time, it's not.
[N10] Number ten. Bottled salad dressing. A bottle of ranch or thousand island is four or five dollars now, and it's mostly oil, water, sugar and things to keep it on the shelf for a year. Diner thousand island was half a cup of mayonnaise, two tablespoons of ketchup, a tablespoon of sweet pickle relish, a little grated onion and a pinch of paprika. Stir it with a fork. Ranch was mayonnaise and buttermilk, half and half, with dried dill, garlic powder, onion powder, parsley and black pepper. Five minutes. About fifty cents a jar. It keeps a week in the refrigerator, and nobody in your house will want the bottle again.
[N9] Number nine. Frozen hamburger patties. A box of them is about twelve dollars for maybe two pounds, and they cook up flat and gray and shrink to half their size. A diner bought chuck, ground, eighty percent lean, and the cook shaped the patties every morning. A pound of ground chuck makes four big patties. Here's the trick. Press your thumb in the middle of each patty to make a little dimple. That way it doesn't puff up into a ball on the griddle. Salt only the outside, right before it goes on. Half the price, and twice the burger.
[N8] Number eight. Bread crumbs and croutons. Both of them are stale bread with a price tag. A little canister of bread crumbs is three dollars. A bag of croutons is three more. At the diner, the end of every loaf went into a bin. Croutons were bread cubes tossed with melted butter, garlic powder and salt, baked at three seventy-five for twelve minutes, shaken once. Bread crumbs were dry bread run through the grinder, or in your kitchen, a blender, or a plastic bag and a rolling pin. Free. You already paid for that bread.
[N7] Number seven. The little seasoning packets. Taco seasoning, chili seasoning, ranch seasoning. A dollar fifty for a packet, and it's half salt and half cornstarch. Here's the diner taco seasoning. Two tablespoons of chili powder, one tablespoon of cumin, one teaspoon each of paprika, garlic powder, onion powder and oregano, a pinch of cayenne, and a teaspoon of salt. Mix a big jar of it, and it's about a dime a batch. You can taste the difference in the first bite, because you can actually taste the spices.
[SALESMAN] I'll tell you a story about number seven. Nineteen sixty-eight, a salesman came into my diner in a nice suit with a sample case. Gravy mix, soup base, seasoning packets, all in shiny foil. He said, Lou, you'll save an hour a day. I said, sit down. I made him a plate of chicken fried steak with my gravy, drippings, flour, milk, pepper, and I timed it. Four minutes. Then I made one with his packet. Three and a half minutes. He ate both of them, wiped his plate with a biscuit, closed his case and said, I'll see myself out. He came back every month for twenty years, but he never opened that case again. He just ordered the steak.
[N6] Number six. And here's the one people think you can't make. The tub of whipped topping. That stuff in the freezer isn't even cream. It's oil, corn syrup and air. Real whipped cream is one cup of cold heavy cream, two tablespoons of sugar and a little vanilla. Put the bowl and the beaters in the freezer for ten minutes first. Then two minutes with a mixer, or five minutes with a whisk if you've got the arms. It stands up in peaks. At the diner, every slice of pie got a spoon of the real thing, and that's half of why people ordered the pie.
[N5] Number five. Boxed broth. A carton of chicken broth is three or four dollars for a quart. At the diner, broth was free. It was the leftovers. Chicken backs and wing tips, the onion ends, the carrot peels and the celery leaves. All of it went into a bag in the freezer, and when the bag was full, into a big pot with cold water, a bay leaf and a few peppercorns, at a slow simmer for three hours. Strain it, cool it, freeze it in jars. Your soup will taste like soup again.
[N4] Number four. Bagged salad and pre-cut vegetables. Diced onions, sliced peppers, the bag of chopped lettuce. You're paying two or three times as much for somebody else to use a knife, and it goes slimy in three days because it's already cut. A diner cook buys a whole head of lettuce, keeps it wrapped in a damp towel, and it lasts two weeks. Onions by the bag, peppers whole. Ten minutes of chopping on a Sunday and you're set for the week.
[N3] Number three. Instant oatmeal packets. Ten packets for four dollars, with sugar and flavoring already in them. A big cylinder of plain rolled oats costs about the same and makes forty bowls. At the diner, the oatmeal was a pot of old fashioned oats, cooked with a pinch of salt, and on the counter a bowl of brown sugar, a pitcher of cream and a dish of raisins. People made it the way they liked. And here's the trick, cook the oats in half milk and half water. It comes out creamy, like a dessert.
[N2] Number two. Biscuit dough in a can. You know the one, you hit it on the counter and it pops. Biscuits are flour, baking powder, salt, cold butter and buttermilk. That's all. Two cups of flour, a tablespoon of baking powder, a teaspoon of salt, six tablespoons of cold butter cut in until it looks like gravel, and three quarters of a cup of buttermilk. Stir with a fork, pat it out, fold it over three times, cut. Fifteen minutes at four twenty-five. About forty cents a batch, and they rise twice as high as the can.
[N1] And number one. The one that would save your family the most. Frozen potatoes. The fries, the hash browns, the tater rounds, the wedges.
