Hour six. Eating dirt.

Now, here's the weird trick I promised you at the start.

All over the world, and across thousands of years of human history, people have done something that sounds completely insane. They eat dirt. Specifically, certain kinds of clay. Pregnant women in many cultures have done it. Children do it. Whole communities have done it as part of normal cooking. And for a long time, scientists assumed it was just a strange habit, or a sign of hunger.

Then, in the nineteen eighties, a researcher named Timothy Johns started looking at a tradition in the Andes of South America. People there ate wild potatoes, and wild potatoes are loaded with bitter, toxic compounds that can cause stomach pain, vomiting, and at high enough doses, much worse. And they ate those potatoes with a dip made of clay. Johns took the local clays into the lab, mixed them with a toxin very similar to the one in those potatoes, and watched what happened. The clay grabbed the toxin. The particles of clay carry an electrical charge, and the poison molecules stick to them like iron filings to a magnet. Clay and toxin then travel through the gut together, and a lot of that poison never makes it into the blood.

So eating dirt wasn't madness. It was medicine. And we know the idea held up, because for decades a very common clay, called kaolin, was a main ingredient in a popular remedy for upset stomachs that you could buy at any drugstore.
