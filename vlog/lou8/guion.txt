[HOOK] Never buy pancake mix again. I mean it. That box in your pantry is flour, sugar, baking powder and salt, with a few things in there you can't pronounce, and you're paying about six dollars for it. The batter we made at my diner in the fifties cost about thirty cents a batch, and it made a stack so tall and fluffy that people drove forty miles on a Sunday for it. Tonight I'm going to give you that batter, every measure, and the tricks that made it work.
[HOOK] My grandson made me pancakes last month. Sweet kid. He used the box, he added the water, he stirred it like he was mad at it, and he poured it in a pan that wasn't hot yet. What came out was flat and gray and rubbery, like a coaster. He looked at me and said, Grandpa, why don't they come out like yours? I didn't have the heart to tell him it was the box. So I showed him. And now I'm going to show you.
[HOOK] Maybe your pancakes come out flat. Maybe they're raw in the middle and burnt on the outside, or tough like a shoe, or they taste like baking soda. Listen to me. It's not you. It's not even the box, not completely. It's the little things nobody tells you. A diner cook made two hundred pancakes every Sunday morning, and every one came out the same. That's not luck. That's a few tricks. That's what I'm here for.
[PROMISE] Here's how we're doing it. First, the batter, the real one, from my diner in nineteen fifty-eight. Then twelve tricks, counting up, that make the difference between a flat pancake and a tall one. Trick number seven is the one my grandson didn't believe until he saw it. And at the end, I'll do the arithmetic, what the box really costs you over a year.
[INTRO] You know me, I'm Lou. Fifty years behind a griddle. Sunday mornings at my diner, the line went out the door and around the gravel lot. Families after church, truckers, farmers, the high school kids who'd been out too late. And every single table ordered the short stack. Grab a cup. Let's go.
[ORIGIN] The batter isn't mine, I'll be honest with you. It came from a woman named Irene who worked the griddle at the first diner I ever worked in, in nineteen fifty-eight. She was maybe sixty, tiny, with her hair in a net, and she made the pancakes every morning from a card taped to the wall over the flour bin. The card was so greasy you could barely read it. When she retired, she took the card off the wall and handed it to me. She said, don't you ever let them put a box on my shelf. I never did. I'm going to read you that card right now.
[ORIGIN] One more thing before we start. You don't need anything special. A big bowl, a small bowl, a fork, a measuring cup, and a heavy pan. A cast iron skillet is perfect. A flat griddle is even better. Nonstick works too, if that's what you've got.
[BATTER] Here's the batter. Write it down. Two cups of flour. Two tablespoons of sugar. Two teaspoons of baking powder. Half a teaspoon of baking soda. Half a teaspoon of salt. That's your dry bowl. In the other bowl, two eggs, two cups of buttermilk, and four tablespoons of melted butter, a little cooled. That's it. That's the batter that fed my Sunday crowd for fifty years. Eight ingredients you already have in your kitchen.
[BATTER] Now the buttermilk. If you don't have any, don't buy a whole quart. Put two tablespoons of white vinegar or lemon juice in a measuring cup, fill it to two cups with regular milk, and let it sit five minutes. It gets thick and a little lumpy. That's what you want. The acid in the buttermilk is what wakes up the baking soda, and that's where the rise comes from.
[T1] Trick number one. Two bowls, never one. The dry goes in one bowl, the wet in the other, and you only put them together at the very end. If you dump everything in one bowl, you have to stir too much to get the flour wet, and stirring is the enemy. I'll tell you why in a minute.
[T2] Trick number two. Whisk the dry bowl first, for thirty seconds. The baking powder and the soda have to be spread all through the flour. If they're in clumps, you get one bite that's flat and the next one that tastes bitter, like soap. Thirty seconds with a whisk fixes it.
[T3] Trick number three. Melt the butter and let it cool. If you pour hot butter into cold buttermilk and eggs, it turns into little hard pieces of butter floating around. Melt it first, before you do anything else, and by the time you've measured the rest, it's just warm.
[T4] Trick number four. Stir with a fork, not a whisk, and stop while it's still lumpy. This is the one everybody gets wrong. When you stir flour with water, you make gluten, the stuff that makes bread chewy. Good for bread, terrible for pancakes. Ten or twelve turns with a fork, until you can't see dry flour anymore, and stop. The lumps are fine. The lumps are what makes it fluffy. A smooth pancake batter makes a tough pancake.
[T5] Trick number five. Let the batter rest. Ten minutes on the counter, or overnight in the refrigerator with a towel on top. While it rests, the flour soaks up the liquid, the baking powder starts to work, and you'll see little bubbles on top. In the diner, the Sunday batter got made Saturday night, in a big steel bowl, and went in the walk-in.
[T6] Trick number six. The griddle has to be hot, but not too hot. Three hundred and seventy-five degrees. At home, medium heat, and give the pan a full five minutes to warm up. Here's how a diner cook checks it. Flick a few drops of water on it. If they just sit there, it's too cold. If they disappear right away, it's too hot. If they dance and skitter around, that's your griddle.
[T7] Trick number seven. Here it is. Wipe the butter off. You put a little butter or bacon grease on the pan, and then you wipe almost all of it off with a paper towel. My grandson didn't believe me. Too much fat in the pan fries the edges and you get those lacy brown rings and a pale spotty middle. A thin film gives you that even golden color all over, like a picture in a magazine. Every diner pancake looks like that because the griddle was wiped, not greased.
[T8] Trick number eight. Pour from a measuring cup, and don't spread it. A quarter cup for a regular pancake, a third of a cup for the big diner ones. Pour it in one spot, from low, and let it spread out by itself. If you push it around with the spoon, you knock out the air.
[T9] Trick number nine. Flip when you see the bubbles. Not when you think it's time. Watch the top. When bubbles come up and pop and the little holes stay open, and the edges look dry, that's when. Usually about two minutes. One flip. Only one. And never, ever press it down with the spatula. I saw a man do that once at my griddle and I almost took the spatula away from him. You just squeezed out everything you worked for.
[T10] Trick number ten. The second side is faster. About a minute, maybe a little more. It doesn't need to be the same color. The first side is the side that goes up on the plate. That's the pretty one.
[T11] Trick number eleven. Keep them warm the right way. Don't stack them on a plate while you cook the rest. They steam each other and go soggy. Put them in one layer on a sheet pan in the oven at two hundred degrees. In the diner we had a warmer over the griddle. Same idea.
[T12] Trick number twelve. The blueberries go on the pancake, not in the batter. Pour the batter, then drop the berries on top, five or six, before you flip. If you stir them into the bowl they bleed and turn the whole batter purple and gray, and they sink to the bottom. Same thing with chocolate chips or sliced bananas.
[VAR] Now once you have the batter, the diner had four more pancakes on the board, all from the same bowl. The first one is the silver dollar stack. Same batter, a tablespoon at a time, little pancakes the size of a coin, twelve on a plate. The kids loved them, and the old men who didn't want a big breakfast ordered them too. Cook them a little lower, they burn fast.
[VAR] The second one is the buckwheat cakes. Swap one cup of the flour for buckwheat flour. They come out darker, with a nutty taste, and the farmers loved them with sausage on the side. In the winter, that was a farmer's breakfast. The third one is cornmeal griddle cakes. Swap half a cup of the flour for yellow cornmeal and add a spoon more sugar. A little crunch on the outside. Wonderful with a fried egg on top.
[VAR] And the fourth one, the one people asked for by name, the apple cinnamon. Grate one apple, skin and all, squeeze out the juice with your hands, and fold it into the batter with a teaspoon of cinnamon. They come out moist and they smell like a pie. We only had them in the fall, and on those Sundays we went through twice as much batter.
[SYRUP] And don't pay for fancy syrup either. Diner syrup was one cup of brown sugar, half a cup of white sugar, one cup of water, brought to a boil and simmered five minutes, with a spoon of butter and a little vanilla stirred in at the end. Warm, in a little pitcher. Pennies. And the butter on top? We whipped it. Soft butter in a bowl with a spoon of milk, beaten with a fork until it's light. It spreads on a hot pancake and melts right in, instead of sitting there in a cold yellow block.
[TROUBLE] Now, if something goes wrong, here's what it means. Flat pancakes, your baking powder is old. Put a teaspoon of it in hot water. If it doesn't fizz, throw it out. Tough pancakes, you stirred too much. Raw in the middle and dark outside, the griddle's too hot. Pale and greasy, too cold, or too much butter. And if they taste bitter, you used too much baking soda, or you didn't whisk the dry bowl.
[MIX] Here's one more thing the old diners did. Make your own mix. Take the dry bowl, but times eight. Sixteen cups of flour, one cup of sugar, five and a third tablespoons of baking powder, four teaspoons of baking soda, four teaspoons of salt. Whisk it, put it in a big jar with a lid, and write on the side, two and a quarter cups mix, two eggs, two cups buttermilk, four tablespoons butter. Now you have your own box. It keeps three months in the pantry.
[MATH] Let me do the arithmetic. That box of mix is about six dollars, and it makes maybe four batches. That's a dollar and fifty cents of mix per batch, and then you still add the eggs and the milk. My dry bowl costs about thirty cents. If your family makes pancakes once a week, that's about sixty dollars a year saved on the mix alone, and you'll never go back. And if you're the kind of family that goes out for a pancake breakfast on Sunday, four people, with coffee and the tip, that's fifty dollars a morning now. At home, with this batter, it's about four.
[STORY] You know, the box came into the kitchens right around the time I opened my diner. The ads said, just add water. And people did. And slowly, a whole generation forgot that pancakes are five things in a bowl. My mother never bought a box in her life. She'd have laughed at it.
[STORY] I'll tell you one more thing. On the last Sunday before I closed my diner, I made the batter Saturday night like always. Two hundred and twelve pancakes that morning.
