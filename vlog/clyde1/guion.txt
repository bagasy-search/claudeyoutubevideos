Last summer my grandson paid thirty-eight dollars for half a rack at a barbecue place out by the interstate, and he chewed one rib for about a minute and set the rest down. Black on the outside, gray on the inside, and stuck to the bone like it owed somebody money.
[HOOK] If you have ever done that to a rack of ribs in your own backyard, I want you to hear this. It was never you. Somebody told you ribs go on a grill, over the fire, and you did what you were told. The fire is the problem. A rib is not a steak, and a grill is built for a steak.
[PROMISE] So here is my promise. Tonight, in your own backyard or in your own oven, you are going to make ribs better than that place out by the interstate. Seven pieces, in order. Not my opinions. What actually happens to the meat. And number seven is a thing my granddaddy did in nineteen fifty-eight that I have never once seen a restaurant do. It is the reason his bones came out clean. The barbecue places are going to hate me for a couple of these. Let them.
[INTRO] I am Clyde Pruitt. Fifty-five years of cutting hogs in east Tennessee, and most of those years in this smokehouse right here. Pine boards, a dirt floor, and hams hanging up there in cloth sacks the same way my granddaddy hung them. You can smell the hickory in the walls. It never comes out.
[STORY] In nineteen fifty-eight I was five years old, and my job was to sit on an upside-down lard bucket in the corner and keep my hands to myself. Every fall the neighbors brought their hogs to my granddaddy, and he cut them, salted the hams, and smoked the sides. And on the Saturday after hog killing, he cooked the ribs. Not on a grate over the coals. Off to the side, in the smoke, all day long.
[STORY] He had a pan of water sitting by the fire, a jar of vinegar with a rag mop stuck in it, and a roll of brown paper on a nail. And when the ribs were done, he did something I did not understand for twenty years. He wrapped them up and buried them. In a barrel of clean, dry oak sawdust, right there in the corner. One hour. Then he dug them out, and the bones came out of that meat like pulling a pencil out of a cup. Everything I am about to show you comes out of that Saturday. Let us start.
[STORY] Granddaddy had one line he said every single time somebody asked him what his secret was. He would hold up one finger and say, the fire cooks it, but the smoke and the waiting finish it. Most people only ever do the first part. I did too, for a long time. Then I watched him close enough.
[ONE] Number one is the rib you buy, and the meat counter is hoping you grab the wrong one. There are three kinds sitting in that case. Baby backs are the short, curved ones off the top of the hog, up by the loin. Lean, quick, and they cost about twice as much a pound, mostly because the name sounds nice. Country style ribs are not ribs at all. They are strips off the shoulder. Good supper, different supper.
[ONE] What you want is spare ribs. The big flat slab off the belly side. Longer bones, more fat between them, more meat, and the cheapest of the three by a mile. That fat and all that tough stuff between the bones is exactly what this method turns into butter. If the package says St. Louis cut, that is a spare rib with the ragged end trimmed square. Same rib, a little neater, a little more money.
[ONE] Now pick it up and look at it. You want a slab that is the same thickness end to end, with meat sitting on top of the bones. If you can see bare bone poking through the meat, we call that a shiner, and it means somebody cut too close. Put it back. One good slab is about three and a half pounds, and it feeds three hungry people or four polite ones.
[TWO] Number two takes thirty seconds and almost nobody does it at home. Turn the slab over, bone side up. See that thin, shiny skin running the whole length of the bones? That is the membrane. Leave it on and that whole side cooks into leather, and nothing you put on it can soak in from underneath. Slide a butter knife under it at one corner, over the end bone. Lift it up just enough to get a finger under there. Then grab it with a paper towel, because it is slick, and pull it back in one long sheet. It wants to come off. Let it. Every rack, every time.
[THREE] Number three is the salt, and the salt goes on the night before, not five minutes before. My granddaddy salted everything. That is what a smokehouse is: salt, smoke and time. Salt put on a rib the night before goes down into the meat, and the meat holds on to its own juice when the heat comes. Salt put on right before cooking just sits on the outside.
[THREE] For one slab, here is his rub. One tablespoon of kosher salt. One tablespoon of brown sugar. Two teaspoons of coarse black pepper. Two teaspoons of paprika. Mix it in a cup with your fingers. Sprinkle it on from about a foot up so it falls even, both sides, and pat it, do not rub it. Then set the slab on a rack over a sheet pan and put it in the refrigerator, uncovered, overnight. The outside dries a little, and dry meat takes smoke and color. That is the whole rub. You will not find any secret powder in this smokehouse.
[FOUR] Number four is why the grill fails, and this is the whole video in one piece. Most of what is between those bones is not soft meat. It is connective tissue, the stuff that held that hog together. That tissue only turns tender one way. Low heat, for hours. Somewhere past one hundred sixty degrees inside the meat it starts to melt, and it needs a long time up around one hundred ninety to two hundred to finish. There is no shortcut through that.
[FOUR] Now look at a grill. Hot fire, right under the meat, from one side. The outside burns long before the inside has spent one hour where it needs to be. The fat drips in the coals, flares up, and burns it worse. So you pull it off black and tough, and you think you did something wrong. You did not. You used a steak tool on a rib.
[FOUR] Granddaddy cooked at about two hundred twenty-five to two hundred fifty degrees, with the fire off to the side and the meat in the smoke. You can do that tonight with what you already own. On a round charcoal grill, pile the coals on one side only and put the ribs on the other side, lid on, with the top vent over the meat so the smoke pulls across it. Two fist-size chunks of hickory on the coals, in the first two hours, and that is plenty. No grill at all? Your oven at two hundred fifty does the same job. You will not get the smoke, so put a teaspoon of smoked paprika in the rub, and you will be closer than you think.
[FOUR] And about the wood, because people get religious about it. Hickory is what this smokehouse has burned for a hundred years, and it is the taste of a Tennessee rib. Apple or cherry is gentler, a little sweet, and good if your family is new to smoke. Oak sits right in the middle. Mesquite is strong and burns hot, and on a long pork cook it turns bitter fast. Whatever you use, use chunks, not chips. Chips flare up and burn out in ten minutes. A chunk smolders for an hour.
[FIVE] Number five is the pan and the mop, and these two keep the meat from drying out over a long cook. The pan first. A disposable foil pan with about two inches of water and a cup of apple juice, set under the ribs or right beside them. In the smokehouse it sat by the fire. That steam keeps the air wet, and wet air cooks low and slow without turning the outside into jerky.
[FIVE] Then the mop. This is east Tennessee. We do vinegar. One cup of apple cider vinegar, half a cup of water, one tablespoon of salt, one teaspoon of red pepper flakes. Shake it up in a jar. Do not touch the ribs for the first hour and a half. Let the rub set into a crust. After that, every hour, dab the mop on. Dab it, do not brush it, or you wipe the crust right off. And do it fast, because every time that lid is up, the heat runs out and you add fifteen minutes to supper.
[SIX] Number six is the clock, and the sauce. Spare ribs at two hundred twenty-five take about five to six hours. In a two hundred fifty degree oven, more like four and a half to five. Baby backs, about an hour less. But you do not cook by the clock. You cook by the rib. Here is how Granddaddy knew, and he never owned a thermometer.
[SIX] So plan backwards. If you want to eat at six o'clock, the ribs go on the fire by about eleven in the morning, they come off around four thirty, and they go in the cooler while you make the beans. Granddaddy lit his fire before the sun came up, and nobody at hog killing ever ate before dark. You have it easier than he did.
[SIX] Pick the slab up in the middle with tongs. If it bends down on both ends and the top of the meat cracks open, it is ready. If it holds out stiff like a board, it is not. Second test: slide a toothpick between two bones. It should go in like the meat is warm butter. If you do have a thermometer, you are looking for one ninety-five to two hundred and three between the bones. Pork is safe to eat at one forty-five. It is tender at two hundred. Different numbers, different jobs.
[SIX] And the sauce goes on at the very end, never at the start. Barbecue sauce is sugar. Ketchup, molasses, brown sugar. Sugar burns black long before a rib gets tender, and that is why so many backyard ribs taste scorched. When the ribs pass the bend test, brush a thin coat on the meat side, give it fifteen minutes with the lid on, and that is it. Thin. You want to taste the pork, not the bottle.
[SEVEN] And number seven is the one from nineteen fifty-eight. The bury. Tear off a sheet of brown butcher paper, about twice as long as the slab. Lay the ribs on it meat side down, pour two tablespoons of the vinegar mop over the bones, and wrap it up tight like a package from the meat counter. Not foil. Paper. Foil holds every drop of steam and turns your crust to mush. Paper lets a little breathe out and keeps the bark.
[SEVEN] Then you bury it. Granddaddy had his barrel of oak sawdust. You have an ice chest. A clean, dry, empty cooler, with an old bath towel in the bottom. Set the wrapped ribs in, cover them with two more towels, close the lid and walk away. One hour. You can go as long as two. In a good cooler the meat stays well above one hundred forty degrees, which is where it needs to stay.
[SEVEN] Here is what happens in that box. The heat keeps working on the tough stuff between the bones, slow and even, without drying anything out. The juice that got pushed to the outside goes back into the middle. And when you open that paper, the steam rolls up, and you take one bone by the end and give it a little twist, and it slides out clean. That is the picture you saw at the start of this video. That is the trick. Nineteen fifty-eight, a five year old on a lard bucket, and I am telling you now.
[SEVEN] To cut them, turn the slab bone side up, so you can see where every bone runs, and cut right down between them with a long knife. One slow stroke each. Do not saw at it.
[TROUBLE] Now, what goes wrong, because something will. If they came out tough, they were not done. That is all. It is not the rib and it is not you. Wrap them, back on the heat for forty-five minutes, and do the bend test again.
[TROUBLE] If they came out dry, the fire ran too hot, or the pan ran dry, or you kept lifting the lid to look. Check your pan every hour and keep that heat under two seventy-five. If they came out mushy, like pot roast on a stick, you held them too long in foil. Paper, and two hours at the most.
[TROUBLE] If the outside is bitter and black, the sauce went on too early, or you put on a whole bag of wood. Two chunks of hickory. Smoke should come out of that grill thin and blue, almost clear. If it is billowing thick and white, it is putting soot on your supper.
[TROUBLE] If you cut one open and there is a pink band right under the outside, a quarter inch deep, do not panic and do not throw it out. That is the smoke ring. It is what wood smoke does to the outside of the meat over a long cook. It is a badge, not a mistake. If the ribs passed the bend test and the toothpick test, they are cooked all the way through, pink ring and all.
[TROUBLE] And if somebody at the table says real ribs should hold on to the bone a little, they are not wrong. That is how the contest people judge them. Pull your ribs at the bend test and skip the cooler, and they will bite clean and hold. My granddaddy was feeding hog killers and children. He wanted them to fall off. So do I.
[MONEY] Now the money, and I am not mad at anybody. I am just doing arithmetic. Half a rack at that barbecue place, thirty-eight dollars. A whole slab of spare ribs at the meat counter runs about three dollars and fifty cents a pound. Three and a half pounds, call it twelve dollars. The rub, the vinegar and the apple juice, maybe a dollar more. Thirteen dollars feeds three or four people. Their thirty-eight fed my grandson one rib.
[MONEY] And here is how I do it. Buy two slabs. Cook both on Saturday, the same fire, the same time. Eat one Saturday night. Wrap the other one in paper, then foil, cool it down and put it in the refrigerator within two hours, and on Tuesday warm it in a two seventy-five oven for about thirty minutes with a splash of the mop. Two suppers, one fire, about twenty-six dollars.
[RECAP] So here is all of it one more time. Spare ribs, even thickness, no shiners. Pull the membrane. Salt, sugar, pepper and paprika the night before, uncovered in the refrigerator. Fire off to the side, two twenty-five to two fifty, lid on, a pan of water and apple juice. Hands off for an hour and a half, then mop every hour with vinegar. Bend test, toothpick test. Thin sauce at the very end. Then wrap them in brown paper and bury them in a cooler with towels for one hour.
[RECAP] And if you only change two things this week, change these two. Get the ribs off the fire and over to the side. And bury them when they are done. Everything else just makes them better. Those two make them right.
