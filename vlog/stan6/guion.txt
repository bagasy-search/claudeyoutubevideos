[INTRO] If you don't know me, I'm Stan. Stan Wozniak. Fifty-two years behind the same meat counter on the South Side of Chicago. My father opened this shop, and I've been cutting pork chops in it since I was fourteen years old and had to stand on a milk crate to reach the block.
[INTRO] And I'll tell you something. In fifty-two years, the question I've heard the most across this counter is not about steak. It's not about sausage. It's this one. Stan, why are my pork chops always dry? Women ask me, men ask me, young people who just got their first apartment ask me. So let's fix it, once and for all, starting at the counter, because that's where most dry pork chops begin.
[ONE] Trick number one. Buy the right chop. When you walk up to the meat case, you see a lot of things called pork chop. They are not the same. The most common one in the package is the boneless loin chop. Pale, lean, perfectly round, no fat on it at all. It looks nice and clean. And it's the one most likely to dry out in your pan, because there's nothing in it to protect it.
[ONE] What you want is the rib chop. Bone in. It comes from the part of the loin closer to the shoulder, and you can tell it by the bone running down one side, a nice cap of fat on the edge, and a little more marbling in the meat. Some places call it a rib cut chop, some call it a center rib chop. If you're not sure, you say to the butcher, give me rib chops, bone in, from the shoulder end. He'll know.
[ONE] Why the bone? Two reasons. The bone slows down the cooking right next to it, so the meat there stays juicy. And the fat around the bone and on the edge melts while it cooks and keeps everything wet. A boneless chop is like a man going out in a Chicago January without a coat. It can be done, but it's not going to be pleasant.
[TWO] Trick number two. Thickness. This is the big one at the counter. Most pork chops in the package are cut about half an inch thick. Sometimes less. And a half inch pork chop is almost impossible to cook right. By the time the outside gets brown, the inside is already overcooked. You never had a chance.
[TWO] You want an inch and a quarter. An inch and a half is even better. Think about the width of your thumb, from the knuckle to the tip, and a little more. That's a real pork chop. With a thick chop, you have time. Time to get a beautiful brown crust on the outside while the middle stays pink and wet.
[TWO] And here's what people don't think about. One thick chop is better than two thin ones, for the same money. Buy one inch and a half chop, cook it right, slice it on the board, and two people share it. My wife and I did that for forty years. One big chop, two plates, and it was always juicy. If your store only has thin ones, ask the butcher in the back. He'll cut you a thick one. That's his job, and believe me, he'd rather do that than stack packages.
[THREE] Trick number three. Read the label. This one nobody tells you, and it's printed right there on the package. A lot of pork in the store has a line that says something like, contains up to twelve percent of a solution. Water, salt, and some other things, pumped into the meat at the plant. They call it enhanced pork. It's supposed to make the pork juicier.
[THREE] Here's the problem. That water comes right back out in your hot pan. The chop steams instead of browning, the texture turns spongy, like a ham, and if you salt it on top of that, it's way too salty. So when you see that line, solution added, you know two things. First, don't add more salt before cooking. Second, you'll get a better chop if you buy one without it. Plain pork, nothing added. Every butcher shop sells it that way. That's how it's supposed to be.
[FOUR] Trick number four. Salt it the night before. This is the trick my father taught me, and he learned it from his father in Krakow. Take your chops out of the paper the night before. Pat them dry. And salt them on both sides. About three quarters of a teaspoon of kosher salt for every pound of meat. Then put them on a plate, or better, on a little wire rack over a plate, in the refrigerator. Uncovered.
[FOUR] Here's what actually happens. First, the salt pulls a little water out of the meat. You'll see it shining on the surface. Then, after a while, the meat pulls that salty water back in. Deep inside. And salt inside the meat holds on to the juice when you cook it. A salted chop loses much less juice in the pan than an unsalted one. That's not my opinion. That's what happens.
[FOUR] And leaving it uncovered dries the outside, so the next day it browns beautiful instead of steaming. If you can't do the night before, salt it at least forty-five minutes before you cook. But never ten minutes. Ten minutes is the worst time to salt. The water is sitting on the outside, it hasn't gone back in yet, and your pan fills with gray juice. Either right before the pan, or forty-five minutes and longer. Remember that. Right before, or the night before.
[FIVE] Trick number five. Cut the fat. Not off. Through. You know how a pork chop curls up in the pan, like a potato chip? One side lifts up off the pan, the middle doesn't touch, and you get one brown spot and the rest is gray? That's the fat and the thin skin on the edge. When it gets hot, it shrinks faster than the meat, and it pulls the whole chop up into a bowl.
[FIVE] So before you cook, take a sharp knife and make little cuts through that fat cap on the edge. About every inch. Just through the fat and the skin under it, right to the meat, but not into the meat. Three or four little cuts on a big chop. That's it. Now the chop stays flat in the pan, the whole face touches the hot iron, and the whole face gets brown. Thirty seconds of work. My mother did it to every chop, and she'd tap the knife on the board after each one, tap, tap, like she was counting.
[SIX] Trick number six. Dry it and let it sit. Take the chops out of the refrigerator about twenty minutes before you cook. Not two hours. Twenty minutes. Just so the meat isn't ice cold in the middle. Then, right before it goes in the pan, pat it dry with paper towels. Both sides, and the edges. Press a little. Water is the enemy of a brown crust. If the surface is wet, the pan spends its heat boiling off that water before it can brown anything. A dry chop starts browning the second it touches the iron.
[SIX] And this is when you put the pepper on, if you like pepper. Right before the pan. Not the night before with the salt. Pepper doesn't need time. It just needs to be there.
[SEVEN] Trick number seven. Start with the fat. Use a heavy pan. Cast iron is best. My mother's was so old and so black you could see your face in it. Heat it over medium high, with one tablespoon of oil, until the oil just starts to shimmer. Now here's the trick. Before you lay the chop down, hold it with tongs and stand it up on its fat edge. Right on the hot pan. Fat side down.
[SEVEN] Hold it there for about two minutes. You'll hear it sizzle, and you'll see that white fat start to turn golden and crispy, and it melts right into the pan. Now you have pork fat in the pan instead of just oil. More flavor. And that fat edge, which most people leave rubbery and white and then cut off the plate, is now the best bite on the whole chop. My customers call it the candy.
[SEVEN] Then lay the chop down flat. And leave it alone. Don't push it around. Don't press it with the spatula. Every time you press, you squeeze the juice right out into the pan, and you hear it hiss, and that's the sound of your dinner getting dry. Let it go about three minutes, until the bottom is deep brown, and turn it over. Another three minutes on the other side.
[EIGHT] Trick number eight. The butter at the end. When both sides are brown, turn the heat down to medium. Put in two tablespoons of butter, a couple of cloves of garlic, crushed with the side of your knife, skin and all. And if you have it, a little sprig of thyme or rosemary. The butter foams up. Tip the pan a little toward you, so the butter runs into the corner, and with a big spoon, pour it over the chop. Again and again. For about a minute or two.
[EIGHT] That's called basting. It cooks the top gently while the bottom keeps going, it puts flavor all over the meat, and the garlic butter fills the whole kitchen. My wife used to come in from the other room when she smelled it. She'd say, Stanley, what did you burn? And I'd say, nothing, sit down. And if your chop is very thick, inch and a half or more, and the outside is done but the inside is not, you put the whole pan in the oven at three hundred seventy-five degrees for a few minutes to finish. No shame in that. Restaurants do it every night.
[NINE] Trick number nine. And this is the one that's going to start an argument in your family. Let it start. Cook your pork chop to one hundred forty-five degrees. Not one sixty. Not one seventy. One hundred forty-five degrees in the thickest part, away from the bone. And it's going to have a little pink in the middle. Light pink. And that's exactly right.
[NINE] I know what your mother told you. Pork has to be cooked all the way through, white, no pink, or you get sick. And she wasn't wrong, in her time. Years ago, pigs were raised differently, and there was a worm called trichinosis, and so everybody cooked pork until it was dry as the Sahara. My mother did. Her mother did. But that was a long time ago. Pork today is raised completely differently, and that worm is almost gone from farm pigs in this country.
[NINE] So the government changed the rule. More than ten years ago now. The safe temperature for a pork chop went from one hundred sixty down to one hundred forty-five, with a three minute rest. That's the official number. But nobody told your mother. Nobody put it on the evening news. And so millions of people are still cooking pork to one sixty and one seventy, and wondering why it's dry. That's the whole secret right there. It was never your cooking. It was the number.
[NINE] So get a thermometer. A simple one, the kind you poke in. It's the best ten or fifteen dollars you'll ever spend in a kitchen. Pull the chop off the pan when it reads one hundred forty degrees. Five degrees early. Put it on a warm plate, not a cold cutting board, and let it rest five minutes. While it rests, the heat keeps moving into the middle, it climbs up to one hundred forty-five, and the juice settles back into the meat instead of running all over the board.
[NINE] Then you cut it. And I want you to look at it. The middle is the color of a rose petal, it's shining, and when you press it with your fork, the juice comes out. That's a pork chop. That's what pork is supposed to taste like. The first time my grandson ate one of mine like that, he put his fork down and said, Grandpa, this is a different animal. No, kid. Same pig. Different number.
[FIX] Now, let's talk about what can still go wrong, because I'm honest with you. If your chop is still dry, and you did everything, check three things. Was it thin? Under an inch, it's almost impossible. Was it enhanced, with the solution? That pork has a spongy texture no matter what you do. And did you trust the thermometer, or did you cut it open in the pan to look? Every time you cut it to peek, the juice runs out. Trust the number.
[FIX] If the outside is burned and the inside is raw, your pan was too hot for that thickness. Turn it down to medium after the first sear, or finish it in the oven like I told you. If the chop curled up, you forgot to cut the fat. If it's gray and not brown, it was wet, or the pan was crowded. Never more than two chops in a ten inch pan. If you have four, use two pans, or do them in two batches and keep the first ones on a warm plate.
[FIX] And if it's tough even at one forty-five, look at how you cut it on the plate. Slice across the lines in the meat, not along them. Short pieces of muscle are tender. Long ones are like rubber bands. That's the butcher in me talking, but it makes a difference you can feel.
