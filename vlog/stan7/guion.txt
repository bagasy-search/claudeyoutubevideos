[STORY] Then he showed me. A pot of cold water. An onion cut in half. Two bay leaves. A few peppercorns. The sausage goes in cold, the fire goes on low, and you never, ever let it boil. [ONE] Trick number one. Buy the right kielbasa. Walk up to the meat case and there are ten things called Polish sausage. They are not the same. Turn the package over and read the label. A real smoked kielbasa is pork, maybe some beef, salt, garlic, black pepper, marjoram, and smoke. That's it. Short list. If the label is long, if you see words like water added, or corn syrup, or a bunch of words you can't say out loud, put it back. That's a sausage made to be cheap, not to be good.
[ONE] Squeeze it a little through the package. A good kielbasa feels firm, like a tight muscle. A cheap one feels soft and smooth, like a hot dog, because inside it's ground so fine it's basically paste. When you bite a good one, you should see pieces of meat. Little bits of pork, little bits of fat. That's what holds the juice. And one more thing. Know if you've got smoked or fresh. Smoked kielbasa is already cooked, it just needs to get hot. Fresh kielbasa, the white one, the biała, is raw. That one has to cook all the way through. Same trick for both, just more time for the white one. I'll give you the numbers.
[TWO] Trick number two. Never put a fork in it. I know. Your grandmother poked holes in every sausage so it wouldn't burst. My aunt Helena did it too, God rest her. Here's what actually happens. That casing is a little bag that holds all the juice and all the fat. Every hole you poke is a door. The heat comes in, the juice goes out, right into the pan, and it burns there. You're not stopping it from bursting. You're bleeding it dry.
[TWO] The reason sausages burst is not the casing. It's the heat. Too hot, too fast, the juice inside turns to steam, the steam has nowhere to go, and pop. Fix the heat, and you'll never need the fork again. Use tongs. Treat that sausage like an egg. Fork in the drawer, tongs in your hand.
[THREE] Trick number three. This is my father's trick. The bath. Take a pot wide enough so the kielbasa lies flat, curled around like a snake sleeping. Put the sausage in first, then cold water from the tap, just enough to cover it by an inch. Cold. Not hot from the kettle. When you start cold, the inside and the outside warm up together, slow and even. Start hot, and the outside is cooked before the middle even knows what's going on.
[THREE] Now put it on medium-low heat. And here's the whole secret. It never boils. Never. You want the water to shiver, not to dance. Little bubbles on the bottom of the pot, a little steam coming off the top, that's it. If you have a thermometer, that's about one hundred seventy, one hundred eighty degrees. If you don't, look at it. The moment you see a big bubble break on top, turn it down.
[THREE] For smoked kielbasa, once it gets to that shiver, give it twelve to fifteen minutes. That's enough to heat it all the way through without pushing the juice out. For fresh white kielbasa, give it twenty-five to thirty minutes, and check it. One hundred sixty degrees in the thickest part, that's done. When you lift it out it should look plump. Shiny. Fatter than when it went in. That's a sausage that kept its juice.
[FOUR] Trick number four. What goes in the water. Plain water works. But my father never put plain water in anything. Half an onion, with the skin on, the skin gives a little color. Two bay leaves. Six or seven black peppercorns. Four allspice berries, that's the Polish part, don't skip it. And one clove of garlic, smashed with the side of the knife. That's all.
[FOUR] And here's something nobody tells you. The water isn't only flavoring the sausage. The sausage is flavoring the water. Some of the smoke and salt and fat comes out into that pot. When you're done, that water is a broth. Smoky, a little salty, beautiful. Don't you dare pour it down the sink. I'll tell you what to do with it later, and it's going to save you money.
[FOUR] Some people use beer instead of water. Go ahead, one bottle of beer and the rest water, it's nice. But don't use all beer, it gets bitter when it sits on the heat. My father drank the beer and cooked in the water. He said that way nothing is wasted.
[FIVE] Trick number five. The step everybody skips. Dry it. Lift the kielbasa out of the water with tongs, put it on a plate or a cutting board, and pat it dry with a paper towel. All over. Then let it sit for two or three minutes. You'll see the skin go from wet and shiny to dry and a little tacky.
[FIVE] Here's why. A wet sausage in a hot pan doesn't brown. It steams. The water has to boil off first, and by the time it's gone, you've been cooking it too long. A dry skin hits the pan and browns in two minutes. That's the whole difference between pale and gray and that deep golden brown you see in the window of a good deli. Wet sausage steams. Dry sausage browns. Remember that.
[SIX] Trick number six. Now, and only now, the pan. And yes, I said never fry your kielbasa. I meant never fry it first. The pan is not for cooking it anymore. It's already cooked. The pan is just for the color and the snap. Two different jobs. Your mistake was asking the pan to do both.
[SIX] Cast iron if you have it. Medium-high heat. One spoon of lard, or butter, or bacon fat if you saved some. Cut the kielbasa into pieces about three inches long, and then cut each one in half the long way, so you have a flat side. Lay them flat side down. Don't touch them. Two minutes. Lift one with the tongs and look. Brown? Turn them over, one more minute on the skin. Done. Three minutes total. The skin gets tight and crisp, and when you bite it, it snaps.
[SIX] If you want it whole, for a party, leave it whole, put it in the pan and roll it every minute with the tongs, four or five minutes. It'll look like the picture on the package, except it's juicy inside. And if you've got a grill going in the summer, same thing. Bath first in the kitchen, then just three minutes over the coals for the marks. Your neighbors will think you're a genius.
[SEVEN] Trick number seven. The onions. Don't wash that pan. All that brown stuff stuck to the bottom, that's flavor. Take one big yellow onion, slice it thin, and put it right in the same pan with the fat that's left. Medium heat. Pinch of salt. Stir it. Eight, ten minutes, until it's soft and golden.
[SEVEN] Now here's my mother's part. When the onions are almost done, pour in half a cup of that sausage water from the pot. It sizzles, it lifts all the brown off the bottom, and in one minute you've got a sauce. Put the kielbasa back on top of the onions for one minute so it gets glossy. Then if you want, a spoon of brown mustard stirred in. That's it. That's the plate that beer garden charged me nineteen dollars for, and theirs didn't even have the sauce.
[EIGHT] Trick number eight. Let it rest and cut it right. Take it off the heat and give it two minutes. A sausage is like a steak. If you cut it the second it comes off the fire, the juice runs out on the board. Two minutes, and the juice stays inside where it belongs.
[EIGHT] Then cut on a slant. Hold the knife at an angle, not straight down. You get long oval pieces instead of little round coins. More surface, more of that brown crust in every bite, and it looks like something from a restaurant. Serve it with the onions, a slice of rye bread, a spoon of sauerkraut warmed up in a little of that same water, and mustard on the side. That's a Polish Saturday.
[FIX] Now let's fix the problems, because I know somebody out there is going to try this and something's going to go wrong. If your kielbasa split open in the water, the water was too hot. It boiled. [FIX] If it came out too salty, good news, the bath fixes that. Some kielbasa is very salty, especially the double smoked. Fifteen minutes in cold water pulls a lot of that salt out.
