[INTRO] You know me, I am Helen. Forty-one years behind this counter, in a little meat market in Iowa. My father cut meat here before me. Most of my customers are retired, cooking for one or two, on a fixed income. And chicken thighs are the best friend those folks have in my whole case. [MISTAKE] First, the one mistake. Walter put his thighs in the pan wet. Straight out of the package, cold, shiny, with that pink juice still sitting on the skin. And then he put a lid on it. Here is what actually happens. Chicken skin is mostly water and fat. Before it can get crispy, all that water has to leave. When the skin is wet and the lid is on, the water has nowhere to go. So the skin does not fry. It steams. It boils in its own juice. And steamed chicken skin is rubber. Every time.
[MISTAKE] Think of a bath towel. You cannot iron a towel crisp while it is soaking wet. You just make steam. The skin on a chicken thigh is the same. Dry first, then heat. Water is the enemy of crispy skin. Every single trick I am about to give you is about getting the water out, and keeping it out.
[ONE] Trick number one is at the meat case, because half of the rubbery chicken in this country gets ruined right there. Buy the thighs with the bone in and the skin on. Not the boneless, skinless ones in the neat little tray. At my counter, bone-in thighs run about a dollar seventy-nine a pound. Boneless, skinless are four forty-nine. You are paying more than double for somebody to throw away the two best parts.
[ONE] The bone holds heat in the middle and keeps the meat juicy. The skin is your crispy part, and underneath it is a layer of fat that bastes the meat while it cooks. Take those away, and you have a piece of meat that goes from raw to dry in about two minutes. With the bone and the skin, you have a lot of room for mistakes. That is what you want when you cook for one.
[ONE] Look at the pack too. Pick the thighs where the skin covers the whole top, edge to edge, not bunched up on one side. Skip any pack with a lot of pink water in the bottom of the tray. That water is going right onto your skin. And if the thighs look very big and puffy, more than half a pound each, they can take longer, so plan for it. A six-pack of good thighs is three suppers for two people, or six suppers for one.
[TWO] Trick number two. Trim the flaps and dry them like you mean it. When you open the pack, you will see loose pieces of skin and fat hanging off the edges. Trim the big flaps with kitchen shears, but leave enough skin to cover the top of the meat. Then pull the skin up over the thigh and stretch it out flat, like you are tucking in a bedsheet. Flat skin crisps. Bunched-up skin stays soft in the folds.
[TWO] Now the paper towels. Do not just pat them. Press. Lay the thighs skin side up on a towel, put another towel on top, and press down with your whole hand, like you are blotting a spill on the carpet. Turn the towel, press again. When you lift the towel and it comes up dry, then you are done. My mother used old clean flour sacks for this, and she would wash them and use them again. Paper towels are fine.
[THREE] Trick number three. Salt them the night before, and leave them uncovered in the refrigerator. I know, your mother told you never to leave meat uncovered in the fridge. For this, you do it. Here is what actually happens. The salt pulls a little water out of the skin. Then the cold, dry air in your refrigerator takes that water away. By morning the skin looks tight and a little see-through, like old parchment paper. That is exactly what you want.
[THREE] The measure is simple. Three quarters of a teaspoon of kosher salt for every pound of chicken. Six thighs is usually around two pounds, so a teaspoon and a half. Sprinkle it on both sides, more on the skin. Put the thighs on a wire rack set over a plate, so the air gets underneath. Put them on the bottom shelf, so nothing drips on your other food. Twelve hours is good. Twenty-four is better. Even one hour helps.
[THREE] And here is the old butcher part. Mix half a teaspoon of plain baking powder into that salt. Baking powder, not baking soda. Soda will make the chicken taste like soap. Baking powder helps the skin dry out and brown, and it makes those tiny little bubbles on top, the ones that crackle when you bite. Half a teaspoon for two pounds. You will not taste it. You will only hear it.
[FOUR] Trick number four is the one Walter will never forget. Start the chicken in a cold pan. Cold. Not hot. I know every recipe says get the pan smoking hot. That works for a steak. It does not work for a thigh. Under that skin is a layer of fat, and that fat has to melt out slowly. If you throw the skin on a screaming hot pan, the outside burns before the fat underneath has a chance to melt, and you get skin that is black on top and rubbery underneath.
[FOUR] So here is how. Take a heavy skillet, cast iron if you have one. Put the thighs in skin side down, in the cold pan. No oil, or just a teaspoon to get it started. Then turn the burner to medium. As the pan heats up, the fat starts to melt out of the skin, and the chicken fries itself in its own fat. After about five minutes you will hear it start to sizzle. That is the sound of the water leaving.
[FOUR] And now the hardest part. Leave it alone. Do not move it, do not lift it, do not peek. For about fifteen to eighteen minutes, the skin stays down on that pan. When it is ready, it lets go by itself. If you slide a spatula under it and it sticks, it is not ready. Give it two more minutes. When it is ready, it comes up golden brown, all in one piece, and you will hear the difference.
[FIVE] Trick number five. Press it down. A chicken thigh is not flat. The skin curls up at the edges and humps up in the middle, so only part of it touches the pan. That part gets crispy, and the rest stays pale and soft. So put a weight on it. I use a second smaller skillet set right on top of the thighs. My father used a brick wrapped in foil. A heavy pot with a can of beans in it works too.
[FIVE] Put the weight on for the first ten minutes, while the skin is setting. Then take it off and let the rest of the water steam away. If you have no weight at all, press each thigh down flat with your spatula for ten seconds, a couple of times in those first minutes. Every inch of skin that touches hot metal is an inch of crispy skin.
[SIX] Trick number six. Give them room. If you crowd the pan, you are back to Walter's problem. Every thigh lets out water as it cooks. If the thighs are packed in tight, all that water turns to steam between them, and steam makes rubber. Leave about a finger of space between each piece. In a ten-inch skillet, four thighs. In a twelve-inch, five or six. If you have more, cook two pans, or cook them in two batches.
[SIX] And no lid. Not at the start, not at the end, not ever on crispy chicken. A lid is a little steam room. If grease is spitting on your stove, use a splatter screen, or lay a sheet of foil loosely over the top with the sides wide open. But no lid. When I tell my customers that, they always say, Helen, but my mother used a lid. Honey, your mother was making smothered chicken. That is a different supper.
[SEVEN] Trick number seven, the one that starts the argument. A chicken thigh is not done at one hundred sixty-five degrees. Everybody learned one sixty-five for chicken. And one sixty-five is safe. But a thigh is not a breast. A thigh is a working muscle, full of tough little strings that hold the leg together. At one sixty-five, those strings are still tight. That is why the meat near the bone was pink and chewy for Walter. It was safe. It was just not finished.
[SEVEN] Here is what actually happens. Between about one seventy-five and one ninety, those tough strings melt, and they turn into the juice that makes dark meat taste so rich. The meat goes from chewy to soft. And because the thigh has all that fat and the bone, it does not dry out like a breast would. So cook a thigh to one eighty to one eighty-five, measured in the thickest part, next to the bone but not touching it. Breast, one sixty-five. Thigh, one eighty-five. Write that on your fridge.
[SEVEN] Here is how I finish it. When the skin is golden and lets go of the pan, flip the thighs skin side up. Then slide the whole skillet into the oven at four hundred degrees for about fifteen to twenty minutes. The skin stays up in the dry heat, and the meat finishes slowly around the bone. If you do not have an oven you trust, turn the burner down to medium low, flip them, and cook them on the meat side another twelve to fifteen minutes. Skin up. Always skin up.
[EIGHT] Trick number eight. How you rest it decides if the skin stays crispy. Most people take the beautiful crispy thighs and put them on a plate and cover them with foil to keep them warm. And in five minutes, the steam coming out of the meat has turned that skin back to rubber. All that work, gone. So never cover them. Put them skin side up on a wire rack, or on the cutting board, and let them sit for five minutes, open to the air.
[EIGHT] And never, ever put them skin side down on a plate. The juice runs out of the meat, the skin sits in it, and you are back to a wet towel. Skin up, open air, five minutes. Then eat it while it still crackles. That is the sound we came here for.
[TROUBLE] Now, if it still did not work, here is what went wrong. If the skin is brown but soft, it was still wet, or the pan was crowded, or there was a lid. Next time, dry it overnight. If the skin burned before it got crispy, your burner was too high. Medium, not high. Every stove is different. If the meat is chewy near the bone, it was undercooked, not overcooked. Give it ten more minutes, and use a thermometer. A thermometer costs less than one supper you have to throw out.
[TROUBLE] If the skin stuck to the pan and tore, you moved it too early. Wait until it lets go. And if your thighs come from the freezer, thaw them all the way in the refrigerator first. Then dry them extra well, because frozen meat lets out more water than fresh. A thigh still frozen in the middle will never cook evenly, and the skin will be soft before the bone is done.
[FAT] And here is a bonus, because I hate to see anything wasted. When the thighs come out, look in that skillet. You will see a couple of spoons of clear golden fat. That is chicken fat, and my mother called it liquid gold. Pour it through a little strainer into a clean jar and keep it in the refrigerator. It keeps for weeks.
[FAT] Fry your potatoes in it. Wilt your cabbage in it. Cook your morning eggs in a spoonful of it. One pan of thighs gives you enough fat to cook four or five other meals that taste like Sunday dinner. My customers who learned this stopped buying cooking oil for weeks at a time. That is money back in the purse.
