[ONE] Trick number one is buying the right piece, because half the people who ruin a pork shoulder ruin it right here at the meat case. There are two pieces with the word shoulder on the label, and they are not the same.
[ONE] The top half of the shoulder is called the Boston butt. Funny name, I know. It's not the back end of the pig at all. It's the upper shoulder. Back in the old days, here in Boston, butchers packed these cuts in barrels, and a barrel was called a butt. So, Boston butt. That's the one you want.
[ONE] The bottom half is called the picnic. It's got the skin on, it's leaner, and it's got more tough muscle around the leg. It's cheaper by maybe twenty cents a pound, and it's a good piece of meat, but it's harder to get right. If you're new at this, buy the butt.
[ONE] And buy it with the bone in. Look for a piece about seven or eight pounds, with a flat bone showing on one side. That's the blade bone. The bone holds heat in the middle of the meat, it keeps the meat from drying out, and later, it's going to tell you exactly when the pork is done. A boneless roast all tied up in a net looks neater, but it cooks drier every time.
[ONE] Look at the color too. You want a deep pink, almost red, with white fat running all through it, like marble. Pale meat with no fat inside is going to come out dry, no matter what you do. In my shop, a bone-in butt runs about two dollars a pound. Eight pounds, sixteen dollars. That's the cheapest dinner in the building.
[TWO] Trick number two. Leave the fat on. Everybody wants to trim it all off, because fat is bad, and they stand there at the counter cutting off the white cap on top. Don't do that. That fat cap is your basting. For eight hours it melts slowly down over the meat and keeps the outside from drying out.
[TWO] What you do instead is score it. Take a sharp knife and cut a crosshatch pattern into the fat, about an inch apart, like a tic-tac-toe board. Cut through the fat, but not into the meat. A quarter inch deep is plenty. That lets the salt get in, and it lets the fat render out slowly instead of sliding off in one sheet.
[TWO] If the fat cap is very thick, more than an inch, you can trim it down to about a quarter inch. Only the top. Leave all the fat inside the meat alone. That's the fat that makes it juicy. And any skin, if you bought a picnic, take the skin off, or score it deep. Skin never gets soft in a dry oven.
[THREE] Trick number three. Salt it the night before. Here's what actually happens. Salt on the surface pulls a little juice out of the meat. Then, over the next few hours, that salty juice goes back in, deep into the meat, and it changes the meat so it holds on to its own juice when you cook it. Salt it right before the oven, and it only seasons the outside.
[THREE] The measure is simple. One teaspoon of kosher salt for every pound of meat. Eight pounds, eight teaspoons. Mix it with two tablespoons of brown sugar and a spoon of black pepper, and rub it all over, into the cuts in the fat, into every corner around the bone.
[THREE] Then put it on a plate in the refrigerator, uncovered. No plastic wrap. The cold air dries the outside a little, and a dry outside is what gives you that dark, sticky crust later. Leave it there overnight. Twelve hours is good. Twenty-four is better. My mother salted hers on Saturday night, every week, and she never once owned a thermometer.
[FOUR] Trick number four. Low heat. This is the big one, and this is where that bus driver went wrong. Four hundred degrees is for chicken. A pork shoulder wants two hundred seventy-five. That's it. Two hundred seventy-five degrees, for a long time.
[FOUR] Here's why. A shoulder is a muscle the pig used every single day, walking around. It's full of tough connective tissue. Collagen. And collagen only melts slowly, at low heat, over hours. When it melts, it turns into gelatin, and gelatin is what makes pulled pork soft and silky. You can't hurry it. It takes the time it takes.
[FOUR] When you blast the oven, the outside of the meat squeezes tight like a fist, and it pushes all the juice out before the collagen inside even starts to melt. So you get the worst of both. Dry on the outside, tough on the inside. That's exactly what was in his foil.
[FOUR] So take the shoulder out of the refrigerator an hour before. Set the oven to two hundred seventy-five. Put the meat fat side up in a heavy roasting pan, on a rack if you have one, or on a bed of thick sliced onions if you don't. Count on about an hour and a half for every pound. An eight pound shoulder, about ten, eleven hours. Put it in the oven before bed, or first thing in the morning, and go live your day.
[FOUR] And here's the old Irish part. Pour one bottle of dark stout into the bottom of the pan. Not over the meat. Into the pan. A cup of apple cider works too, or plain water if that's what you have. That liquid keeps the drippings from burning, and the steam keeps the oven moist. And at the end, those drippings are gold.
[FIVE] Trick number five. The one that sounds crazy. Somewhere around the middle of the cook, the meat is going to stop cooking. You'll check the thermometer at a hundred and sixty degrees, and an hour later, it's still at a hundred and sixty. Two hours later, maybe a hundred sixty-five. And everybody panics. They turn the oven up to four hundred. And that's how they ruin it.
[FIVE] Here's what actually happens. That's called the stall. The meat is sweating. The juice comes to the surface and cools it off, the same way you cool off when you sweat. And at the same time, inside, that collagen is finally melting. That's the most important part of the whole cook. The stall is not a problem. The stall is the meat getting tender.
[FIVE] So you leave it alone. Don't open the door every half hour. Every time you open the door, you lose about twenty minutes. If you need to speed it up a little, there's one old trick. Once the outside is dark and firm, wrap the shoulder tight in foil, or butcher paper, with a few spoons of the pan juice inside. It pushes it through the stall faster and keeps every drop of juice in the package. That's all. Wrap it, don't crank it.
[SIX] Trick number six. Forget the clock. The pork is done when the pork says it's done. And there are two ways it tells you. The first one is the number. Push a thermometer into the thickest part, away from the bone. You want two hundred to two hundred five degrees.
[SIX] I know, the store sticker says pork is safe at a hundred forty-five, and that's true. It's safe, and it's sliceable. But it's not pulled pork. At a hundred seventy, it's still tight. Somewhere past one ninety-five, the meat lets go. That's the number where collagen is gone and gelatin is everywhere.
[SIX] The second way is the one my father taught me, and it never lies. Grab that blade bone with a towel and give it a little wiggle. If it's done, the bone moves like a loose tooth, and it slides out clean, without a single piece of meat on it. If the bone fights you, it's not ready. Close the oven, another hour, and try again.
[SIX] And one more test. Poke the meat with the thermometer, or a fork. It should go in like butter, no push at all. If you have to push, it's not done. Every shoulder is different. One might be done in nine hours, the next one in eleven. That's why you never trust the clock.

[SEVEN] Here's the old way. Leave it wrapped in the foil. Wrap a couple of old bath towels around it, and put the whole thing in an empty picnic cooler, and close the lid. Leave it there one hour.
