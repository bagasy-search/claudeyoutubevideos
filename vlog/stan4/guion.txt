[BUY] Let's start at the counter, because that's where most of the money goes. Trick number one. Don't buy the foam tray. Ask the butcher to grind it fresh. You point at a chuck roast in the case, and you say, can you grind this for me? Most shops will do it for nothing. Chuck roast on sale is often cheaper than the ground beef in the tray, and you know exactly what went into it. One piece of meat. Not ten.
[BUY] And here's what the foam tray doesn't tell you. That package was ground days ago, from scraps of who knows how many animals, and it's been sitting under the lights turning brown on the inside. Fresh ground chuck is red all the way through, it smells like nothing at all, and it holds together in the pan. You'll use less of it, because it tastes like more. That's the first place your five dollars starts stretching.
[BUY] Trick number two. Buy eighty twenty, not the lean stuff. People pay a dollar more a pound for ninety percent lean because it sounds healthier, and then the food tastes like cardboard. That fat is flavor. And when you stretch the meat with other things, the fat is what carries the taste of beef into everything else in the pot. Cheap meat, more flavor. That's a rare deal.
[BUY] Trick number three. Buy the big package, the three pound or five pound chub, the long tube wrapped in plastic. Per pound, it's almost always the cheapest beef in the store. You split it in one pound portions the day you buy it. Flatten each one in a bag, thin as a book, so it thaws in twenty minutes in a bowl of cold water.
[BUY] Trick number four. Ask the butcher for his trim. Every shop has a tub of beef trimmings, the pieces left over when he squares off the steaks and roasts. Most of it goes in his own grinder. But if you ask nicely, early in the morning, a lot of butchers will sell you a few pounds of that trim, ground, for less than the case price. My father always kept a bag for the widows on our block.
[BUY] Trick number five. Ask for a little beef heart in the grind. I know. Stay with me. Heart is a muscle, just like steak. It tastes like beef, a little richer, and it costs about half. One part heart to four parts chuck, and nobody at your table will ever know. In the old neighborhood, that's how every grandmother made her meat go further.
[STRETCH] Now the stretching. This is where one pound turns into two. Trick number six. The old bread trick. Two slices of stale bread, torn up, soaked in half a cup of milk until it's mush. You mix that into a pound of beef. The Italians call it a panade, my mother called it common sense. The bread holds the juice inside the meat, so your burger or your meatloaf comes out bigger and softer. Not drier. Softer.
[STRETCH] Trick number seven. Oats. Plain old fashioned rolled oats, the cheapest box on the shelf. Half a cup in a pound of beef for meatloaf. They soak up the fat and the juice, they disappear completely, and you just turned one pound of meat into a meatloaf for six people. Ten cents worth of oats.
[STRETCH] Trick number eight. Mushrooms, chopped fine. Fine like the meat. You cook them in the pan first until all the water comes out and they turn brown. Then they taste like meat. Half a pound of mushrooms in a pound of beef for tacos or meat sauce, and you can't tell where one stops and the other starts.
[STRETCH] Trick number nine. Lentils. Brown lentils, one cup dry, cooked soft. In chili, in sloppy joes, in a meat sauce for spaghetti, they look like ground beef, they take the color of the beef, and a bag of lentils costs less than one pound of meat and feeds a crowd.
[STRETCH] Trick number ten. Beans, but cook them with the beef, not next to it. Most people dump a can of beans in at the end. Don't. You put them in the pan with the browned beef and the onions, and you let them fry for five minutes. They soak up the fat and they start tasting like the meat. A can of beans for a dollar doubles a pot of chili.
[STRETCH] Trick number eleven. Grate a potato right into the meat. One raw potato on the big holes of the grater, squeeze the water out with your hands, and mix it in. In a burger, in meatballs, in a meat patty for the frying pan. The starch holds it all together. That's how the Polish ladies made their kotlety go around a table of eight.
[STRETCH] Trick number twelve. Rice. Cooked rice in the meat, one cup for one pound. That's the secret of stuffed cabbage, my grandmother's golabki. A head of cabbage, a pound of beef, a cup of rice, and a can of tomatoes. That's sixteen rolls. Sixteen. Two each for a family of eight.
[STRETCH] Trick number thirteen. The onion goes in grated, not chopped. A grated onion melts into the meat, it gives you juice and sweetness, and you need less meat for the same flavor. One big onion, grated, into a pound of beef. My father said, the onion is the poor man's steak sauce.
[COOK] Now the cooking. Because a pound of beef cooked the wrong way looks like a handful. Cooked the right way, it looks like a feast. Trick number fourteen. Brown it hard, in one flat layer, and don't stir it for three minutes. You want a dark crust. That brown is where the flavor lives, and the more flavor every little piece has, the less meat you need in every bite.
[COOK] Trick number fifteen. Don't pour the fat down the sink. Never. That fat is free flavor. You spoon it out into a cup, and you fry your onions in it, or your potatoes tomorrow morning. When I was a boy, there was always a coffee can of beef drippings next to my mother's stove. Nothing was wasted.
[COOK] Trick number sixteen. Break it up small. Smaller than you think. When the beef is broken into little crumbles, it spreads through the whole dish, and every forkful has some meat in it. Big chunks, and half the plate has nothing. Use a potato masher in the pan. That's the old butcher's tool for it.
[COOK] Trick number seventeen. Add a spoon of tomato paste to the browned beef and let it fry for one minute until it turns dark red. Then a splash of water to scrape the bottom of the pan. Those brown bits and that paste make the whole pot taste like it has twice the meat in it.
[COOK] Trick number eighteen. Season like you mean it. Cheap food needs more salt and pepper, not less. One teaspoon of salt per pound of meat, and a good pinch of black pepper, and taste it before it goes on the table. Bland food makes people eat more. Good food makes them happy with one plate.
[MEALS] Now what to make with it. Trick number nineteen. The meat sauce rule. One pound of beef, two cans of tomatoes, one grated carrot, one grated onion, and a pound of spaghetti. That feeds six people for under ten dollars, and the carrot makes the sauce sweet so the kids don't complain.
[MEALS] Trick number twenty. Shepherd's pie. A pound of beef and a bag of frozen peas and carrots on the bottom, mashed potatoes on top, and into the oven. Potatoes are the cheapest food in the store, and they make the pound of meat look like three.
[MEALS] Trick number twenty-one. Make the burgers thin. Smash them. A quarter pound of beef in a ball, on a hot iron pan, and you push it flat with a heavy spatula. You get a big crispy burger, wider than the bun, and you get four burgers out of a pound instead of three. Two thin patties on one bun eat better than one fat one.
[MEALS] And a little secret from the lunch counter next to my shop. The cook there put a slice of onion right on top of the ball before he smashed it, so the onion pressed into the meat and fried in the beef fat. One onion, eight burgers, and the whole street could smell it. People lined up out the door for a burger that had three ounces of meat in it.
[MEALS] Trick number twenty-two. Soup. Half a pound of beef, browned with onions, in a pot with potatoes, carrots, cabbage and water. My mother made it on Thursdays, when the money was gone before payday. Half a pound of meat, a big pot, a family of six, and bread on the table.
[MEALS] Trick number twenty-three. Stuffed vegetables. A pepper, a zucchini, a big tomato, cut open and filled with the beef, rice and onion mix. The vegetable is the plate. Every pepper looks like a whole dinner, and there's barely two ounces of meat in each one.
[LAST] Trick number twenty-four. Cook once, eat twice. Whatever you make on Sunday, you make it double with the same pound of meat and the stretchers. Half goes on the table, half goes in a jar for Tuesday. You use the stove once, you clean the pan once, and you never buy dinner on the way home.
[LAST] And trick number twenty-five, the one my father said was the most important. Make friends with your butcher. Learn his name. Go on the same day every week. Ask him, what's good this week? A butcher who knows you will tell you when the chuck is on sale, he'll save you the trim, and he'll grind it fresh while you wait. That friendship is worth more than any coupon.
[TROUBLE] Now, if something goes wrong, here's what it was, and it isn't you. If your meatloaf came out mushy, like wet bread, you used too much filler or too much milk. The rule is simple. Never more than one cup of stretcher for one pound of meat, and squeeze the wet things before they go in. If it falls apart in the pan, you forgot the egg. One egg per pound holds everything together.
[TROUBLE] If the chili tastes thin, like it's missing something, you didn't brown the beef hard enough, or you skipped the tomato paste. Go back and give the meat its three minutes without touching it. If the burgers shrank to the size of a coin, your pan was too cold, or your meat was too lean. And if the kids picked out the mushrooms, you cut them too big. Fine like the meat. They'll never find them.
[TROUBLE] And if your butcher says, we don't grind to order? You ask him one more thing. You say, when do you grind for the case? Come in that morning, and buy it fresh off the grinder, before it ever sees a foam tray. Any good butcher respects a customer who asks that question.
