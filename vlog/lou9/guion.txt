[HOOK] For forty years, a big steel pot of chili sat on the back of my stove, and the truckers planned their routes around it. Men would drive two hundred miles out of their way to sit at my counter on a cold night and eat a bowl of it with a sleeve of crackers. And every one of them asked me for the recipe. I never gave it out. Not once. Tonight, I'm going to give it to you. Every step, every measure, and the four things the old diner cooks never wrote down.
[HOOK] Last winter my son brought home a can of chili from the store. Four dollars. He heated it up and put it in front of me like it was a gift. It was sweet, it was mushy, the beans were falling apart and the meat was little gray crumbs. I ate it, because he's my son. But I thought about those drivers, and I thought, they'd have walked out on me.
[HOOK] Maybe you make chili at home and it comes out thin, like soup. Or greasy, with an orange puddle on top. Or it tastes like ground beef and tomato sauce and nothing else. Listen to me. It's not your fault. A diner chili is not a recipe. It's a handful of small things that nobody puts in the cookbook. That's what I'm here for.
[PROMISE] Here's what you're getting. The whole recipe, start to finish, the way I made it for forty years. The four secrets, one at a time, as we go. Secret number three is the one that makes people ask, what is that? And at the end, I'll tell you how to make a pot that feeds ten people for about twelve dollars.
[INTRO] You know me, I'm Lou. Fifty years behind a griddle. My diner sat right off the state highway, with a gravel lot big enough for six rigs. The chili went on every morning at six, and by nine at night, the pot was scraped clean. Grab a cup. Let's go.
[STORY] The recipe started with a driver, believe it or not. Nineteen seventy-one, a big quiet man from west Texas named Royce. He came in on a January night with ice on his beard and ordered a bowl of my chili, which back then was nothing special. He ate two spoons and pushed it away. I asked him what was wrong. He said, son, that's not chili, that's spaghetti sauce with beans in it. I was young and I was mad. But he came back to the kitchen, and he showed me.
[STORY] Royce drove that route for twenty years, every two weeks, and every time he came in he'd taste the pot and tell me what was missing. Not much salt. More time. Toast the spices. By nineteen seventy-five, he took a bite, put the spoon down, and didn't say anything. He just nodded. That was the recipe. It never changed after that.
[POT] Before we start, the pot. You want the heaviest pot in your kitchen. A thin pot scorches on the bottom in the second hour, and once chili scorches, the whole pot tastes burnt and there's nothing you can do. A cast iron Dutch oven is perfect. A heavy stainless steel soup pot works too. And a wooden spoon with a flat end, so you can scrape the corners. That's all the equipment. No slow cooker, no pressure cooker. Just a heavy pot and a low flame.
[MEAT] Let's start with the meat. Three pounds of beef. Here's the first thing. Not lean. Eighty percent lean, twenty percent fat, the cheap stuff. And if you can, one pound of it should be chuck roast cut into little cubes, about the size of a sugar cube, instead of ground. The ground beef gives you the body, and the cubes give you something to bite. That's what the drivers loved. A spoon of my chili had chunks in it.
[MEAT] Brown the meat in a heavy pot, a Dutch oven if you have one, over medium high heat. Don't crowd it. Do it in three batches. If you put all three pounds in at once, the pot cools down, the meat lets out its water and it boils gray instead of browning. Every batch should sit without stirring for a few minutes until the bottom gets a dark brown crust. That brown is flavor. Take each batch out with a slotted spoon and leave the fat in the pot.
[SECRET1] Secret number one. The spices get toasted in the fat. Here's my mix. Four tablespoons of chili powder. Two tablespoons of ground cumin. One tablespoon of paprika. Two teaspoons of dried oregano. One teaspoon of cayenne, or more if you like it hot. A teaspoon of black pepper. Two big onions, chopped, and six cloves of garlic go into the fat first, five minutes until they're soft. Then all the spices go right on top, and you stir them around in the hot fat for one minute, no more. The whole kitchen fills up with the smell. That's when you know. Raw spices dumped into tomato taste like dust. Toasted in fat, they taste like chili.
[POWDER] Now, a word about that chili powder. The jar from the store is fine. But if you want to do what the old cooks did, make your own. Four or five dried ancho chiles and two dried guajillo chiles. Cut off the stems, shake out the seeds, and toast them in a dry skillet for thirty seconds a side, until they puff up and smell like raisins. Don't let them go black. Then you break them up and grind them in a coffee grinder with a spoon of cumin seeds. That's your chili powder, and it's darker and sweeter than anything in a jar. I made a fresh batch every Monday, and the whole diner smelled like it until noon.
[TOMATO] Now the meat goes back in. Then a big can of crushed tomatoes, twenty-eight ounces, and two tablespoons of tomato paste. Then two cups of beef stock. Some cooks used a bottle of beer instead of part of the stock. I did on Fridays. Stir it all up and scrape the bottom of the pot, all those brown bits, they go into the chili.
[SECRET2] Secret number two. Time. The diner chili went on at six in the morning and we didn't serve the first bowl until eleven. Low heat, the lid cracked open, a slow little bubble every few seconds. At home, give it at least two hours, three is better. That's when the cubes of chuck go soft and the fat melts into the sauce instead of sitting on top. A chili that cooked for forty minutes will always taste like ground beef in tomato. Stir it every half hour and scrape the bottom.
[SECRET3] Secret number three. This is the one. About an hour before it's done, two things go in. One square of unsweetened baking chocolate, about an ounce, and one tablespoon of strong black coffee, or a teaspoon of instant coffee. That's it. You won't taste chocolate. You won't taste coffee. But the chili tastes deeper and darker, like it's been cooking for two days. Royce taught me that, and in forty years, not one driver ever guessed it. They'd ask, Lou, what is that? And I'd just pour them more coffee.
[BEANS] Now the beans. And I know, in Texas they'll tell you real chili has no beans. Royce was from Texas, and he said, son, the drivers are hungry, put the beans in. Two cans of red kidney beans, drained and rinsed, or a pound of dried beans soaked overnight and cooked separately. They go in for the last forty-five minutes only. If you put them in at the start, they turn to mush by the end.
[SECRET4] Secret number four. The masa. When the chili is almost done, if it's thin, you don't thicken it with flour. Take three tablespoons of masa harina, the corn flour they use for tortillas, mix it with half a cup of warm water into a paste, and stir it in. Ten more minutes. It thickens the chili and it gives it that corn taste in the background, like a tamale. If you don't have masa, crush a handful of corn chips into fine crumbs and stir those in. Same idea.
[SALT] Last, the salt and the vinegar. Taste it at the end, not at the start, because it cooks down and gets saltier. Usually two teaspoons of salt. Then a tablespoon of cider vinegar, or the juice of a lime. You won't taste sour. It just wakes everything up. Without it, the chili is heavy and flat.
[SERVE] Here's how we served it at the truck stop counter. A heavy white bowl, filled to the top. A handful of chopped raw onion, a little shredded sharp cheddar, and a sleeve of saltine crackers on the side. Some drivers wanted it over a split hot dog, some over spaghetti, some on top of a pile of french fries, and the farmers wanted a square of cornbread under it. Seventy-five cents a bowl in nineteen seventy-five. A dollar fifty in the eighties. Refills on the crackers were always free.
[CORNBREAD] Now you can't have truck stop chili without the cornbread. Here's the one from my diner. One cup of yellow cornmeal, one cup of flour, a quarter cup of sugar, a tablespoon of baking powder, a teaspoon of salt. Then one cup of buttermilk, one egg and a third of a cup of melted bacon grease or butter. Stir it just until it's wet. Here's the trick. Put your cast iron skillet in the oven while it heats up to four hundred and twenty-five, with a spoon of bacon grease in it. When the batter hits that hot skillet, it sizzles, and that's what gives you the crunchy brown edge. Twenty minutes. Cut it in wedges right in the pan.
[STORM] I'll tell you about the blizzard of seventy-eight. The highway closed on a Thursday night, and I had eleven rigs in my gravel lot and nowhere for those men to go. Forty-one people, counting a family in a station wagon. The power stayed on, thank God, and I had two cases of beef in the walk-in. I made chili for two days straight. Pot after pot. Nobody paid. The drivers washed dishes and shoveled the lot, and the kids from the station wagon handed out crackers. When the plows came through on Saturday, one of the drivers left a twenty dollar bill under every bowl on the counter. I still think about that.
[VARIATIONS] Once you have the pot, the diner had a whole menu out of it. Chili mac was a bowl of elbow macaroni with chili ladled over it and cheese on top, for the kids. A chili dog was a split hot dog off the griddle, on a bun, with chili, raw onion and mustard. The chili size was an open faced hamburger patty on a slice of toast, smothered in chili and cheese, eaten with a knife and fork. And the three way was spaghetti, chili and a big pile of shredded cheese. Same pot, five dishes, five prices on the board.
[FREEZE] And one more thing. Make a double pot and freeze half. Chili freezes better than almost anything. Let it cool, put it in quart containers, leave an inch at the top, and it keeps three months. On a cold night when nobody wants to cook, one quart, a pot, ten minutes, and you've got dinner for four that tastes like it cooked all day. Because it did.
[NEXTDAY] And here's something every diner cook knows. Chili is better the next day. Put it in the refrigerator overnight. The fat comes up to the top and turns white, and you can scrape off as much as you want. And the flavor goes all the way through the meat. At the diner, we always served yesterday's pot and cooked today's for tomorrow. That's why the drivers said my chili tasted different from everybody else's. It had a day on it.
[MISTAKES] Now the mistakes. Lean meat, it comes out dry and grainy. Spices not toasted, it tastes like powder. Not enough time, it tastes like taco meat. Beans in too early, mush. Too much sugar, and I see people put sugar in chili now, it tastes like the can. And too much tomato, it tastes like spaghetti sauce. Royce would push it away.
[MATH] Let me do the arithmetic. Three pounds of beef, about fifteen dollars now if you buy the cheaper cuts on sale, maybe less. The tomatoes, the beans, the onions and the spices, about six. That big pot feeds ten people, two full bowls each. About two dollars a person. That can my son bought was four dollars for one bowl, and it wasn't even chili.
[STORY] Royce stopped coming in around nineteen ninety-one. I didn't know why until his wife came in one day that summer and sat at the counter. She told me he'd passed. She said he always talked about my chili, and she wanted to taste it once. I gave her a bowl and I didn't charge her. She ate the whole thing. Then she said, it tastes like he described it. I never forgot that.
