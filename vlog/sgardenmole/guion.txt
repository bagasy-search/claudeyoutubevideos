[HOOK] A small bottle of castor oil, a jar of garlic and a bowl of old coffee grounds. That is what my mother used to send the moles away from her garden in Osaka. No poison. No traps. Never once. And last week a man in a truck came to my neighbor's lawn, walked around the mounds for fifteen minutes, and asked her for four hundred dollars. Four hundred. Come, sit with me on the back step. I will show you what my mother did instead, and why it still works in an American yard, fifty years later.
[PAIN] You know the feeling. You open the back door in the morning and there they are. Little brown hills of fresh dirt in the middle of your nice grass, five, six, ten of them, like somebody buried a row of cats overnight. Soft lines under the lawn that sink when you step on them. Your tulip bed pushed up and dry. You flatten the hills with a shovel, and the next morning there are new ones. And you start to feel like the whole yard belongs to somebody you cannot even see.
[NOTYOU] I want to tell you something, and please listen. It is not because you are a bad gardener. It was never you. A mole does not come because your lawn is ugly. It comes because your lawn is good. Soft, wet soil full of worms and grubs means dinner, and the mole has a very good nose. So the answer is not to fight it. My mother had a simpler answer. Make the dinner taste bad and smell bad, and the mole moves on to an empty field.
[PROMISE] So today I will show you all three things, one by one. What each one does, exactly how to mix it, and where it goes. I will show you how to find the one tunnel that is really in use, because most of the hills are old and you are wasting your time on them. The one mistake that makes everything useless. And at the end, how to fix your lawn after they leave. The first thing is the one that surprises people most, because it is sitting in your medicine cabinet right now.
[INTRO] You know me, I am Sachiko. On my kitchen channel I cook the old Japanese way, the way my mother cooked. But my mother's house was more than her kitchen. Behind our little wooden house in Osaka there was a tiny yard with a persimmon tree, a few rows of daikon and eggplant, and a strip of soft green moss that made her very proud. She swept that moss with a bamboo broom every morning, and she talked to it like it was a cat.
[CTA1] Quick, while I have you. If you want to keep these three things, I wrote all of them in the description, with every measure, so you can find them again in the spring. And if you like the old ways, please subscribe. This is my garden channel, and here we go outside, to the yard, the garage and the shed. Now, back to the moles.
[STORY] When I was a little girl, the farmers at the edge of Osaka had a word for the mole, mogura, which means the one who digs in the earth. The farmers hated the mogura, because it pushed up their rice seedlings and their young radishes. Some of them put out poison, and the poison killed the cats and the birds too. My mother did not. She said, the mogura is not a thief. It is only hungry. Make the table unpleasant, and it will eat at the neighbor's field, where nobody minds.
[STORY_B] My mother learned this from her own mother, in the countryside, before the war. My grandmother used three things from the kitchen and one old trick with a bottle, which I will tell you at the end. When I came to America in nineteen seventy, with my husband, our first little house had a back lawn full of mole hills. I wrote to my mother. Her letter came back three weeks later. Three lines. Oil and soap in the water. Garlic in the holes. Coffee on the beds. I still have that letter.
[ONE] Number one, castor oil. This is the most important one, so I start with it. Castor oil is a thick, pale oil, and many old houses kept a small bottle in the medicine cabinet or the pantry. Most people try to chase moles with noise, or with water from the hose in the tunnels. That only moves them three feet. Castor oil does something different. Moles live on earthworms and grubs, and when castor oil soaks into the soil, everything in that soil starts to taste and smell bad to them. The mole does not get hurt. It just decides your yard is a bad restaurant and moves away.
[ONE_B] Here is how my mother mixed it, and how I do it. In an old watering can, I pour one gallon of water. Then three tablespoons of castor oil and one tablespoon of plain dish soap. The soap is very important. Without the soap, the oil floats on top of the water and never goes into the ground. With the soap, the oil mixes in and goes down with the water. Stir it well with a stick until the water looks a little milky. That is your mole water. Make it fresh each time, because the oil and the water separate again after a few hours.
[ONE_3] Now, where it goes. Not only on the hills. My mother watered a strip all around the edge of the yard first, like a fence, about two feet wide. Then the middle of the lawn, in rows, like you are mowing. Then a little extra right on the active tunnels. One gallon covers a patch about the size of a parking space, so for a normal backyard you will make the mix four or five times. Water it in best just after a rain, or water the lawn first with plain water, so the soil is soft and the castor oil soaks deep where the mole lives.
[ONE_4] Do it once, then again after ten days, and then once a month through the season, or after a heavy rain. In my yard, the new hills stopped after the second watering. The first time I did it, I did not believe it either. My husband laughed at me with my watering can at six in the morning. Two weeks later he stopped laughing, because his lawn was flat again, and he asked me to do the front yard too.
[ONE_5] One more thing about number one. Moles have a tunnel that is like their main street, and many little side streets. The castor oil works best on the main street. And here is how my mother found it. Step lightly on each tunnel, so it is flat. Put a little stick next to each one. Next morning, look. The tunnels that are raised again are the ones in use. The others are old, and you can forget them. Most people waste their whole weekend on old tunnels.
[TWO] Number two, garlic. This is my grandmother's favorite, and the second thing in your pantry. Moles have a very strong nose, much stronger than ours, because they live in the dark and find their food by smell. Garlic is too loud for that nose. You can use the dried garlic powder from the spice shelf, or old garlic that is getting soft and sprouting, the kind you were going to throw away anyway.
[TWO_B] Here is what my grandmother did. She crushed three or four cloves of garlic, or one big spoon of garlic powder, into a jar of warm water and let it sit overnight on the windowsill. In the morning, she found the active tunnel with her stick test. With a pencil or a thin stick, she made a small hole in the top of the tunnel, every two or three steps, and poured a little garlic water into each hole. Then she pushed one peeled clove of garlic into the hole and closed it with her foot.
[TWO_3] The moles hate walking through that. They will dig a new tunnel to go around it, and usually the new tunnel goes toward the fence and out. My mother also planted garlic and onions along the edge of her vegetable rows, every year in October, for the same reason. Garlic in the ground all winter is like a little fence, green and quiet, and it asks you for nothing but a little water in spring. In spring you have garlic for the kitchen too. Nothing in that garden was ever only one thing. The garlic kept the moles away, fed the family, and the dry skins went into the soup pot for flavor.
[TWO_4] A small warning. Garlic works for a week or two in the tunnel, then the smell gets weak. So use it on the main street and near your flower beds, and refresh it after a rain. And wash your hands well after crushing it, unless you want your husband to sleep on the sofa. My mother said that every time, and every time she laughed.
[MID] Before I show you number three, tell me in the comments. Where are your mole hills? In the lawn, in the flower beds, or under the vegetable garden? I read every comment, and I want to know. And if you have a friend who is fighting moles right now, send this to her. It may save her four hundred dollars.
[THREE] Number three, used coffee grounds. In Osaka my mother did not drink coffee, she drank green tea. So she used the wet tea leaves and the dry pepper from her kitchen. But here in America, I have coffee every morning, and when I asked an old gardener at my church, he said, Sachiko, coffee grounds. He was right. The smell of old coffee is strong and bitter in the soil, and moles do not like it near their food.
[THREE_B] Here is how I do it. I keep a metal can with a lid by the coffee pot. Every morning, the grounds go in. Once a week, I take the can outside and spread a thin layer of grounds in my flower beds and around the tomatoes, and I scratch them into the top of the soil with a little rake. Then I water. Not too thick, about as thick as a light frost on the grass. For the active tunnels, I open a hole like with the garlic, and I put a spoon of grounds inside and close it.
[THREE_3] The coffee grounds also do something nice for the garden. They feed the soil a little bit, and the worms like them. Yes, I know, the moles eat worms. But the smell keeps the moles away while the worms do their work. My roses have never been so pretty. My neighbor started asking me for my coffee grounds, and now she has a can by her coffee pot too. We trade, a little. She gives me coffee grounds, I give her persimmons from my tree, and we both have flat lawns and a reason to talk over the fence every Saturday.
[THREE_4] My mother's old trick with the bottle is my little extra for you. In the countryside, the farmers buried empty glass bottles in the ground at an angle, with only the neck sticking out. When the wind blows across the neck, the bottle hums, very softly. You can hear it if you put your ear down. The mogura feels that humming in the dirt and does not like it. Some people use little pinwheels on a stick in the lawn for the same reason. I put three old bottles at the edge of my lawn, and in the evening they sing a little. I like the sound.
[MISTAKE] The one mistake that makes everything useless. Doing only the hills. Most people pour everything right on the mound and stop there. But the hill is only where the mole threw the dirt out. The mole is not there. It is somewhere down the line, eating. So first find the tunnel in use with the stick test, then water the whole yard with castor oil, then the garlic in the tunnels, and the coffee in the beds. All three together, the whole yard, not one corner.
[CHECK] How do you know it worked? Use the stick test again after two weeks. Flatten every tunnel with your foot and put a stick next to it. If nothing is raised the next morning, and the next, and the next, the mole has moved out. If one tunnel comes back, that is the main street. Give it one more watering and some garlic, right there.
[TROUBLE] If the hills keep coming after a month, look at your neighbors' yards. If the whole street has moles, they will keep visiting. That is when I talked with my neighbors, and we all did the castor oil on the same weekend. After that, the moles went to the empty field behind the houses. And remember, in very dry summers moles go deep and you see nothing, then in the fall rains they come up again. So start again every fall. My mother kept a little calendar by the back door, and on the first rainy weekend of October she wrote one word on it, mogura, so she would not forget. I do the same thing on my kitchen calendar now, in pencil, and every year it reminds me of her.
[FIX] Now, the fixing. When the moles are gone, rake the hills flat, step down the soft tunnels so your ankles are safe, and throw a little grass seed on the bare spots with a handful of good soil. Water gently every day for a week. In one month nobody will know they were there. Keep a little of the soil from the hills in a bucket, because it is the finest, softest soil in the whole yard. My mother used it for her seedlings in the spring, and she said the mogura had done her digging for her.
[MONEY] Let me do the arithmetic, because this house does not do villains, it does arithmetic. The man with the truck, four hundred dollars, and next spring maybe again. My way. A bottle of castor oil, a few dollars. A little dish soap you already have. Some garlic, a few dollars. Coffee grounds, nothing at all, you already throw them away every morning. Less than twenty dollars for the whole season.
[MEMORY] I remember one autumn evening in Osaka. I was maybe eight. My mother and I were on our knees in her little yard, next to her moss. She found a soft line in the moss, very straight. She did not say anything. She just poured a little of her oil water into it from a teapot, pushed a garlic clove into the ground, and patted it with her hand, the way you pat a child on the head. Then she said, there. Now go eat somewhere else, little digger. And it did.
[RECAP] So, here is everything one more time. Number one, castor oil and a little dish soap in a gallon of water, poured around the edge and over the whole lawn, again after ten days. Number two, garlic water and a peeled clove in little holes along the tunnel that is in use. Number three, used coffee grounds scratched into the flower beds and spooned into the tunnels. And my mother's bottle, humming at the edge of the lawn.
[COMMENT] Your homework this week is very small. Tonight, step on the tunnels in your yard and put a little stick next to each one. Tomorrow morning, look. And tell me in the comments, how many tunnels came back up? I want to know how busy your little digger is.
