[HOOK] And so many of you have written to me. My father was a lumberjack in the Upper Peninsula. My grandfather worked in a camp near the Canadian border. My great uncle was a teamster in Wisconsin. And you tell me you wish you knew what he ate out there. What kept him going in the snow.

[TABLE] First, how supper worked, so you can picture your grandpa there. The cook got up at four in the morning. Breakfast at five, by lantern light. The men took their lunch out to the cut, and they came back at dark, cold, wet, and hungry like you've never been hungry. The cookee banged the iron triangle by the door, and they came in stamping the snow off their boots.
[TABLE] No talking at the table. That was the rule in almost every camp. The cook was boss in the cook shack, and talking slowed down the eating, and forty men had to be fed and out in twenty minutes so the cook could clean up. So your grandpa ate fast and quiet, and he ate a lot. Those men burned seven or eight thousand calories a day out there.
[ONE] Supper number one. Pot beans with salt pork. Every camp in Minnesota had beans on the table nearly every day. Pinto or navy beans, a chunk of salt pork, an onion, cooked slow in a big kettle on the back of the range. If your grandpa worked the camps, he ate more beans than anything else in his life.
[ONE] The trick the good cooks knew was salt at the start, and a hard boil for ten minutes, and then just a whisper of a simmer for an hour and a half. The beans come out creamy, not hard. And the broth, what the old cooks called pot liquor, was never thrown out. It went over the beans and over the bread.
[ONE] Today, one pound of dry beans and a quarter pound of salt pork, with an onion, is about three dollars and forty cents for a pot that feeds eight. About forty-five cents a bowl. That's your grandpa's everyday supper, for less than the price of a candy bar.
[TWO] Supper number two. Baked beans, the Maine way. Out in the Maine woods, the camps made bean-hole beans. They dug a hole in the ground, built a fire in it all afternoon, set a big iron pot of beans with molasses and salt pork right down into the hot coals, covered it with dirt, and left it all night. In the morning they dug it up and it was dark and sweet and perfect.
[TWO] At home you use a slow oven. Cook the beans until they're nearly tender, then into a Dutch oven with salt pork, onion, molasses, a little brown sugar, dry mustard, a spoon of ketchup and a splash of vinegar. Three hundred degrees, about three hours. And the trick. The molasses goes in only when the beans are nearly soft. Put it in too early and they stay hard all day.
[TWO] Navy beans, salt pork, molasses and the rest, about four dollars and fifty cents for eight people. About fifty-five cents a plate. Saturday night baked beans with brown bread. If your grandpa came from Maine, he ate this every Saturday of his life.
[THREE] Supper number three. Salt pork and potatoes in cream gravy. This one was in every camp, everywhere, because salt pork kept all winter without ice and potatoes came out of the root cellar by the bushel. Slices of salt pork fried crisp, boiled potatoes, and a white gravy made in the pork fat.
[THREE] The trick is to soak the salt pork twenty minutes in cold water first, to pull out some of the salt. Then fry it slow until it's crisp. Pour off all but a quarter cup of fat, cook the flour in it a full minute until it smells nutty, then whisk in the milk a little at a time. Fold in the potatoes, crisp pork on top.
[THREE] A pound of salt pork, two pounds of potatoes, three cups of milk. About five dollars and eighty cents, and it feeds five. Around a dollar fifteen a plate. My men called it white supper, and they never once complained about it.
[FOUR] Supper number four. Pasties. If your grandpa worked the Iron Range or the Upper Peninsula of Michigan, he knew pasties. The Cornish miners brought them, and the loggers took them up. A whole supper folded into a crust you could carry in one hand, beef, potato, rutabaga and onion, baked in a half-moon of pastry.
[FOUR] The trick is in the cutting. Everything small and thin, about the size of your fingernail. The filling goes in raw, so if the pieces are big, they come out hard while the crust burns. A pat of butter on top of the filling before you fold it. Crimp the edge in little pleats. Bake about an hour until it's deep golden.
[FOUR] A pound of beef, potatoes, a rutabaga, an onion, flour and lard for the crust. About nine dollars and fifty cents for six big pasties. About a dollar sixty each. And the men ate them cold at noon out in the woods, wrapped in paper inside their coats, to keep them from freezing.
[FIVE] Supper number five. Booyah. If your grandpa was from Wisconsin, especially up around Green Bay, he'll know the word. It's a big stew made in a kettle for a crowd, chicken and beef, every vegetable you have, cabbage, tomatoes, potatoes, cooked for hours until it's so thick a spoon stands up in it for a second.
[FIVE] The trick is the old Wisconsin one. Booyah is never better than on the second day. Make it the day before, let it cool overnight, lift off the hard fat on top, and warm it slowly. And a splash of vinegar at the end, to wake it up.
[FIVE] Chicken legs and thighs, a piece of beef shank, a whole cellar of vegetables. About twenty dollars for a kettle that feeds thirteen. A dollar and a half a bowl. Booyah is how a whole town fed itself at a church picnic, and it's how a camp fed itself in January.
[SIX] Supper number six. Pea soup. If your grandpa worked with the French Canadian boys, and in the Minnesota and Maine woods there were a lot of them, he ate pea soup. Yellow split peas for the French, green for the Swedes, and they argued about it all winter. Cooked with a ham bone all afternoon until it was thick and smooth.
[SIX] The trick is to stir it and scrape the bottom every ten minutes, because split peas sink and scorch, and one burned layer flavors the whole pot. And a splash of vinegar at the end. With a ham bone you already had, about thirty cents a bowl. It's the cheapest supper on this list, and I made a whole video about it.
[SEVEN] Supper number seven. Beef and barley stew. This was the stew for the coldest weeks. Beef chuck in cubes, browned dark in lard, then onions, carrots, a cup of pearl barley, and a long slow simmer until the barley is plump and the beef falls apart. One kettle, one spoon, and a bowl that kept a man on his feet until morning.
[SEVEN] The trick is the browning. Pat the meat dry, toss it in flour, and brown it in two batches, never crowding the pot. Wet meat steams and goes gray. Dry meat gets a dark crust, and that crust and the brown bits on the bottom of the pot are the whole flavor of the broth. The barley stretches two pounds of beef into a pot for eight.
[SEVEN] Two pounds of chuck, a cup of barley, onions and carrots. About seventeen dollars for seven or eight bowls. Around two dollars and twenty cents. It's one of the dearer ones. In camp we made it when the meat sled came in, and the men knew it was a good week.
[EIGHT] Supper number eight. Cabbage rolls. The Finnish families on the Iron Range made them, and the Polish families, and the Swedes, all a little different. Cabbage leaves wrapped around ground meat and rice, browned in butter in a pan, then baked slow in broth, with a brown gravy over the top.
[EIGHT] The old-country trick is a spoon of molasses or dark syrup drizzled over the rolls in the hot pan. It caramelizes, browns the cabbage, and gives the gravy a dark, sweet edge. A head of cabbage and one pound of meat, stretched with rice, feeds eight people.
[EIGHT] About seven dollars for the whole pan, around a dollar a plate. Cabbage keeps for weeks in a cold cellar, wrapped in newspaper, so in the camps it was one of the last fresh things left in March.
[NINE] Supper number nine. Swedish meatballs in brown gravy. In the Minnesota camps, with so many Swedes and Norwegians in the crew, this was the Saturday treat. Little meatballs, soft inside and brown outside, in a gravy the men would mop up with bread until the plate shone.
[NINE] My mother's trick, and it's in my cookbook. Grate the onion, don't chop it. The juice goes into the meat and keeps the meatballs soft and tender. Soak the bread crumbs in milk first, a pinch of allspice and nutmeg, roll them the size of a walnut, brown them in butter, and make the gravy right in the same pan.
[NINE] A pound of ground beef and half a pound of pork, crumbs, an egg, butter, broth. About nine dollars for six people, a dollar and a half a plate, with boiled potatoes and a spoon of cranberry or lingonberry on the side.
[TEN] And supper number ten. The Sunday boiled dinner. Corned beef, simmered three hours, and then every root in the cellar into that same broth. Potatoes, carrots, rutabaga, onions, and the cabbage in wedges on top for the last fifteen minutes. Mustard and horseradish on the table. Every old logger I ever met remembered Sunday boiled dinner first.
[TEN] The trick is to taste the broth before the vegetables go in. If it's very salty, ladle some out and add plain water, because the vegetables soak up whatever the broth tastes like. And slice the meat thin, across the grain, or it chews like a boot.
[TEN] It's the dearest supper on this list. A corned beef brisket, a whole cellar of vegetables, about twenty-five dollars for eight people. About three dollars a plate. But it was Sunday, and the men rested, and the cook made sure that one supper a week tasted like home.
[BONUS] Now, before I add it all up, I owe you one more, because every camp had a supper that wasn't on any list. In our camp it was Saturday night hash. Whatever was left from the week, the corned beef ends, the cold boiled potatoes, an onion or two, all chopped together and fried hard in the biggest skillet I had until the bottom was brown and crisp.
[BONUS] The trick is patience. You press it down flat, you leave it alone, and you don't touch it for a good ten minutes. Then you turn it in big pieces, press again, and wait again. The crust is the whole point. A fried egg on top if the hens were laying, and a spoonful of ketchup if the store had it.
[BONUS] Nels, our teamster, said Saturday hash was the best meal of the week, and he meant it as a compliment to me, but really it was a compliment to the leftovers. It cost almost nothing, because everything in it was already paid for. That's the kind of supper your grandpa would recognize.
[BONUS] And here's something about those suppers you might not know. The men ate fast. Ten minutes, maybe fifteen, and not a word at the table. That was the rule in most camps. Talking was for the bunkhouse. So if your grandpa ate his supper quickly and quietly and wiped the plate clean with a piece of bread, now you know where he learned it.
