[STORY] When my mother turned sixty, her children were gone, my father was retired and sleeping in front of the television, and the house was very quiet. One night she told me on the telephone, Sachiko, I think my life is finished. I was twenty-seven, in a small apartment far away, and I did not know what to say. Two weeks later a letter came. [ONE] Number one, she started going to the market every day. Not once a week with a big list, like most people do. Every single morning, with one small cloth bag. [TWO] Number two, she started learning one new thing every year. At sixty-two it was shodo. Shodo, it means the way of writing, Japanese calligraphy with a brush and black ink. She was the oldest in the class, and her first characters looked like spiders walking. The teacher was thirty. My mother did not care. She went every Tuesday for six years. At sixty-eight, she started the koto, a long wooden harp with thirteen strings. At seventy-five, flower arranging. At eighty-one, she learned to fold a hundred kinds of paper cranes from a book. At ninety, she started to learn English words from the radio, ten words a week, so she could talk to my children on the telephone. Her first sentence to my son was, how is the weather, and he laughed for a week.
[TWO_B] My mother told me, the year you stop being a beginner is the year you start being old. My grandson puts them on his refrigerator anyway. Every January, pick your one new thing. Write it on the calendar. Small is fine.
[THREE] Number three, she started writing one letter every week. Every Sunday afternoon, after lunch, she sat at the low table with her good pen and wrote one letter by hand. To me, far away, to my brothers, to a cousin in the countryside, to her old teacher, to a neighbor who moved away. One letter, every week, for more than thirty years. She bought her stamps ten at a time. When people wrote back, she kept the letters in an old biscuit tin, and on rainy days she read them again. When the tin was full, she started a second tin, and then a third. She said, the telephone is for news, but a letter is for feelings, and you cannot hold a telephone call in your hand when you miss somebody.
[THREE_B] Most people today send a quick message on the phone and forget it in one minute. A letter, she said, somebody keeps. I still have one hundred and forty of her letters. [FOUR] Number four, she started cooking for one more person. When she was sixty-four, the widow next door, Mrs. Tanaka, lost her husband and stopped eating properly. She was getting thin. So my mother began cooking one more portion every Thursday and carrying it next door on a tray, covered with a cloth. Then Mrs. Tanaka started coming over to eat it at my mother's table. Then a third lady came. By the end, every Thursday there were five old women at that little table, eating rice and talking until dark. Each one brought something small. One brought pickles, one brought a sweet, one brought the gossip. Nobody was in charge, and nobody ate alone on a Thursday for twenty-five years.
[FOUR_B] My mother said, cooking for one is sad, cooking for two is a pleasure. She called it Thursday rice. It is not about the food. It is about having a reason to set the table nicely, and a reason for somebody to knock on your door. If you live alone now, pick one day. Cook one more portion. Knock on one door.
[FIVE] Number five, she started getting up from the floor thirty times a day. In an old Japanese house, there are no sofas. You sit on the tatami floor, on a thin cushion, at a low table. You eat on the floor, you read on the floor, you fold laundry on the floor, and every time, you get up again with your own legs. After sixty, a lot of her friends bought tall chairs and soft sofas. My mother did not. She said, the day I cannot get up from the floor, that will be the day I need a chair. That day did not come until she was ninety-eight. I counted once, when I visited her. [SIX] Number six, she started helping the children cross the street. In Japan, near every school, there are older ladies and men in a green armband and a little yellow flag who stand at the corners every morning and help the children cross. People call them the green aunties. My mother became one at sixty-six. Every school morning, rain or snow, from seven thirty to eight fifteen, she stood at the same corner by the post office with her yellow flag. She did it for twenty-two years.
[SIX_B] My mother knew every child by name. She knew who lost a tooth and who had a new baby brother. [SEVEN] Number seven, she started going to the public bath every evening. In Osaka there was a sento two streets from her house. [SEVEN_B] The sento was not about getting clean. She said, at home I am warm alone. At the sento I am warm with everybody. The same ladies came every day at the same hour. [EIGHT] Number eight, she started putting one coin a day in a jar. Every night before bed, she dropped one coin into an old glass jar on the shelf. One hundred yen, about the price of a small bun.
