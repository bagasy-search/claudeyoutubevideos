[HOOK] When I am gone, dear, my children will throw away nine out of every ten things in this house. I know it, because I did it to my mother's house in Osaka. Two weeks, my brother and me, and a mountain of bags at the curb. So at eighty, I follow eleven Japanese rules, and my children will not have to do what I did.
[HOOK_B] Maybe you have a closet you are afraid to open. Maybe your daughter already said, "Mom, what are we going to do with all of this?" and you laughed, and it hurt a little. It is not your fault. Nobody teaches us how to leave a house light. We only learn how to fill it.
[PROMISE] So here is what I will show you. Eleven rules, the order to do them in, and the one small box that matters more than all the furniture together. The first rule will surprise you, because it starts in the wrong place on purpose. And rule number eleven is the one I wish my mother had told me out loud.
[STORY] You know me. I am Sachiko. I came to America in nineteen seventy, and I have kept this little house for fifty years. In Japan we have a word for this work, seizen seiri. It means putting your things in order while you are still alive. It is not sad. It is a gift. My mother's house in Osaka had a closet we call an oshiire, with sliding paper doors, and when I opened it after she passed, it was packed to the ceiling. I still remember the smell of that house, old tatami and camphor wood, and the cold stone floor of the genkan, the little entry where we leave our shoes.
[ONE] Rule number one is to start with the closet, not with the photographs. Most people begin with the old albums, because that is what they worry about most. They sit down on the floor with one album, and three hours later they are crying, and nothing has left the house. Photographs stop you. Memories stop you. So you start with the easy things that have no story: clothes first, then books, then papers, then the drawers, and the memories last of all. By the time you reach the photographs, you are strong, and your hands know how to decide.
[ONE_2] Here is how I do it. Three bags by the door: one for keep, one for give, one for go. A kitchen timer for thirty minutes, no more. One shelf, one drawer, one corner a day. When the timer rings, you stop, even if you are winning, and the give bag goes in the car that same afternoon, before you change your mind. My mother used to say, a decision made at the door is made forever, and a decision taken to the basement is never made at all.
[ONE_B] My mother's oshiire had six guest futons for guests who never came anymore. My brother and I carried them out one by one, and I remember thinking, she could have let these go thirty years ago and nobody would have missed them.

[TWO] Rule number two is the four seasons rule for clothes. Most of us keep clothes for the person we might be one day: the dress for the wedding that never comes, the trousers that will fit again, the coat we wore once in nineteen eighty-two. In Japan, twice a year, we have koromogae, the day you change your closet from winter clothes to summer clothes. On that day, every piece you hold gets one question. Did I wear this in the last four seasons? If the answer is no, it goes, with a thank you. I keep enough for one week of each season, and the closet door closes with one finger.
[TWO_B] And here is a little trick if you cannot decide. On koromogae day, turn every hanger backward. When you wear something, hang it back the right way. Next season, everything still hanging backward has told you the truth by itself. You do not have to argue with it. Last October I had eleven backward hangers, and I gave away eleven pieces, and I have not missed a single piece, not even the purple blouse I loved in nineteen ninety.
[THREE] Rule number three is one of each. Go to a drawer right now and count the scissors. Count the flashlights, the tape measures, the pens that work and the pens that do not. Most houses have five of everything, because we cannot find the first one, so we buy another. In Japan we have a word, mottainai. People think it means keep everything. It does not. It means do not waste the life of a thing. A pair of scissors sleeping in the back of a drawer for twenty years is the real waste. So keep one, maybe two, and give the rest to someone who will use them this week.
[THREE_B] The school down the street always wants scissors and pens. The neighbor with three little boys wants the flashlights. A thing that is used is a happy thing, my mother said, and a thing in a drawer is only waiting. When I counted my own drawers two years ago, I found seven pairs of scissors and fourteen flashlights, and nine of them had no batteries.
[FOUR] Rule number four is the just in case drawer. You know the one. Plastic bags folded inside other plastic bags. Rubber bands. Twist ties. Cords for machines you threw away ten years ago. Empty jars and nice boxes, because they are too nice to throw out. Every house in the world has this drawer, and it grows like a garden. My rule is simple: the just in case things live in one bag, and when the bag is full, the oldest ones go. One bag, not one room. Nobody ever needed forty jars just in case. And the cords, dear. Take a piece of tape and write on each cord the name of its machine. If you cannot name the machine, the cord is an orphan, and it goes today.
[FIVE] Rule number five is for the gifts you never liked. Most people keep them out of guilt. The vase from your sister-in-law, the sweater from a friend, the set of glasses still in its box. In Japan we give gifts all year, so my mother's house was full of boxed towels and gift soap, still in the paper, from forty years of New Year visits. Here is what I believe. The gift did its job on the day it was given. It said, I thought of you. That job is finished. So hold it, say thank you out loud, and let it go to someone who will love it. I do this, and I sleep fine. If it is from someone who is gone, take one photograph of it on the table where it lived, and write their name on the back. The photograph keeps the memory, and it takes no room at all.
[SIX] Rule number six is to ask your children what they want, and believe the answer. Most of us are sure they want the dining table, the china cabinet, the good dishes for twelve. Dear, they do not. They have their own houses, and their houses are small. When I asked my son, he thought for a long time and said, "Mom, I want the little teacup you drink from every morning, and the card with your handwriting." That was it. A teacup and a card. So ask them, at the table, with a pencil, and write down what each one says. Then the big furniture can go now, while you can still choose where it goes.
[SIX_B] And when they say they want nothing, do not be hurt. That is not love leaving. That is them telling you that you are what they wanted, not the cabinet.

[MOTHER] Let me tell you what those two weeks in Osaka were really like. My brother and I slept on the floor of her empty room. Every morning a small truck came for the bags, and every afternoon we found another closet. We were not sad the whole time. Mostly we were tired, and a little angry, and then sorry for being angry. I do not want that for my children. I want them to come to this house, drink tea, open one box, and go home lighter.
[SEVEN] Rule number seven is to give your grown children their own things back. Their trophies, their school papers, the stuffed rabbit from second grade, the boxes in the basement with their names on them. Most parents keep these for forty years, like a museum nobody visits. Here is what I did. I called each child and said, "Your boxes are ready. You have until the end of the month. After that, I decide." My daughter came that same Saturday. She kept three things and laughed at the rest. The basement breathed again. My son lives far away, so I mailed his boxes, two of them, with a note that said, keep what you love, and the rest is yours to let go. He called me that night laughing about his old baseball cards. It was the best phone call of the year.
[EIGHT] Rule number eight is for the photographs, and now you are ready for them. Most of us keep every photograph, the blurry ones too, in shoeboxes nobody opens. When I emptied my mother's house, I found hundreds of faces I did not know. Nobody alive could tell me who they were. So here is my rule. Keep the best one or two from each day, not twenty. On the back, in soft pencil, write the names, the place and the year. A photograph with names on the back is a story. Without names, it is just paper, and your children will not know what to do with it. My grandson sat with me for three Sunday afternoons and put the best ones in the computer, and then we made one small album for each child, with their own baby pictures in it. The shoeboxes went out, and nobody cried, not even me.
[NINE] Rule number nine is to give with warm hands. There is an old saying, it is better to give with warm hands than with cold ones. Most of us save the good things to be given after we are gone. But then you never see the face. Last spring I gave my granddaughter my mother's pearl ring, the one I wore at my wedding. She cried, I cried, and now I see it on her finger every Sunday. If something is meant for someone, give it now. You get to see the smile. That is your reward for being brave.
[TEN] Rule number ten is the folder for the day you cannot speak. In Japan, many older people now keep what we call an ending note. It is a simple notebook. Where the bank papers are. Which drawer has the house key and the car key. The insurance papers, the phone numbers of the friends who must be called. What you want and what you do not want. Most families spend weeks looking for these things. Mine is one blue folder, in the second drawer of my desk, and my children know exactly where it is. Make yours this week. It is the kindest paper you will ever write. Put in it who will take the cat, which neighbor has the spare key, and the passwords written on one sheet, in big clear letters. Your children will open that folder and feel you holding their hand.
[ELEVEN] Rule number eleven is the one box. My mother kept one, and I did not know it until the end. In the back of that packed oshiire, behind the futons, there was a small box of paulownia wood, no bigger than a shoebox. Inside was a letter for me and a letter for my brother, a lock of our baby hair wrapped in paper, and a list in her handwriting of who should get what. Everything else in the house we could let go without guilt, because she had already told us what mattered. That box is the only thing I carried home to America.
[ELEVEN_B] So my last rule is this. Choose one box. Put in it only what you truly want them to keep: a letter to each one, a few photographs with names, one small thing with your story in it. Tell them where it is. Then the rest of the house can go, and nobody has to feel bad about a single bag at the curb. That is how you leave a house light, dear. Not empty, just light.
[RECAP] Let me say them all again, so you can keep them. One, start with the closet, not the photographs. Two, the four seasons rule for clothes. Three, one of each. Four, one bag for just in case. Five, thank the gifts and let them go. Six, ask your children and believe them. Seven, give their own things back. Eight, the best photographs with names on the back. Nine, give with warm hands. Ten, the ending note in one folder. Eleven, the one box.
[HONEST] I want to be honest with you. You will not do all eleven this month, and that is fine. My mother's house took fifty years to fill. Yours will not empty in a weekend. But one drawer every Saturday is fifty two drawers in a year, and that is most of a house. Start small, and do not stop. And when it feels hard, when you hold your husband's old sweater and your throat closes, sit down, have some tea, and keep only the one thing that smells like him. The rest can keep someone warm this winter. That is not forgetting. That is love moving to where it is needed.
[HOUSE] Let me tell you what my house looks like today, so you can see it is possible. In the genkan, three pairs of shoes, not twelve. In the spare room, a bed for my grandson and nothing under it. In the basement, two shelves, and every box has a name and a date on it. In my desk, the blue folder. And on the top shelf in the living room, my own small wooden box, with a letter for each of my children already inside. It took me six years, dear, one drawer at a time, and I am not finished. But when my children visit now, they sit down and drink tea with me. Nobody looks at the closet with worry anymore.
