[STORY] The first funeral dinner I ever worked was when I was twenty-two. I was the youngest woman in that basement, so I got the job nobody wanted, which was peeling potatoes for a hundred people. Bernice ran that kitchen like a general. She had one rule. Nobody sits down to eat until the family has eaten first. I thought she was being bossy. It took me years to understand that she was not being bossy at all. She was giving the family somewhere to be fed, and she was giving the rest of us something to do with our hands.
[VERSE1] Here is the first lesson, and the verse it comes from. It is from the sixth chapter of Galatians, the second verse. It says: "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ." I used to read that as a nice thing to say at a funeral. Now I read it as a job description.
[L1] When Harold died, the casseroles came for two weeks. Then the quiet came. Nobody means to forget. Life simply picks back up for everybody but you. I learned more about grief in the third month than I learned in the first two weeks. So now I keep a little note on the calendar, and about three months after a loss, I take a covered dish to the door, or I just pick up the phone and say, honey, I was thinking about you.
[L1B] The lesson is not about the food, though the food helps. The lesson is about the timing. The crowd comes the first week, when everybody is looking. The ones who stay are the ones who come back when the house is quiet and the mail is piling up on the table. Be one of those people. Write down the name of someone whose loss was a few months ago, and go see them this week.
[L2] The second lesson comes from the bread. For sixty years the church ladies made the bread for every funeral dinner, the yeast rolls and the loaves, and you do not make bread fast. While it rose, we talked about the person who had died. We told the stories, the bad jokes, the way he whistled through his teeth at the feed store. I think that was the real reason we made it. The bread gave our hands something slow to do while our hearts caught up.
[VERSE2] The verse for that one is from the sixth chapter of John, the thirty-fifth verse. It says: "I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger." Bread is a plain thing, honey. It takes flour, water, a little salt and a lot of waiting. So does grief.
[STORY2] One autumn we lost a man who had run the grain elevator for forty years. His widow could not stand to look at the kitchen. So we moved her chair to the window, where she could see the parking lot, and we put the rolls in front of her. She did not eat much. But she said the bread smelled like her mother's, and she cried, and that was the first time she had cried in a week. Food does not fix a broken heart. It gives a broken heart a place to sit.
[L3] The third lesson is the empty chair. Most families have one. At a funeral dinner, somebody always sets one place too many, or one place too few, and nobody knows what to say about it. I still set out two cups some mornings without thinking. Harold has been gone nine years. Grief does not go away, honey. It gets smaller around the edges, and you grow bigger around it.
[VERSE3] The verse for the empty chair is from the Psalms, the hundred and forty-seventh psalm, the third verse: "He healeth the broken in heart, and bindeth up their wounds." I do not know a better promise for a house with one chair empty. He is not in a hurry about it either.
[L3B] So here is what you can do at a funeral dinner, or at any table with a gap in it. Say the name. Say the name out loud, and let the family say it back to you. Nobody in that basement ever got hurt by hearing the name of the one they lost. They got hurt by the silence that followed when nobody said it.
[L4] The fourth lesson took me sixty years to learn, and I was the one who needed it. I fed half the county for most of my life. When I broke my hip, I could not ask anyone to bring me supper. It was pride. I thought a church lady was the one who brings the casserole, not the one who receives it. But the Lord does not keep a list of who is allowed to need help. [VERSE4] The verse for that is from the seventh chapter of Matthew, the seventh verse: "Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you." [STORY3] When my hip healed, the women who had brought me supper told me something I have never forgotten. [L5] The fifth lesson is about talk, and every church kitchen has it. Gossip comes in with the coffee urn and sits down at the long table with the pies. [VERSE5] The verse is from the book of Proverbs, the eighteenth chapter, the twenty-first verse: "Death and life are in the power of the tongue." [RECIPE_START] All right, now the funeral potatoes. This is the dish my church ladies carried to grieving houses more than any other. Every family in mourning got a pan. It is comfort you can eat with a spoon. This recipe serves twelve, and the amounts are exactly the ones in my little cookbook, page thirty-three, so you can check me.
[ING1] Here is what you need. One thirty-ounce bag of frozen shredded hash browns, thawed. One ten-and-a-half-ounce can of cream soup. Two cups of sour cream. Half a cup of butter, melted, and we will split that in two. Half a cup of onion, finely chopped. Two cups of shredded sharp cheddar. One teaspoon of salt, and half a teaspoon of pepper. And two cups of crushed cornflakes for the top.
[STEP1] Step one. Heat the oven to three hundred fifty degrees Fahrenheit, and butter a nine by thirteen pan. The butter keeps the bottom from sticking when it bakes, and the pan is the right size for a crowd. A smaller pan makes the whole thing too thick.
[STEP2] Step two is the one I want you to pay attention to, honey. Take the thawed hash browns and squeeze them dry in a dish towel, a handful at a time. Wring them like you mean it. That water is what turns cheesy potatoes into potato soup, and I have served a lot of soup by mistake in my life.
[STEP3] Step three. In a bowl, mix the cream soup, the sour cream, a quarter cup of the melted butter, the chopped onion, the cheese, the salt and the pepper. Stir it until it is one smooth, creamy mixture. This is the sauce, and it does the work of holding everything together.
[STEP4] Step four. Fold the dry potatoes into that creamy mixture, gently, so they stay in shreds. Then spread it all into the buttered pan and smooth the top with the back of a spoon. Do not press it down hard. You want it to stay light.
[STEP5] Step five is the topping, and the topping is where people go wrong. Toss the two cups of crushed cornflakes with the last quarter cup of melted butter. Then scatter it evenly over the top. We add it last on purpose, because cereal that sits under a sauce goes soft, and nobody wants a soggy top at a funeral.
[STEP6] Step six. Bake it uncovered, for fifty to fifty-five minutes, until it is golden on top and bubbling at the edges. Do not cover it, honey. Covering it traps the steam, and the steam is the enemy of a crisp topping. When the edges are bubbling and the top is brown, take it out and let it rest a few minutes before you serve it.
[BIGNUM] So that is three hundred fifty degrees, and fifty to fifty-five minutes, uncovered. Write those two numbers on the card in your kitchen. Those are the two numbers my church ladies never got wrong.
[MISTAKE] The three mistakes I see most, and I have seen them in every church kitchen in Iowa. The first is a watery pan, from potatoes that were never squeezed. Wring them. The second is a soggy topping, from crumbs added too early and covered up. Add them last and bake uncovered. The third is bland cheese, from a mild cheddar. Use the sharp cheddar. It makes a difference you can taste from across the room.
[SCALE] For a crowd, a nine by thirteen pan gives you twelve to fifteen scoops, and you can put it together the night before and add the topping in the morning, with ten extra minutes in the oven. For one or two people, build it in two small eight by eight pans and freeze one of them before you add the topping. It keeps in the fridge for three days and in the freezer for three months. And if you want a main dish, stir in some diced ham. Nobody has ever complained about ham.
