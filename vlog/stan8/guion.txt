[TRICK_ONE] Trick number one. The dollar thing is baking soda. Yes, the box in your refrigerator. Baking soda changes the surface of the meat, it makes it hold on to water, and that's what makes the bite soft. You need about half a teaspoon for every pound of meat. Mix it in with a little water or with your hands, and let it sit for twenty minutes. Then rinse it well under cold water and pat it dry, because if you leave it on, it tastes soapy, and then you blame me. People think more is better. More is how you ruin a good steak. Half a teaspoon per pound. Write that down.
[TRICK_TWO] Trick number two. Look at the grain before you touch the knife. On a chuck or a round, the muscle fibers run in long lines, and you can see them if you look. Those lines are the enemy of tender. You cut across them, not along them. A cut against the grain means every bite is already half broken down for your teeth. Most people do it backwards because they're in a hurry and they cut the roast into whatever shape is easy. Slow down. Turn the piece in your hand. Find the lines. Then cut across them.
[TRICK_THREE] Trick number three. Salt early. Not the morning of. Salt the meat the night before, or at least forty minutes ahead, and put it in the fridge uncovered on a plate. Dry skin, better crust, and the salt goes all the way into the middle. The old rule says salt right before the pan, because salt draws out the juice. That's half true. The salt draws it out, and then the meat takes it back in, carrying the salt with it. Give it time to do both. If you salt right before the pan, you get a salty outside and a bland middle, and you wonder why.
[TRICK_FOUR] Trick number four. Pull it out of the cold and let it sit for thirty minutes before the pan. Not two hours. Thirty minutes. A piece of meat that goes into a hot pan straight from the case cooks its outside before the inside even wakes up. You get the gray ring I told you about. Thirty minutes on the counter, and it starts to cook evenly from edge to middle. Cover it with a clean towel so the flies leave it alone, and that's all.
[TRICK_FIVE] Trick number five. Don't hit it with a hammer. People buy those flat metal mallets and go crazy on a round steak until it's the size of a newspaper and as thin as a receipt. That breaks the muscle, but it also tears it, and it dries out in the pan the second it touches the iron. Tap it, don't beat it. Take it to about three-quarters of an inch and leave it a little thick in the middle, because the middle is the part that's going to be pink and happy.
[TRICK_SIX] Trick number six. Use an iron pan, and get it hot before the meat goes in. Hot enough that a drop of water jumps around and disappears in a second. You want the crust in the first ninety seconds, and then you leave it alone. The biggest mistake I see at the counter is people flipping the meat every thirty seconds, like they're nervous. Every time you move it, you cool the pan. Put it down, walk away, let the crust form. One flip. That's the whole job.
[TRICK_SEVEN] Trick number seven. Oil goes on the meat, not in the pan. Rub a thin film of oil over the meat with your hands, and salt it again right before it goes down. That way the oil's already touching the meat when the heat hits, and the crust forms faster and more evenly. Pour it into the iron and it smokes before you're ready, and then your kitchen smells like a fire, and you open the window in January for no reason.
[TRICK_EIGHT] Trick number eight. Pork shoulder, and any pork that's going to be cheap and tough. Make a brine. One tablespoon of kosher salt for every cup of water. Two cups of water, two tablespoons of salt, and a spoon of sugar if you like it a little sweet. Put the pork in for one hour, no more, and then rinse. That's enough to hold the water inside when it hits the heat. Longer than that and it turns the meat soft and mealy, like wet bread, and you can't fix it after. One hour. Set a timer on your phone. I'll wait.
[TRICK_NINE] Trick number nine. Ground beef. The store version has been squeezed and packed so hard it's a brick. Don't squeeze it. Don't knead it. Don't make a meatball by rolling it tight in your fist like you're angry at it. Take it out of the paper, put it in a bowl, and lightly shape it with your fingertips. Loose meat is tender meat. Eighty percent lean, twenty percent fat, and the fat does the rest. My father used to say you should be able to see the hand that made it. He wasn't talking about art. He was talking about meatballs.
[TRICK_TEN] Trick number ten. Acid. Vinegar, lemon, wine. Lots of people think acid makes meat tender, so they soak a chuck in vinegar overnight and then wonder why it's mushy. Don't. A short soak, twenty minutes, is fine. Overnight, the outside turns soft and chalky and the middle stays tough, because the acid never gets deep enough to do anything useful. Acid is for flavor. It's not a tenderizer. The baking soda does the tenderizing, and a splash of acid at the end brings the whole thing to life.
[TRICK_ELEVEN] Trick number eleven. Stop cooking a good steak to death. For a thick steak, pull it at about one hundred thirty degrees for medium rare, and let it rest. Your thermometer is worth more than anybody's opinion, including mine. Pull the meat early, because it keeps climbing in the pan and on the plate after you take it off. That's called carryover, and it's the reason so many good steaks end up gray at the table.
[TRICK_TWELVE] Trick number twelve. Rest it. Everybody cuts into a steak the second it comes off the heat, and the juice runs all over the cutting board, and then the meat is dry. Give it five to ten minutes on a plate, with loose foil over the top, not tight, or it steams and goes soft. The juice goes back into the meat where it belongs. Use the time to wash your hands and pour yourself something cold. You earned it.
[TRICK_THIRTEEN] Trick number thirteen. Cheap roasts need the low and slow, and a lid. A chuck roast in a heavy pot with a little water, a cover, and a low oven, about three hundred degrees, for three hours or more. That's where the connective tissue gives up and turns to silk. Don't open the lid every twenty minutes to check on it. Every time you open it, you let out the steam, and the steam is what's doing the work. Look at it once at the start, once in the middle, and then leave it alone like it's your grandmother's Sunday nap.
[TRICK_FOURTEEN] Trick number fourteen. Freeze it for twenty minutes before you slice. A roast that's been sitting in the fridge is soft, and it tears when you cut it thin. Twenty minutes in the freezer, and the meat firms up just enough. Then your knife goes through it like it was meant to. Thin slices, against the grain, every time. That's the sandwich trick. That's the stir-fry trick. That's the one the customers ask me about when they come back for the second pound.
[TRICK_FIFTEEN] Trick number fifteen. Don't crowd the pan. Four pieces of meat in a pan built for two, and you're not frying anymore, you're steaming. The pan drops in temperature, the juice comes out, and the meat turns gray and sad in its own water. Cook in batches. Leave room between the pieces so the steam can get out. It takes five extra minutes, and it's the difference between a steak and a boot. Your patience is an ingredient too.
[TRICK_SIXTEEN] Trick number sixteen. Check the color of the meat in the case before you buy it. Bright red with a little white fat. Not brown at the edges, not slimy, not sitting in a pool of its own juice in the tray. A cheap cut that's fresh is better than an expensive cut that's been sitting there since Sunday. Ask the person behind the counter what came in today. If they don't know, buy somewhere else. That's not a trick for the meat. That's a trick for your wallet, and it's the one that saves the most money in a year.
[TRICK_SEVENTEEN] Trick number seventeen. The last one is the one nobody believes. Don't throw away the juice in the pan. Take that brown stuff stuck to the iron, add a splash of water, scrape it up with a wooden spoon, let it bubble for a minute, and pour it over the meat. That's the gravy you'll spend twenty dollars on at a restaurant, and it's sitting in your pan for free. Every good sauce I ever made started as something stuck to the bottom of a pan that somebody was too lazy to scrub.
