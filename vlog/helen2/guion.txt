[HOOK] Stop boiling your cabbage in water. Put the big pot away, because that pot is the reason your cabbage tastes like nothing. Last month a retired mailman named Walter stood right where you are, at my counter, and told me his house smells like a wet sock every Sunday. His wife cuts a whole head of cabbage, fills the big pot with water, and boils it for forty-five minutes. It comes out gray and soft and it floats. Then they pour half of it down the drain, and the flavor goes down the drain with the water.
[HOOK_B] I sold him one smoked ham hock. It cost him two dollars and eleven cents. I told him what to do with it, and the next Saturday he came back and bought three more. He said, Helen, my wife cried. She said it tasted like her mother's Sunday dinner. That is what I am going to give you today. The same head of cabbage, about two dollars of the right pork, and one cup of liquid instead of a whole pot. Tonight, in your kitchen, with the pan you already have.
[PROMISE] I am going to give you fifteen things, in the order you cook them. Number two is the cheap piece of pork almost nobody asks for, and I will tell you exactly what to say at the counter. Number six is the Sunday version, the pot liquor my mother made, and it turns one ham hock into two suppers. And number eleven is what to do with that bone after, because most people throw away the best part. Some of the folks who sell you the pretty little trays will not love me for this one. That is all right.
[INTRO] You know me, I am Helen. Forty-one years behind this counter, in a little meat market in Iowa, and my father cut meat here before me. Most of my customers are retired, on a fixed income, cooking for one or two. And cabbage is still one of the cheapest good vegetables in the whole store. So grab a pen, honey. Let us cook it the right way.
[STORY] This trick is older than me. When my father opened this shop, the farm families around here still butchered their own hogs every fall, on the first cold week of November. Nothing went to waste. The hams and the bacon went to the smokehouse, and the hocks and the trimmings went into the cabbage pot all winter long. Those were the cheap pieces then, and they are the cheap pieces now. My mother came here from Korea, and the church ladies in this town taught her Sunday cabbage the first winter she was here. She went home and made it better. I watched her do it at our stove every Sunday for twenty years.
[STORY_B] And here is the thing nobody tells you. Those farm wives did not have money for a big roast every week. They had a garden full of cabbage and a little bit of smoked pork. That was the whole trick. A little pork does not have to be the meal. It is the seasoning for the meal. Once you understand that, two dollars of pork can feed you better than twenty dollars of steak.
[T01] Number one. Stop drowning it. When cabbage sits in a big pot of boiling water, two things happen. The flavor leaks out into the water, and the longer it boils, the stronger that sulfur smell gets. That smell is the cabbage telling you it has been cooked too long. So we do the opposite. One cup of liquid in the bottom of a heavy pan, a lid on top, and the cabbage cooks in its own steam. Twelve to fifteen minutes, not forty-five. Your cabbage stays green and sweet, and your house smells like supper, not like a wet sock.
[T02] Number two is the two dollar pork, and this is the heart of the whole thing. You do not need a big piece of meat. You need a little bit of smoked, salty pork fat to carry the flavor. Three pieces work. A smoked ham hock, about a pound, usually two or three dollars. A piece of salt pork, about a quarter pound. Or bacon ends and pieces. That last one is the secret. When we slice bacon, the odd ends and the uneven pieces cannot go in the nice package. So we sell them in a bag, for a lot less than the bacon.
[T02_B] Here is what you say at the counter. Do you have any bacon ends and pieces, or a smoked hock? That is all. If they do not have the bag out, ask anyway, because a lot of counters keep them in the back. Half a pound of bacon ends is enough for a whole head of cabbage. Most weeks, in my case, that is under two dollars. It is the same smoke and the same salt as the expensive bacon. It is just not as pretty, and your cabbage does not care what it looks like.
[T03] Number three. Start the pork in a cold pan. This is where most people go wrong with bacon. They heat the pan very hot, throw the pork in, and the outside burns while the fat stays inside the meat. Put the bacon ends in your cast iron skillet or your heavy pot while it is still cold. Turn the heat to medium. In about eight or ten minutes the fat melts out slowly, and you get two or three tablespoons of clear, smoky drippings in the bottom of the pan. That fat is your flavor. Take the crispy bits out with a spoon and set them on a plate. They go back in at the end.
[T04] Number four is how you cut it. Pull off the two or three outside leaves if they are torn or tired. Cut the head in half from top to bottom, right through the core. Then cut each half into quarters. Now you choose. For the Sunday pot, leave the core on each wedge, because the core holds the leaves together and the wedge comes out of the pot in one piece. For the skillet, cut the core out and slice the cabbage in ribbons about half an inch wide. Thin shreds turn to mush. Half an inch keeps a little bite.
[T04_B] And if you buy a red cabbage, here is one thing to know. Red cabbage turns a funny blue or gray color when you cook it in plain water, because most tap water is not sour enough to hold the color. Add the tablespoon of vinegar at the start instead of the end, and it stays a beautiful deep red. It is the same head, the same price, and it looks like you bought it at a fancy market. Savoy cabbage, the one with the crinkly leaves, cooks faster, so take five minutes off.
[T05] Number five is picking the head. Pick it up. A good cabbage feels heavy for its size, like a bowling ball, not like a balloon. The leaves should be tight and shiny, squeaky when you rub them. If the leaves are loose or there are little brown spots at the bottom, put it back. A three pound green cabbage usually costs about two dollars. And that is a lot of food, honey. A three pound head cooks down to about six good servings.
[T06] Number six is the Sunday version, the way my mother made it. Put one smoked ham hock in a pot with six cups of water, one onion cut in half and a bay leaf. Lid on, low heat, and let it simmer for an hour and a half, until the meat starts to come off the bone. That smoky water is called pot liquor. Now take out about four cups and save them. Leave about two cups in the pot, set your cabbage wedges in it, core side down, put the lid back on, and cook them fifteen minutes. That is the whole secret of Sunday cabbage. The cabbage only touches a little liquid, and that liquid already tastes like smoked pork.
[T06_B] And the four cups you saved are your next supper. Put them in a jar in the refrigerator. Two days later, cook a pot of beans in it, or potatoes, or rice. That is why one ham hock is two suppers. My mother never threw out a cup of pot liquor in her life, and she fed six of us on a butcher's pay.
[T07] Number seven. Lid on first, lid off at the end. For the skillet cabbage, put the ribbons in the hot drippings and stir them so every piece gets a little shine. Add half a cup of water or broth, put the lid on, and let it steam for eight minutes. Then take the lid off, turn the heat up a little, and leave it alone for three or four minutes. The water cooks off and the edges start to brown. Those brown edges are where the sweetness is. That is the difference between cabbage that is cooked and cabbage that tastes like somebody loves you.
[T08] Number eight. Salt it last. This is the mistake I hear the most. Smoked pork is already salty. A ham hock and a bag of bacon ends give the cabbage plenty of salt while it cooks. If you salt at the start the way you would a pot of water, it comes out too salty, and you cannot take it back. So do not add any salt until the very end. Taste a piece. Then add a pinch, if it needs it. Most of the time it will not.
[T09] Number nine is the little splash that makes people ask for your recipe. When the cabbage is done, turn the heat off and add one tablespoon of apple cider vinegar and a quarter teaspoon of sugar. Stir it and wait one minute. The vinegar wakes up the smoke and cuts the fat, so it does not taste greasy. The sugar rounds off the edges. You will not taste either one by itself. You will just taste better cabbage. And plenty of black pepper. Cabbage loves black pepper.
[T10] Number ten. Onion first, cabbage second. If you have one yellow onion, slice it and cook it in the pork drippings for five minutes before the cabbage goes in. It goes soft and a little golden, and it melts into the cabbage. One onion costs about fifty cents and it makes the whole pan taste like it cooked all afternoon. If you have a clove of garlic, it goes in for the last minute only. Garlic burns fast in hot fat, and burnt garlic is bitter.
[T11] Number eleven, the part most people throw away. When the ham hock is done, do not throw it in the trash. Let it cool until you can hold it. Pull off the skin and the fat, and underneath you will find real meat, sometimes a good half cup of it. Chop it and stir it into the cabbage. Now it is not a side dish anymore, it is supper. And the bone still has flavor in it. Wrap it, put it in the freezer, and next week it goes into a pot of beans or split pea soup.
[T12] Number twelve is the smoked sausage version, for when you want it heartier. One link of smoked sausage, about a quarter pound, usually around a dollar and a half. Slice it in coins as thick as your finger and brown them in the skillet before the onion goes in, about two minutes on each side. Take them out, cook your cabbage in the same pan, and put them back for the last five minutes. The sausage leaves a brown crust in the bottom of the pan, and the cabbage picks up every bit of it. That is a whole supper in one skillet for under four dollars.
[T13] Number thirteen is the one the church ladies brought to every potluck in this town. Cabbage and noodles. Cook half a pound of wide egg noodles in salted water, drain them, and toss them right into the skillet with the browned cabbage and the crispy pork bits. Add one tablespoon of butter and a lot of black pepper. Half a bag of noodles is about seventy-five cents. It feeds four people, and I have never once brought any of it home from a potluck.
[T14] Number fourteen. Keep the drippings. When you cook a bag of bacon ends, you will have more fat than you need for one pan of cabbage. Do not pour it down the sink, it clogs your pipes. Let it cool for a few minutes, pour it through a small strainer into a clean jar, put the lid on, and keep it in the refrigerator. A spoonful of that fat is how you fry an egg, warm up green beans or start your next pan of cabbage. My mother kept that jar next to the stove for fifty years, and so do I.
[T15] And number fifteen, Sunday dinner itself. Set the cabbage wedges on a warm platter, spoon the pot liquor over them, scatter the crispy pork bits and the chopped ham on top, and crack black pepper over all of it. Put a pan of cornbread or a few boiled potatoes next to it, and that is the meal my mother put on the table every Sunday after church. A head of cabbage, two dollars of pork, and the house smelling like home.
[T15_B] If you cook for one, here is how I do it. Make the whole head on Sunday. Eat a wedge with cornbread that day. On Tuesday, fry a scoop of the leftover cabbage in the skillet until the edges are brown, and put a fried egg on top. On Thursday, stir the last of it into the pot liquor with a few potatoes for soup. Cooked cabbage keeps three or four days in the refrigerator. Get it in there within two hours of cooking, in a container with a lid.
[TROUBLE] Now, a few problems I hear about at the counter. If your cabbage came out too salty, you salted it before you tasted it. Add a peeled potato cut in chunks and a cup of water, and simmer ten minutes, then take the potato out. If it came out mushy, it cooked too long or the pieces were too thin. Twelve to fifteen minutes, half inch ribbons. If it tastes greasy, you kept too much fat. Pour off all but two or three tablespoons before the cabbage goes in. And if it tastes bitter, either the garlic burned or the cabbage was old. Go back to number five and pick a heavy one.
[TROUBLE_B] Two more. If your ham hock is still tough after an hour and a half, it is not ruined, it just needs more time. Some hocks are bigger and some come from older hogs. Give it another thirty minutes with the lid on and check again. And if your store has no bacon ends and no hocks at all, buy a quarter pound of salt pork and rinse it under the tap first, because it is very salty. Or cut two strips of regular bacon into pieces. Two strips is enough to season a whole skillet.
[MONEY] Let me do the arithmetic, because that is what my customers care about. One three pound head of cabbage, about two dollars. Half a pound of bacon ends, or one smoked hock, about two dollars. One onion, fifty cents. A splash of vinegar and a little sugar, maybe a dime. That is about four dollars and sixty cents for six servings. Under eighty cents a plate. And the pot liquor gives you a second supper of beans or potatoes for almost nothing. A plate like that at a diner costs you twelve or fourteen dollars. At home, on a Sunday, it costs less than a cup of coffee.
[MONEY_B] Here is a simple week, for one or two people. Sunday, the ham hock and the cabbage wedges. Tuesday, the fried cabbage with an egg. Wednesday, a pot of beans in the saved pot liquor with the bone. Friday, cabbage and noodles. Four suppers built on one head of cabbage, one ham hock and one bag of bacon ends, for about eight dollars all together. When you live on a fixed income, that is a week you can breathe.
[CLOSE] So here is your homework. Do not try all fifteen this week. Do two. Next time you are at the meat counter, ask for bacon ends and pieces or a smoked hock. And next time you cook cabbage, put away the big pot and use one cup of liquid with the lid on. That is all. Then come back and tell me in the comments: how did your mother cook cabbage? With pork, with butter, or in a big pot of water? Be honest. I will not tell her.
