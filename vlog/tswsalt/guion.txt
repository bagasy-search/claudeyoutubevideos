[INTRO] If you are new here, this is the Scrap Workshop. I build heat out of things people throw away. Old oil, old metal, old parts. The nights are getting cold, and the cheapest heat there is, is heat made from something that was headed for the trash.
[INTRO] Fire in a hole is about as old as people. Hunters and soldiers have dug fire pits for hundreds of years, because the ground hides the flame, blocks the wind, and holds the heat. One of the best known is called a Dakota fire hole. Two holes in the ground, joined by a tunnel. One for the fire, one for the air.
[INTRO] I took that old design and gave it a modern fuel. Instead of sticks, a slow drip of used oil. Instead of a dirt floor, an old steel bucket with sand in it. And the salt, well, we will find out together.
[SAFETY] One quick word before the shovel comes out. This is an outdoor fire, always. Dig it on bare dirt, far from the house, the shed, dry grass and anything that hangs low. Call before you dig so you do not hit a buried line. Keep a bucket of sand and an extinguisher right next to it, and keep the oil jug a few feet away from the hole. That is it. Let's dig.
[MATERIALS] Here is everything for this build. One old steel bucket, about ten inches across, the kind paint or roofing tar comes in. Burn it out first so there is nothing left inside. A piece of three inch steel pipe, eighteen inches long, for the burner. A short piece of four inch pipe or an old coffee can with both ends cut off, for the air tunnel.
[MATERIALS] Then the drip. A plastic jug, a few feet of quarter inch copper line and one small brass needle valve. A wooden stake to hang the jug on. A bag of coarse salt, the kind for water softeners or icy steps. A bucket of dry sand. And a scrap of old steel grate for the pot to sit on.
[MATERIALS] Tools. A shovel, a drill, a step bit, a tape measure and a marker. No welding, no grinder needed if the bucket is already open on top.
[STEP1] Step one, the fire hole. Dig a round hole a little wider than your bucket, about twelve inches across and sixteen inches deep. Keep the sides straight. Pile the dirt to one side, you will use it later to pack around the bucket.
[STEP1] Why sixteen inches? The bucket goes in with its rim about two inches below the ground. That keeps the flame below the wind, and it keeps the hot steel away from feet and dogs.
[STEP1] If your ground is rocky, do not fight it. Dig where the shovel goes in easy, even if it is a few feet over. A round hole with straight walls matters more than the exact spot. And keep the sod you cut off the top in one piece, so you can put it back when the season is over.
[STEP2] Step two, the air hole. This is the part nobody shows. Twelve inches away from the fire hole, on the side the wind usually comes from, dig a second, smaller hole, about six inches across and just as deep.
[STEP2] Then join the bottoms of the two holes with a tunnel. Push a broom handle through first, then work it bigger with your hand or a trowel until the short piece of four inch pipe slides in. The pipe keeps the tunnel from caving in.
[STEP2] Why two holes? Hot air rises out of the fire hole and pulls fresh cold air down the air hole and through the tunnel, right into the bottom of the fire. It is a chimney turned upside down, and it costs nothing. That steady draft is what lets the oil burn hot instead of smoking.
[STEP3] Step three, the bucket. Drill a ring of six holes around the bottom of the bucket with the step bit, one inch across. Then cut one bigger hole on the side, three inches across, at the very bottom, lined up with the tunnel. The air comes through the tunnel and straight in.
[STEP3] Set the bucket in the fire hole with that big hole facing the air tunnel. Pack the dirt back in around the outside of the bucket and press it down hard with your boot. The ground around the steel is your insulation. It holds the heat in, and after a while the dirt itself gets warm.
[STEP3] Give the bucket a quarter turn back and forth as you pack the dirt, so it settles flat. Then put a level across the rim. A bucket that leans puts the drops on one side of the sand, and one side of the fire smokes while the other side starves.
[STEP4] Step four, the salt and the sand. Here is where the salt goes. Pour two inches of dry sand in the bottom of the bucket, then mix a heavy handful of coarse salt right into the top of the sand. That is the salt bed.
[STEP4] Now, I am going to be honest with you before we even light it. Salt does not burn. It is not a fuel, and it does not have any hidden energy in it. So what is it going to do? That is exactly what we are going to test.
[STEP5] Step five, the burner. The three inch pipe stands in the middle of the bucket, on three short bolts, so it sits a little above the sand. Mark four rings around it, one inch apart, starting two inches from the bottom. Six holes on each ring with the quarter inch bit. That is twenty four holes.
[STEP5] Those holes are where the air from the tunnel meets the oil vapor rising from the hot sand. That mix is what gives you the blue flame. The top of the pipe pokes up just above the ground, right where your pot sits.
[STEP5] Start each hole with a punch mark so the bit does not walk on the round pipe, and put a drop of oil on the bit. Clamp the pipe in a vise, go slow, and twenty four holes take about ten minutes.
[STEP6] Step six, the pot stand. The scrap of steel grate goes across the top of the hole, resting on the dirt on both sides. The pot sits on it, an inch above the top of the burner pipe. That little gap matters. If the pot sits right on the pipe, it chokes the fire.
[STEP7] Step seven, the drip. Drive the wooden stake into the ground three feet from the hole, on the side away from the air hole. The jug hangs on the stake, two feet off the ground. Copper line from the cap, down through the needle valve, and then along the ground and over the rim of the bucket, so the end stops right over the sand.
[STEP7] Put one loop in the copper right before the bucket. That loop keeps heat from climbing back up the line. And poke one tiny hole in the top of the jug so air can get in and the oil flows steady.
[FIRE] Now the fun part. Drop an oily rag on the sand, a piece of paper on top, and light it. Let it burn three minutes with the valve closed. You will see the air hole start to pull right away. Hold a little smoke near it and watch it get sucked straight down.
[FIRE] Now open the valve a crack. One drop every two seconds. At first the flame is lazy. Then the sand gets hot, the burner pipe gets hot, the tunnel pulls harder, and you hear it. A deep roar coming up out of the ground.
[FIRE] And now look at the color. Where the salt is, the flame is bright yellow orange. That is the salt. Sodium burns with a strong yellow color, the same yellow you see from old street lamps. It looks wild on camera. That is why those videos look so dramatic.
[FIRE] Once it roars, turn it up to one drop a second. Any more than that and you will see dark smoke curling up off the top of the pot. So you turn it back a little. The flame tells you exactly where it wants to be.
[TEST] Now the test. Same pot, same two liters of cold water from the same hose, same drip setting, one drop a second. First with the salt bed. Then I let it cool, dug the salt out, put in clean sand, and ran it again without salt.
[TEST] With the salt, the water came to a full boil in fourteen minutes. Without the salt, thirteen and a half minutes. Basically the same. The salt did not add any heat. It changed the color of the flame, and that is it.
[TEST] But without the salt, the flame inside the burner pipe went back to clean blue. And clean blue means the oil is burning all the way. So my honest answer is this. The salt is a show. The real heat comes from the hole, the tunnel and the pipe.
[TEST] The oil. At one drop a second, the hole burns about half a liter an hour. With a pot on it, that is dinner and a few rounds of coffee from one jug. And when the pot is off, people pull their chairs in around it, because the ground and the steel keep throwing heat for a long time.
[TEST] I left a thermometer in the dirt six inches from the bucket. Before the fire it read forty one degrees. After an hour of burning, the dirt read one hundred and thirty. That warm ring of ground is why a fire hole keeps your feet warm on a cold night.
[TEST] One more thing I noticed. With the salt, a white crust built up on the sand after an hour, and the drops started to bead up on it instead of soaking in. Without salt, the sand stayed dark and even. So the salt is not just useless for heat. Over a long night it actually gets in the way.
[WHY] Why does a hole in the dirt burn so clean? Three reasons. The hot sand turns every drop into vapor, because oil burns as a gas, not as a liquid. The air tunnel feeds fresh air straight into the bottom of the fire. And the burner pipe mixes that air into the vapor before it burns.
[WHY] When you see black smoke, one of those three is missing. The sand is still cold, the tunnel is blocked with dirt, or you are dripping faster than the air can keep up. Fix that one thing and the flame cleans right up.
[OILS] What can you burn? Used motor oil is the best. Old hydraulic and gear oil work if you mix them half and half with motor oil. Old cooking oil from the fryer works too, and it smells better when you are cooking over it.
[OILS] What never goes in the jug is gasoline, thinner, or anything that smells sharp. Those make vapor in the jug itself, and vapor next to a fire is how people get hurt. Heavy oil, slow drip, hot sand.
[MISTAKES] Now the mistakes. Number one, one hole only. Without the air tunnel, the fire starves at the bottom, and a starving oil fire is a smoky oil fire. Dig the second hole.
[MISTAKES] Number two, wet ground. If you dig where water sits, the bottom of the hole steams and the fire never gets hot. Pick a high, dry spot, and line the bottom of the hole with a little gravel if your dirt is heavy clay.
[MISTAKES] Number three, too much oil on cold sand. Open the valve before the sand is hot and the oil pools. When it finally catches, it all goes up at once. Warm it first, then drip slow.
[MISTAKES] Number four, leaving it alone. This is an open fire in the yard. Somebody stays with it the whole time, and when you are done, close the valve, let it burn out, and put the lid of an old trash can over the hole.
[CLEAN] Cleaning is easy. When it is cold, pull the burner pipe, scoop out the old sand with a can, and put fresh sand in. Tap the pipe to clear the holes. Check the tunnel with the broom handle, and you are ready for the next cold night.
