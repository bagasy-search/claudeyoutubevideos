[HOOK] I am sixty-eight, and I cook for one. And last Monday a lady named Dolores put one twenty dollar bill on my counter. She is eighty-one, she lives alone, and she said, Helen, this is all I have for meat this week. Most people would sell her two packs and wish her luck. I bagged up nineteen dollars and ninety-one cents of meat, and I wrote her a little plan on the back of an order slip.
[HOOK_B] Saturday she came back with the slip in her hand. Fourteen suppers and lunches with real meat in them, and she still had a little sausage left over. Today I am going to give you every trick on that slip, and a few more, the same ones I use in my own kitchen, so twenty dollars of meat lasts you a whole week too.
[PROMISE] I am going to give you twenty-five butcher tricks, in the order you use them, from the meat counter on Sunday to the last supper on Saturday. Number eight turns one pound of ground beef into four different suppers. Number sixteen turns one pork steak into two. And number twenty-five is my Saturday supper, and it is the best meal of the week. Some of the people selling little frozen dinners are not going to like this one.
[INTRO] You know me, I am Helen. Forty-one years behind this counter in a little meat market in Iowa. My husband passed nine years ago, and I had to learn to cook for one all over again. Most of my customers are in the same boat. So grab a pencil and the back of an envelope, honey. Let us make twenty dollars stretch.
[STORY] Here is what was in Dolores's bag. One pound of ground beef, four ninety-nine. Two pounds of bone-in chicken thighs, three fifty-eight. One big pork shoulder steak, three ten. One ring of smoked sausage, three ninety-nine. A pound of bacon ends, two fifty. And a bag of soup bones for one seventy-five. Nineteen dollars and ninety-one cents. Not one fancy cut in there. Every one of those is a cut that gives you more than one meal.
[STORY_B] I know what cooking for one feels like. After my husband died, I kept buying the family packs out of habit, and half of it went bad in the back of my refrigerator. I was a butcher throwing away meat. So I sat down at my kitchen table with a pencil and figured out how to buy for one person and cook like a butcher. Everything I am going to tell you came from that table and from forty years of customers telling me what worked.
[T01] Number one. Plan seven suppers before you leave the house. Not a grocery list, a menu. Write the days of the week down the side of a piece of paper, and next to each day, one supper. When you shop with a list, you buy food. When you shop with a menu, you buy meals, and you do not come home with a pack of something that sits in the back of the refrigerator until it turns.
[T02] Number two. Tell the person behind the counter you cook for one. Those are the magic words. A real meat counter will cut you one pork chop, half a pound of stew meat, or a single chicken leg. You do not have to buy the tray of six. I cut single portions all day long, and nobody ever asks. Ask.
[T03] Number three. Ask for the ends. Bacon ends and pieces, the ends of the ham, the end of the bologna, the last bit of a roast. They taste exactly the same as the pretty slices, and they cost about half. At my store, a pound of bacon ends is two fifty. Sliced bacon is six dollars. You are going to chop it anyway.
[T04] Number four. Buy chicken thighs, bone-in, skin on. Not breasts. Thighs cost less than half as much per pound, they stay juicy when you reheat them, and the bones make broth. A two pound pack is usually five or six thighs. For somebody cooking alone, that is four meals of chicken and a pot of soup.
[T05] Number five is the pork shoulder steak. Most people walk right past it. It is cut from the same shoulder you use for pulled pork, it has a little fat running through it, and it is one of the cheapest pieces of pork in the case. One big steak is about a pound and a quarter. It is tender if you cook it right, and I am going to show you how to make two suppers out of it.
[T06] Number six. Buy one ring of smoked sausage. It is already cooked and already seasoned, and it keeps longer than fresh meat. Two ounces of smoked sausage, sliced thin, flavors a whole pot of beans, a skillet of potatoes or a pan of cabbage. It is the best friend of anybody on a budget. One ring is about eight servings when you use it like a seasoning.
[T07] Number seven. Know what a serving is. Three ounces of cooked meat. That is the size of a deck of cards. Most of us were raised on a plate with a big piece of meat in the middle, and that is how the money runs out by Wednesday. A little kitchen scale costs about ten dollars, and it pays for itself in two weeks. Weigh it for a month, and after that your eye will know.
[T08] Number eight. Brown the whole pound of ground beef on Sunday, all at once, with a chopped onion. Drain it and split it in four little containers. Monday it goes into tacos. Tuesday it goes into a jar of spaghetti sauce. Wednesday it goes into a pot of chili with a can of beans. Thursday it goes into a shepherd's pie with mashed potatoes on top. One pound, four suppers, and you only washed the skillet once.
[T08_B] One thing about browning it. Let the skillet get hot first, and do not stir the beef for the first three or four minutes. Let it get a dark brown crust on the bottom before you break it up. Brown is flavor. Gray ground beef tastes like nothing, and then you need more of it to feel like you ate meat. Brown beef, a little salt and that onion, and two ounces tastes like four.
[T09] Number nine. Stretch the meat you do not brown. If you make a meatloaf or meatballs, add half a cup of quick oats and one egg to a pound of beef. Nobody can tell, it holds together better, and it stays juicier. That is how my mother made five meatballs out of what should have been three. Finely chopped mushrooms work the same way.
[T10] Number ten. Roast all the chicken thighs on Sunday at the same time. Salt and pepper, skin side up, four hundred twenty-five degrees, about forty minutes until the thickest one reads one hundred seventy-five degrees. One pan, one time. Eat one hot that night. Then pull the meat off the rest while it is warm, and save every bone. Pulled chicken is ready for four lunches.
[T11] Number eleven. Make broth out of the bones you already paid for. The thigh bones, the skin and the bag of soup bones go in a pot with water, an onion, a carrot and a bay leaf. Barely simmer it for three or four hours. Strain it, and you have about two quarts of real broth. That is the base for Thursday's soup and the rice all week. Free food, out of what most people throw away.
[T12] Number twelve. Cook the bacon ends low and slow in a cold skillet, so the fat melts out. Keep the crispy pieces in a jar in the refrigerator, and pour the fat into a little cup. A spoonful of bacon fat to fry your eggs, your potatoes or your greens makes a meal taste like there is twice as much meat as there is. My grandmother never threw away a drop.
[T13] Number thirteen. Put a piece of tape on every container and write the day you are going to eat it. Not the day you cooked it. Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday. When you open the refrigerator, the food tells you what to eat, and nothing gets lost in the back. Cooked meat is good for three or four days in the refrigerator, so anything for Friday or later goes in the freezer on Sunday.
[T14] Number fourteen. Eat the most perishable meat first. Ground beef and chicken go first in the week. Pork second. Smoked sausage and bacon go last, because the smoke and the salt keep them good much longer. If you plan your menu in that order, nothing spoils, and you never throw away money.
[T15] Number fifteen. Make the meat the seasoning, not the whole plate. One part meat to three parts vegetables, rice or noodles. A stir fry with two ounces of pork and a skillet full of cabbage and carrots feeds you better than a pork chop alone, and it costs about a third. That is how most of the world eats, and they are not hungry.
[T16] Number sixteen is the pork steak. Cut the bone out, then lay your knife flat and slice the steak in half through the middle, like opening a book. Now you have two thin steaks. Put each one in a bag and pound it with the bottom of a skillet until it is a quarter inch thin. Two big tender cutlets from one steak. Fry one Tuesday with a little flour and pepper. The other one goes in the freezer for next week.
[T16_B] And keep the bone you cut out. Do not throw it away. It goes right in the pot with the chicken bones for the broth. And if the steak has a strip of fat on the edge, cut little slits in it every inch before you fry it, so the cutlet stays flat in the pan instead of curling up like a potato chip.
[T17] Number seventeen. Slice cooked meat thin and across the grain. Look for the little lines in the meat and cut against them. Thin slices are more tender, they warm up in a minute, and they spread out across the plate. Two ounces of thin pork over rice looks like a meal. Two ounces in one chunk looks like a snack. Your eyes eat first.
[T18] Number eighteen. Breakfast is where the sausage goes. Two ounces of sliced smoked sausage, one potato cut in little cubes, half an onion, in a skillet with a spoon of that bacon fat. Fry it until the edges are brown and crack an egg on top. That breakfast holds you until supper, and it costs about a dollar.
[T19] Number nineteen. Let the eggs do half the work. One ounce of crumbled bacon in a three egg omelet, or a little pulled chicken in egg fried rice, feels like a full meat supper. Eggs are the cheapest protein in the store, and a dozen of them stretches a pound of meat further than anything else I know.
[T20] Number twenty. Thursday is soup night. Two cups of that bone broth, a cup of pulled chicken, a handful of noodles and whatever vegetables are getting soft. Fifteen minutes. If you make a big pot, freeze two bowls for next week. A bowl of soup with real broth is the cheapest good supper there is.
[T21] Number twenty-one. Friday is fried rice. Cold rice from earlier in the week, a little bacon, the last of the pork or chicken chopped small, a frozen bag of peas and carrots and two eggs. My mother made it every Friday to clean out the refrigerator, and I still do. Three ounces of meat odds and ends feeds you twice.
[T22] Number twenty-two. Freeze single servings in a muffin tin. Fill each cup with chili, pulled chicken in a little broth, or soup, freeze it overnight, then pop them out and put them in one big freezer bag. Each one is one portion for one person. You take out exactly what you need, and nothing gets thawed and thrown away.
[T23] Number twenty-three. Never eat the same supper twice in a row. The same pulled chicken can be chicken salad on Monday, chicken and gravy over toast on Tuesday, and chicken soup on Thursday. Change the sauce, change the dish. When you get bored, you buy takeout, and takeout is how twenty dollars turns into sixty.
[T24] Number twenty-four. Keep a scrap bag in your freezer. Every bone, every onion end, every carrot peel, the rind of the ham, the skin of the chicken. When the bag is full, it all goes in a pot for broth. Next week's soup is already paid for. My scrap bag is older than some of my customers' grandchildren.
[T25] And number twenty-five is my Saturday supper. The clean-out pie. Everything that is left in the refrigerator, the last of the chili, a little sausage, the vegetables, goes in a baking dish with a little broth and a spoon of flour to thicken it. Leftover mashed potatoes or a biscuit dough go on top. Forty minutes in the oven. It is different every week, and it is always the best supper of the week.
[TROUBLE] Now, a few problems I hear about at the counter. If your pulled chicken gets dry when you warm it up, warm it in a spoon of broth with the lid on, never naked in the microwave. If your frozen portions taste like the freezer, you left air in the bag. Squeeze it out, or fill the bag with liquid. And if you get tired of cooking every night, do not. Cook twice a week, Sunday and Wednesday, and just warm things up the other days.
[MONEY] Let me do the arithmetic, because that is what my customers care about. Dolores spent nineteen dollars and ninety-one cents on meat. She got fourteen lunches and suppers with real meat in them, plus two quarts of broth and a jar of bacon fat. That is about one dollar and forty-two cents of meat per meal. Add the potatoes, rice, eggs, noodles and vegetables, about fifteen dollars, and her whole week of meals cost her about thirty-five dollars.
[MONEY_B] Now say she bought little frozen dinners instead, at about three dollars and fifty cents each. Fourteen of them is forty-nine dollars, and they are smaller. That is fourteen dollars a week she keeps, about seven hundred dollars a year. On a fixed income, that is the heating bill in January.
[MONEY_C] And it is not only the money. Dolores told me she feels better. Real meat, real broth, real vegetables, and nothing went in the trash all week. She said it was the first week since her husband passed that she sat down at the table every night instead of eating standing up at the counter. That is worth more than the seven hundred dollars.
[CLOSE] So here is your homework. This week, write seven suppers on a piece of paper before you shop, and tell your butcher you cook for one. Brown your ground beef all at once on Sunday, and put a day of the week on every container. That is all. Then come back and tell me in the comments: what is the one meal you make that stretches the furthest? I read every one of them.
