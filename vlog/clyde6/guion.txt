[HOOK] Never buy breakfast sausage again. Look at this little roll from the store. One pound. Five dollars and forty-nine cents. And now look at this pork shoulder right here. Eight pounds, seventeen dollars. That shoulder is going to make you more sausage than eight of those rolls, and it is going to taste like the sausage your granddaddy ate. And you do not need a grinder, a stuffer, or one single machine. Just a knife, a bowl, and a cold morning.
[HOOK_B] Last fall my neighbor Darlene came by the smokehouse with a roll of the store kind in her hand. She said, Clyde, I fry this every Saturday and it comes out gray, it sits in a puddle of grease, and it falls apart when I flip it. And I said, friend, that is not your fault. That is what happens when somebody grinds it warm, salts it heavy, and sells you mostly water and fat. I chopped her a pound by hand, right here on this block. She has not bought a roll since.
[PROMISE] So here is my promise. By the end of this, you will make breakfast sausage that holds together in the skillet, browns dark on the outside, stays juicy in the middle, and costs you under three dollars a pound. Eight old smokehouse tricks, in the order you do them. Not opinions. What actually happens to the meat. And trick number eight is the one my granddaddy did in nineteen fifty-eight that nobody does anymore. The folks who sell you those little rolls are not going to like this one bit. That is all right.
[INTRO] I am Clyde Pruitt. Fifty-five years of cutting hogs in east Tennessee, most of them right here in this smokehouse. Those are hams hanging up there in the cloth sacks. But hams take a year. Sausage takes one cold morning. And it was the first thing my granddaddy ever trusted me to make by myself.
[STORY] In nineteen fifty-eight I was five years old. Every November, after hog killing, the whole family sat around a long table in this same building. The men cut the shoulders and the trimmings into little pieces with knives. My grandmother mixed in the salt and the sage with her bare hands, in a big white enamel dishpan with a chip on the rim. And my job was to carry the pieces from the table to the pan, and not drop any. I dropped plenty.
[STORY_B] Then she packed that sausage into long cloth sacks she had sewn out of flour sacks, tied them off with string, and Granddaddy hung them right up there from these rafters, next to the hams. All winter long, when we wanted breakfast, somebody climbed up, cut off a round as thick as your thumb, and fried it in the iron skillet. No machine in the whole building. Just knives, cold weather, and patience.
[STORY_C] Granddaddy used to say, keep it cold, keep it coarse, and keep your hands out of the warm. I did not understand a word of that until I was forty. Every trick I am about to give you comes out of that one sentence.
[ONE] Trick number one is the meat you buy, and this is where the money is. Walk up to the meat counter and ask for a whole pork shoulder. Some places call it a Boston butt. It is not from the back end of the hog at all, it is the top of the front leg. Around here it runs about two dollars and nineteen cents a pound, and it is the cheapest good meat in the whole case.
[ONE_B] Now here is the mistake. Folks think lean is healthy, so they buy a pork loin or they trim off all the fat. And then the sausage cooks up dry and hard as a hockey puck. Friend, sausage needs fat. The shoulder already has it, about one part fat to three parts lean, right out of the package. That is exactly the ratio. Do not trim it. Cut out the bone, cut off the thick skin if it has any, and keep every bit of the rest.
[ONE_C] One shoulder of eight pounds gives you about seven pounds of meat once the bone is out. Remember that number. We are going to come back to it.
[TWO] Trick number two is the cold, and this is the one that fixes the grease puddle. Cut the meat into cubes about the size of a walnut. Spread them out on a sheet pan in one layer, and put the whole pan in the freezer for forty-five minutes. Put your mixing bowl in there too. And your knife.
[TWO_B] I know, your mother never froze anything before she made sausage. Here is what actually happens. Pork fat starts to soften at about room temperature. When it is soft, the knife does not cut it, it smears it. And smeared fat does not stay inside the sausage. It melts out in the skillet, and that is your gray puddle. Cold fat stays in little white pieces, and those pieces melt slow inside the patty and keep it juicy.
[TWO_C] You want the cubes stiff on the outside but not frozen through. Press one with your thumb. If your thumb leaves a little dent, it is ready. If it feels like a rock, give it ten minutes on the counter. Keep it cold, that is the first part of what Granddaddy said.
[THREE] Trick number three is the no machine part. You do not need a grinder. You need one big heavy knife, or two if you have them, and a good wooden board. Take a handful of those cold cubes, and chop. Straight up and down, rocking the knife, then turn the pile with the side of the blade and chop again.
[THREE_B] Here is why this beats the grinder. A grinder at the store runs warm and squeezes the meat through little holes until it is a paste. When you chop by hand, every piece stays separate. You want the pieces about the size of a pea, some a little bigger, some a little smaller. That is what gives old country sausage that loose, coarse bite instead of a spongy store patty.
[THREE_C] Chop one handful at a time and put each one right back in the cold bowl. Do not leave a big pile sitting on the board while you work. Seven pounds takes me about thirty minutes. My grandmother did forty pounds a morning, and she sang while she did it. Keep it coarse. That is the second part.
[FOUR] Trick number four is the salt, and almost everybody gets this one wrong one way or the other. The rule in this smokehouse is one and a half teaspoons of coarse kosher salt for every pound of meat. Seven pounds of meat, that is ten and a half teaspoons. Measure it. Do not shake it out of the box.
[FOUR_B] Now listen, if all you have is fine table salt, use only one teaspoon per pound. Table salt is heavier in the spoon. The same spoonful of table salt is almost twice as salty as the coarse kind, and that is why somebody's sausage comes out tasting like the ocean. And salt is not just for flavor. Salt is what makes the meat grab onto itself and hold together. Too little and it crumbles.
[FIVE] Trick number five is the sage, and this is where the store sausage really lets you down. For every pound of meat you want one teaspoon of dried rubbed sage, one teaspoon of black pepper ground fresh, half a teaspoon of red pepper flakes, and half a teaspoon of brown sugar. That is the old Tennessee breakfast blend. Nothing else.
[FIVE_B] But here is the trick. Before the sage goes in the bowl, put it in the palm of your hand and rub your hands together, hard, right over the meat. You will smell it the second you do it. That wakes the sage up. A jar of sage that has been sitting in the cupboard for two years tastes like dust, and rubbing it is the only way to get the flavor back out. If you can smell it from across the room, it is good.
[FIVE_C] And grind your pepper fresh, coarse, right over the bowl. Pepper out of a can has lost half its bite. Mix all your seasoning together in a little cup first, so it spreads even, and then sprinkle it over the chopped meat a little at a time.
[SIX] Trick number six is the one that stops your sausage from falling apart in the skillet. It is called the bind. Pour in two tablespoons of ice water for every pound of meat. Then put your hands in the bowl and work it. Squeeze it, fold it, press it, for about two minutes.
[SIX_B] Here is what actually happens. The salt pulls a little sticky protein out of the meat, and when you work it, that protein turns into glue. You will feel the whole mix change under your hands. It goes from loose crumbs to tacky, like bread dough. Here is the test. Grab a ball the size of an egg, press it into your flat palm, and turn your hand upside down. If it sticks to your hand, you are done. If it falls off, work it one more minute.
[SIX_C] And do it fast. Your hands are warm. Two minutes is plenty, and if the meat starts to feel soft and greasy, put the whole bowl back in the freezer for ten minutes. Keep your hands out of the warm. That is the last part of what Granddaddy said, and now you know why.
[SEVEN] Trick number seven takes one minute and saves you seven pounds of regret. Before you make a single patty, pinch off a piece the size of a quarter, flatten it, and fry it in a small skillet. Then taste it. That is called the test patty, and every sausage maker I ever knew did it.
[SEVEN_B] If it tastes flat, add a little more salt. If it does not bite back, a little more red pepper. If you want it sweeter, the way they make it down in the valley, a little more brown sugar. Mix it in, fry another little one, taste again. Raw sausage always tastes saltier in your head than it does in the pan, so trust the skillet, not the bowl.
[EIGHT] And here is trick number eight, the one from nineteen fifty-eight, the old smokehouse trick. Do not cook it today. Pack the sausage tight into a clean cotton cloth sack, the kind you can buy for a few dollars, or a clean flour sack folded over. Press out every bit of air, tie the top with string, and lay it in your refrigerator overnight. One night. Two is even better.
[EIGHT_B] Here is what actually happens. Overnight, the salt goes all the way through, the sage and the pepper soak into every piece of meat, and the whole thing firms up into one solid log. The cloth pulls off a little moisture from the outside. Sausage you cook right away tastes like seasoned pork. Sausage that rests in the sack tastes like sausage. That is the difference Darlene could not figure out.
[EIGHT_C] The next morning, peel back the cloth, and slice rounds as thick as your thumb, straight off the log, just like my family did off the rafters. No patty press, no shaping, every round the same size, so every round cooks the same. Granddaddy smoked his sacks over hickory, but you do not need a smokehouse for this. Your refrigerator does the important part.
[COOK] Now cooking it. Put the rounds in a cold iron skillet, no oil, and turn the heat to medium low. Cold pan, slow heat. The fat comes out slow and the sausage fries in its own grease. Leave it alone for five minutes. Do not press it down with the spatula. Every time you press, you squeeze the juice right out into the pan.
[COOK_B] Flip it once, when the bottom is dark brown, and give it four or five more minutes. Pork has to be cooked all the way through, so if you have a thermometer, you want one hundred sixty degrees in the middle. If you do not, cut one open. No pink, clear juice, and it is done. Then save that grease in the skillet. That is where your gravy comes from.
[GRAVY] And while that skillet is still hot, here is the breakfast my grandmother made every Sunday. Leave about three tablespoons of sausage grease in the pan. Sprinkle in three tablespoons of flour and stir it for two minutes, until it smells like toast. Then pour in two cups of cold milk, a little at a time, stirring the whole while. Black pepper, a pinch of salt, and let it bubble until it coats the spoon. Crumble one round of sausage back into it. That is sausage gravy, and it costs you about forty cents.
[FIX] Now, if something went wrong, here is what it was. If it crumbled in the pan, it was too lean, or you did not work the bind long enough. If it sat in a puddle of grease, the fat got warm while you were chopping. If it came out gray and tough, the heat was too high, or you pressed it. And if it was too salty, you used table salt with the kosher measure. Every one of those has a fix, and none of them means you cannot do this.
[ASK] Now, folks always ask me the same three questions, so let me save you the trouble. First one. Clyde, can I just buy ground pork from the store and season it? You can, and it will be better than the roll. But it was ground warm and fine, so it will never have that coarse bite, and you do not know how much fat is in it. The shoulder costs less and you know exactly what you have.
[ASK_B] Second one. Can I use my food processor instead of the knife? You can, if the meat is very cold, and only in short little pulses, three or four at a time. Go too long and it turns to paste, and you are right back to the store patty. The knife never makes that mistake. Third one. Do I need casings? No. Casings are for links. Breakfast sausage in this part of the country was always loose, or in a sack, or in a patty. No casings, no stuffer, no machine.
[ASK_C] And one more thing, because somebody always asks. How long does it keep? In the cloth sack in the refrigerator, three days at the most. In butcher paper in the freezer, three months, and it tastes just as good. Write the date on the paper with a pencil. My grandmother wrote it on every single package, and she never once threw out sausage.
[MONEY] Now let's do the arithmetic. This smokehouse does not do villains, it does arithmetic. The store roll is five forty-nine a pound. My shoulder was seventeen dollars and change, and the spices for all seven pounds cost maybe a dollar. That is about two dollars and sixty-five cents a pound for the real thing, against five forty-nine for the roll. Seven pounds of sausage for the price of three rolls.
[MONEY_B] And here is how I would do it. One Saturday morning a month. One shoulder. Thirty minutes of chopping, two minutes of mixing, one night in the sack. Then wrap it in one-pound bricks in butcher paper and put them in the freezer. That is a month of breakfasts for a family of four, and every one of them tastes better than anything in that little roll.
[CLOSE] So here is your homework. Do not do all eight this week. Do two. Buy the shoulder instead of the roll, and freeze the cubes for forty-five minutes before you chop. Just those two, and you will taste the difference on Saturday.
[CLOSE_B] And tell me in the comments, did anybody in your family make sausage at home? Did they pack it in cloth sacks, or make patties? And what county were they from? I read every single one of them. Next time I am going to show you the old way we cured a whole ham in this building, with nothing but salt, sugar, and time, the one that hangs up there for a full year. You are going to want to see that one.
[CLOSE_C] My granddaddy has been gone a long time now. But every time I chop a pan of sausage on a cold morning in this smokehouse, I can hear him. Keep it cold, keep it coarse, and keep your hands out of the warm. Go make some sausage, friend.
