[HOOK] The pie case was the first thing you saw walking in and the last thing you ordered. A tall glass case right by the register, three shelves, and a little light inside, so the meringue on the lemon pie glowed like a cloud. Every night at closing I'd look at what was left in that case and I knew exactly what kind of day we'd had. Twenty-five desserts lived in and around that case for fifty years, and most of them nobody serves anymore. Tonight you're going to get every one.
[HOOK] My granddaughter took me to a nice restaurant downtown for my birthday. When the waiter brought the dessert menu, I ordered the pie. It came out on a slate board. A little square of something, about two bites, a smear of sauce dragged across the board with a spoon, and a leaf on top. Fourteen dollars. I asked the young man where the rest of the pie was. He didn't laugh.
[HOOK] Maybe you remember a slice of pie at a diner counter with your father. The fork going through the crust, the coffee, the waitress calling you honey. And when you try to make that pie at home, the crust is tough, the meringue weeps, the cream filling runs all over the plate. Listen to me. It was never you. The cooks who made those desserts never wrote down the little things. That's what I'm here for.

Number nine is a pie made with no fruit at all, from the hard years, and people still can't believe it tastes like apples. [INTRO] You know me, I'm Lou. Fifty years behind a griddle. My wife made the pies for the first twenty of those years, in the back kitchen at five in the morning, and after she passed, I learned every one of them from her recipe cards. Grab a cup of coffee. Let's go.
[N25] Number twenty-five. Fruit gelatin. Red or green, set in a big square pan with canned fruit cocktail suspended in it, cut into squares and served with a spoon of whipped cream. Ten cents. The trick nobody knows is to let the gelatin set halfway, thick like syrup, before you stir in the fruit. Put the fruit in too early and it all sinks to the bottom.
[N24] Number twenty-four. Rice pudding. Rice cooked slow in whole milk with sugar, a pinch of salt, a few raisins and a stick of cinnamon, stirred on the back of the stove for an hour, then cooled in a deep pan with nutmeg on top. Fifteen cents a bowl. The old men at the counter ate it warm in the winter. The trick is to stir in one beaten egg at the end, off the heat. It turns the pudding silky.
[N23] Number twenty-three. Tapioca pudding. The little pearls, cooked in milk with sugar and egg, with vanilla stirred in at the end, served cold in a glass dish. Fifteen cents. Kids called it fish eyes and ate it anyway. The trick is to beat the egg white separately and fold it in at the end. It makes the tapioca light instead of heavy, like a cloud.
[N22] Number twenty-two. Bread pudding. Day old bread, never fresh, torn in pieces, soaked in milk, eggs, sugar, cinnamon and melted butter, baked in a pan until the top got brown and crunchy. Warm with a little vanilla sauce poured over. Twenty cents. This is how a diner never threw out a loaf of bread. The trick is the day old bread. Fresh bread turns to mush. Stale bread drinks up the custard and holds its shape.
[N21] Number twenty-one. The ice cream soda. A tall glass, two tablespoons of chocolate syrup, a splash of milk, soda water from the fountain, two scoops of vanilla ice cream, and a little more soda on top to make it foam over. Twenty-five cents. The teenagers sat in the booths and made one last all night. The trick is the order. Syrup, milk, soda, then ice cream. Put the ice cream in first and it never foams right.
[N20] Number twenty. Peach cobbler. Sliced peaches, sugar and a little lemon juice in the bottom of a deep pan, and a soft biscuit dough dropped on top in spoonfuls, so it looks like a cobblestone road. Baked until the juice bubbles up between the biscuits. Twenty-five cents with a scoop of ice cream. The trick is a spoon of cornstarch in the peaches. Without it, the cobbler swims. With it, the juice turns thick and shiny.
[N19] Number nineteen. Banana pudding. Layers of vanilla wafers, sliced bananas and a cooked vanilla custard, made from scratch on the stove, with meringue on top browned in the oven. Twenty cents. The southern drivers would ask for it by name. The trick is putting the wafers in while the custard is still warm. They go soft, like cake, and that's the whole point.
[N18] Number eighteen. Chess pie. Just sugar, butter, eggs, a spoon of cornmeal and a splash of vinegar, baked in a pie shell. That's it. Twenty cents a slice. Somebody once asked the cook what kind of pie it was, and she said, it's just pie. Jes' pie, chess pie. That's the story they told me, anyway. The trick is the vinegar. It cuts the sweetness so you can finish the slice.
[N17] Number seventeen. Shoofly pie. A molasses pie the Pennsylvania Dutch made, sticky on the bottom and crumbly on top, with a crumb of flour, brown sugar and butter. Twenty cents. The farmers who came in at dawn ate it with their coffee for breakfast. The trick is hot water mixed into the molasses with a little baking soda. It foams up, and that's what gives you the wet bottom.
[N16] Number sixteen. The banana split. A long glass boat, a banana split down the middle, three scoops, vanilla, chocolate and strawberry, pineapple sauce, chocolate sauce, strawberry sauce, whipped cream, chopped nuts and a cherry on top. Forty-five cents. A couple of kids would share one on a date. The trick is a cold boat. We kept the dishes in the freezer. A warm dish and that split was soup before it reached the table.
[N15] Number fifteen. Strawberry shortcake. Not the sponge cake you buy in the store. A real shortcake is a sweet biscuit, split warm, with sugared strawberries piled on and fresh whipped cream. Thirty cents in June. The trick is to sugar the strawberries an hour ahead and leave them on the counter. They make their own syrup, and that syrup soaks into the biscuit.
[N14] Number fourteen. Coconut cream pie. A vanilla custard with shredded coconut cooked right in, in a baked crust, piled high with whipped cream and toasted coconut on top. Twenty-five cents. The ladies from the church circle ordered it every Wednesday. The trick is toasting half of the coconut in a dry pan until it's golden. Raw coconut tastes like paper. Toasted tastes like a vacation.
[N13] Number thirteen. Pineapple upside-down cake. Butter and brown sugar melted in the bottom of a cast iron skillet, pineapple rings laid in with a cherry in each hole, and a yellow cake batter poured over. Baked, then flipped onto a plate. Twenty cents. The trick is to flip it after five minutes. Not right away, not after it cools. Five minutes, and every ring comes out clean.
[N12] Number twelve. Apple dumplings. A whole apple, peeled and cored, the middle filled with cinnamon sugar and butter, wrapped in pie dough, and baked in a pan of hot syrup. Twenty-five cents with a little cream poured over. People drove out from town for them in October. The trick is the syrup. Pour it hot over the dumplings before they go in the oven, so they bake in it and come out glazed.
[N11] Number eleven. The black and white milkshake. Vanilla ice cream with chocolate syrup, spun on the green mixer until it's so thick the straw stands up by itself, served in the tall glass with the steel cup on the side so you got a shake and a half. Thirty cents. The trick is the steel cup. Freeze it first. And don't put too much milk. A diner shake you eat with a spoon for the first five minutes.
[N10] Number ten. Cherry pie with the lattice top. Sour cherries, sugar, a little almond, and tapioca to thicken it, in a crust woven on top like a basket. Twenty-five cents. That pie was the prettiest thing in the case. The trick is the crust. Ice water, cold butter and lard, and you handle it as little as you can. Warm hands make tough crust.
[N9] Number nine. And here's the one with no fruit. Mock apple pie. During the hard years, apples cost money, but crackers didn't. The cooks broke saltine crackers into a pie shell, poured over a hot syrup of sugar, water, lemon juice and cream of tartar, and a lot of cinnamon, and baked it. Twenty cents. I served it to a man once who swore it was the best apple pie he ever had. I never told him. The trick is the lemon and the cream of tartar. That's where the apple taste comes from.
[N8] Number eight. Boston cream pie. It's a cake, not a pie, don't ask me why. Two layers of sponge cake with vanilla custard in the middle and chocolate glaze poured over the top so it runs down the sides. Thirty cents. The trick is pouring the glaze warm, not hot. Hot glaze runs right off. Warm glaze sits on top and sets shiny.
[N7] Number seven. Pumpkin pie. Not just in November. My wife made it all winter. Pumpkin, eggs, brown sugar, evaporated milk, cinnamon, ginger and a little cloves, in a deep crust. Twenty cents with whipped cream. The trick is to bake it at a high heat for fifteen minutes and then turn the oven down. The crust sets before it gets soggy, and the filling doesn't crack.
[N6] Number six. Butterscotch pie. Brown sugar and butter cooked together in a pan until it's dark and smells like caramel, then milk, egg yolks and a little cornstarch, cooked into a pudding, in a crust with meringue. Twenty-five cents. People don't even know this pie anymore. The trick is cooking the brown sugar and butter longer than you think. That's where the flavor comes from. Take it off early and it's just sweet.
[N5] Number five. Banana cream pie. Vanilla custard, sliced bananas laid in the bottom of a baked crust, more custard, and whipped cream on top. Twenty-five cents. The trick is to brush the banana slices with a little lemon juice. They don't go brown in the case overnight. And slice the bananas right before. Never the night before.
[N4] Number four. Chocolate cream pie. Dark chocolate custard, cooked on the stove until it's thick enough to hold a spoon standing up, poured into a baked crust and chilled overnight, with a tall pile of whipped cream on top. Thirty cents. The waitresses fought over the last slice at the end of the night. The trick is cooking the custard a full minute after it starts to bubble. Take it off too early and it never sets, and you get chocolate soup in a crust.
[N3] Number three. Apple pie à la mode. Tart apples, sliced thick, with sugar, cinnamon, a little nutmeg and butter, in a double crust, served warm with a scoop of vanilla ice cream melting into it. Thirty cents. If you ate at a diner in America, you ate this. The trick is mixing two kinds of apples. One tart that holds its shape, and one sweet that melts down into the juice. One kind of apple gives you a flat pie.
[N2] Number two. Coconut layer cake under the glass dome. Three layers of white cake, a seven minute frosting made of egg whites and sugar beaten over hot water, and a whole coat of coconut pressed into it. Thirty-five cents a slice. It sat right on the counter where everybody could see it, and it was always gone by two in the afternoon. The trick is beating the frosting the full seven minutes, over steam. Stop at five and it slides off the cake.
[N1] And number one. The slice that sold more than everything else in that case put together. Lemon meringue pie. A tart lemon filling, made from fresh lemons, egg yolks, sugar and cornstarch, cooked on the stove, poured hot into a baked crust, with the meringue piled four inches high and browned in the oven. Twenty-five cents. That was the pie that glowed under the little light. I cut twelve slices out of every pie, and some days I baked eight of them. The trick everybody gets wrong is the weeping, those little beads of water on the meringue. Pour the filling in hot, put the meringue on right away while the filling is still hot, and spread it all the way to the crust so it seals. That's it. Fifty years, and that's the whole secret.
[STORY] Now why did all these desserts disappear? It was the refrigerated case. In the seventies the companies came around with pies already made, frozen, in boxes. You just thawed them and put them in the case. No baker at five in the morning, no flour on the floor. A lot of diners took the deal. And slowly, the case filled up with pies that all tasted the same, and people stopped ordering dessert.
[STORY] But every one of these still works in your kitchen. A pie pan, a rolling pin, a pot, a whisk. Most of them cost you two or three dollars for the whole pie today. And when you put one on the table, warm, the whole house will smell like my diner did at five in the morning.
