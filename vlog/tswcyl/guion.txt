[HOOK] This is an old gas cylinder, the kind you swap at the hardware store, with a heavy gray lining of cement poured inside it. And this is what happens when you drip old motor oil into it. A blue ring of fire, roaring, and a stove that stays hot for an hour after you shut it off.
[HOOK] Most people think cement and fire do not mix. Plain cement does crack if you do it wrong. But mix it the right way, pour it into an old cylinder, let it cure, and it becomes a heavy stone heart that soaks up heat and gives it back slowly. That is the whole secret of this stove.
[HOOK] Today I build it in front of you, step by step. How to open the old cylinder safely, the cement mix, the mold, the burner and the drip. Then we light it and I show you the numbers. The temperature in the garage, the oil it burns, and how long it keeps giving heat after the fire is out.
[HOOK] And stay for the part about drying the cement. There is one mistake there that makes the lining pop and crack the first night, and almost everybody makes it.
[INTRO] If you are new here, this is the Scrap Workshop. I build heat out of things people throw away. Old oil, old metal, old parts. It is getting cold out, and the cheapest heat there is, is heat made from something that was headed for the trash.
[INTRO] Old gas cylinders are everywhere. Expired, dented, rusty ones sit behind every farm shed and every scrap yard. Their steel is thick and the shape is perfect for a stove. You just have to open one the right way.
[SAFETY] So the safety word comes first today, and it matters. Only use a cylinder that is completely empty, with the valve already removed, and fill it all the way to the top with water before you cut anything. Water pushes out every last bit of gas. Never cut a closed cylinder. And like every stove, chimney outside, never in a bedroom, a carbon monoxide alarm on the wall and an extinguisher by the door.
[MATERIALS] Here is everything on the bench. One old twenty pound gas cylinder, empty, valve already removed. A bag of plain cement. A bucket of sand. A bag of perlite from the garden store, or a bucket of sifted wood ash. A small plastic bucket for the mold, about seven inches across.
[MATERIALS] Then the steel. A piece of two and a half inch steel pipe, eighteen inches long, for the burner. A short piece of half inch pipe, six inches long, for the oil channel. A plastic jug, a few feet of quarter inch copper line and one small brass needle valve. And a few lengths of three inch stove pipe for the chimney.
[MATERIALS] Tools. An angle grinder, a drill, a step bit, a trowel, a bucket for mixing and a tape measure. No welding on this one either.
[STEP1] Step one, open the cylinder. It is already full of water, standing on its foot ring. Mark a line all the way around, four inches below the top seam, where the dome starts. Cut along that line with the grinder, slowly, with the water still inside. The water keeps the steel cool and keeps everything safe.
[STEP1] Lift the dome off and pour the water out on the grass. Now you have a heavy steel pot about twelve inches across and fourteen inches deep, and a dome with a handle ring on top. Keep the dome. It is going to be the lid.
[STEP2] Step two, the door. On the side of the cylinder, two inches up from the bottom, cut a window three inches wide and two inches tall. This is where you light the stove and where the first air comes in. Keep the piece you cut out, two small bolts make it a sliding cover.
[STEP2] File the edges of the window smooth with a flat file. A sharp cut edge on a stove you touch every day is a cut finger waiting to happen, and a smooth edge lets the little cover slide without catching.
[STEP3] Step three, the cement mix. This is not the mix for a sidewalk. One part cement, two parts sand and one part perlite. The perlite is full of tiny air pockets. They let the cement breathe in the heat, so it does not crack, and they make it hold heat like a brick oven.
[STEP3] Mix it dry first, really well. Then add water a little at a time until it is like thick oatmeal. Not runny. If it pours like soup, it is too wet and it will be weak.
[STEP3] No perlite in your town? Sifted wood ash from last winter works almost as well. Same amount, one part. Shake it through an old window screen so you only keep the fine gray powder, no chunks of charcoal. Chunks burn out later and leave holes in the lining.
[STEP4] Step four, the mold. Set the small plastic bucket upside down in the center of the cylinder. Wrap it with a couple of layers of newspaper so it slides out later. That bucket makes the burn bowl, the hollow in the middle where the fire lives.
[STEP4] Before you pour, push the short half inch pipe through the door hole at an angle, pointing down into where the bowl will be. That is the oil channel. The copper line slides into it later, and the oil drips right into the middle of the bowl.
[STEP4] Now pour the cement all around the bucket, five inches deep, and tamp it down with the trowel so there are no air pockets against the steel. Smooth the top. Leave the door hole clear, that is your air and your lighting hole.
[STEP4] Give the steel a few taps with the handle of the trowel all the way around the outside. You will see little bubbles come up to the top of the cement. That is trapped air leaving. Every bubble that leaves now is a weak spot you will never have to worry about.
[STEP5] Step five, the cure. And here is the mistake I warned you about. Do not light it the next day. Wet cement is full of water, and if you heat it fast, that water turns to steam inside and the lining pops and cracks. Leave it three days, covered with a wet rag, in the shade.
[STEP5] After three days, pull the bucket out. Then dry it slowly. First a candle in the bowl for an evening. Then a small wood fire for an hour. Only after that, the real oil fire. Slow drying is the difference between a lining that lasts years and one that cracks the first night.
[STEP6] Step six, the burner. The two and a half inch pipe stands up in the center of the bowl. Mark four rings around it, one inch apart, starting two inches from the bottom. Eight holes on each ring with the quarter inch bit. That is thirty two holes.
[STEP6] That pipe is where the blue flame lives. The chimney pulls air in through those holes, the air mixes with the oil vapor rising from the bowl, and it burns clean and blue instead of lazy and orange.
[STEP6] Set the pipe in the bowl so it stands on three small stones or three bolts, half an inch off the bottom. That little gap at the foot of the pipe lets the vapor in from all sides. If the pipe sits flat on the cement, the fire only breathes from the top and it burns lazy.
[STEP7] Step seven, the lid and the chimney. The dome goes back on top, upside down is fine or right side up, whichever sits flat. The old valve hole in the top is already right in the middle. Open it up to three inches with the step bit, and the burner pipe pokes right through it. The stove pipe slides over the top of the burner pipe and goes up and out through the wall.
[STEP7] Keep the chimney tall and straight. Eight feet, going up. A tall chimney pulls hard, and a hard pull is what makes the blue flame.
[STEP7] Where the pipe goes through the wall, use a metal wall thimble or a piece of sheet steel with a ring of clearance around it. Keep at least eighteen inches between the pipe and anything made of wood. The pipe near the top of the stove gets hotter than you think.
[STEP8] Step eight, the drip. The oil jug goes on a shelf, a couple of feet higher than the stove and three feet off to the side. Copper line from the cap, down through the needle valve, and into the half inch channel you cast into the cement. Put one loop in the copper before the stove so the heat does not walk back up the line.
[FIRE] Now the fun part. Slide the little door open, drop an oily rag into the bowl, and a piece of paper on top. Light it and let it burn three minutes with the valve closed. The cement is cold and it drinks heat at first, so give it time.
[FIRE] Now open the valve a crack. One drop every two seconds. At first the flame is orange and the cement is cold. It takes ten minutes or so. Then the bowl gets hot, the pipe gets hot, the chimney starts to pull and you hear it. A deep roar. Look in through the door. Blue flame all around the holes.
[FIRE] Once it roars, turn it to one drop a second. More than that and you will see smoke at the top. Turn it back a little. The flame tells you where it wants to be.
[TEST] Now the numbers. A cold morning in a four hundred and fifty square foot garage, an old block building. The thermometer on the wall read thirty nine degrees when I lit it.
[TEST] This stove starts slow, because the cement soaks up heat first. After thirty minutes, the wall read forty nine. After one hour, sixty three. And the steel around that cement was warm all over, not one hot spot, but the whole cylinder.
[TEST] And here is the part I like most. I shut the valve off at the end of the day. One hour later the cylinder was still too hot to hold your hand on, and the garage was still at fifty eight. That cement keeps giving heat back long after the fire is out.
[TEST] The oil. At one drop a second, it burns about two fifths of a liter an hour. Turned down once the room is warm, it holds the heat with even less. A ten liter jug runs it for a full day in the shop.
[TEST] One more number, because a lot of you asked about it last time. The outside of the cylinder, measured with a cheap infrared thermometer, sat at about two hundred and eighty degrees once it was roaring. Hot enough to heat the room, never glowing red. That even heat is the cement doing its job.
[WHY] Why does the cement make such a difference? A thin steel stove gets hot fast and cools fast. It throws heat in bursts. The cement lining is heavy. It drinks the heat, holds it, and lets it out slowly through the steel. So the room stays even, and you do not get that blast of heat right next to the stove and cold everywhere else.
[WHY] And the clean blue flame comes from the same three things as always. A hot bowl that turns every drop into vapor, a tall chimney that pulls, and a burner pipe that mixes air into the vapor.
[WHY] Think of it like a brick oven for pizza. You fire it, the bricks soak it up, and then they cook all evening. This is the same idea in a size that fits in the corner of a garage, made from a bottle somebody left behind a shed.
[OILS] What can you burn? Used motor oil is the best. Old hydraulic and gear oil work if you mix them half and half with motor oil. Old cooking oil from the fryer works too.
[OILS] What never goes in the jug is gasoline, thinner, or anything that smells sharp. Those make vapor in the jug, and vapor next to a fire is how people get hurt. Heavy oil, slow drip, hot bowl.
[MISTAKES] Now the mistakes. Number one, the one I warned you about. Lighting it before the cement is cured and dried. Steam inside the lining cracks it. Three days of curing, then a candle, then a small wood fire, then oil.
[MISTAKES] Number two, plain cement with no perlite or ash. Plain cement is too dense. It holds water and it spalls, little pieces pop off the surface in the heat. The perlite gives it room to breathe.
[MISTAKES] Number three, cutting a cylinder that was not filled with water. I will say it twice because it matters. Empty, valve out, full of water, then cut.
[MISTAKES] Number four, too much oil while the cement is cold. The bowl is slow to warm up, so oil can pool at the start. Start slow, wait for the roar, then turn it up.
[CLEAN] Cleaning is easy. Once a week, when it is cold, lift the dome, pull the burner pipe, and scrape the carbon out of the bowl with a putty knife. The cement takes it well. Tap the pipe to clear the holes and you are ready for another week.
[CLEAN] And check the lining once a month. Small hair cracks on the surface are normal, they open and close with the heat and they do not hurt anything. If a real piece ever comes loose, mix a cup of the same cement, perlite and sand, wet the spot, press it in, and let it cure two days before you fire it again.
[COOK] And a bonus. The dome on top gets hot enough for a kettle. Morning coffee on the stove that heats the garage. And because the cement holds the heat, the kettle stays warm long after you turn the drip down.
[CLOSE] So that is what happens when you pour cement into an old gas cylinder. One empty cylinder, a bag of cement, some sand and perlite, a piece of pipe and the oil from your oil changes. A blue flame stove that heats a garage in an hour and keeps giving heat an hour after it is off.
[CLOSE] Next time you see an old cylinder behind a shed, ask about it. Make sure it is empty and the valve is out, fill it with water, and you have the start of the best stove in your garage.
