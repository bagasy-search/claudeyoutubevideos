[HOOK] When I am gone, dear, my children will throw away nine out of every ten photographs in this house. I know it, because I did it with my mother's photographs in Osaka. Four shoeboxes, two albums with broken spines, and a paper bag of loose pictures under her bed. We did not know half of the faces. So at eighty, I follow eleven Japanese rules for my photos and keepsakes, and my children will not have to guess like we did.
[HOOK_B] Maybe you have a closet shelf full of albums you never open. Maybe a box of pictures in the garage, getting damp. Maybe your son said, Mom, what are we supposed to do with all of these, and you did not have an answer. It is not your fault. Nobody teaches us how to keep memories light. We only learn how to keep more of them.
[PROMISE] So here is what I will show you. Eleven rules, the order to do them in, and one small notebook on the shelf that will save the pictures that really matter. The first rule takes one evening. And rule number eleven is the one I wish my mother had told me while she was alive.
[STORY] You know me. I am Sachiko, and I have kept this little house for fifty years. In Japan, this work has a name, seizen seiri, putting your things in order while you are still alive. When my mother passed, my sister and I sat on the tatami floor of her house with four shoeboxes. There was a picture of a young woman in a kimono, standing by a river, smiling. My sister said it was our grandmother. I said it was our great aunt. We argued for an hour. Nobody was left to ask. That picture is on my wall now, and I still do not know her name. That is why I made these rules.
[ONE] Rule number one is to bring every picture to one table. Not one box at a time. Every album, every envelope, every shoebox, every frame from every room, all onto the dining table on one evening. In Japan we say, zenbu dasu, take everything out. Most people sort a little box here and a little box there, and they never finish, because they never see the whole mountain. When everything is on one table, you see how much there is, and you stop being afraid of it. I made a pot of tea, and I just looked at it. That evening I did not sort anything. I only looked, and I laughed at my hairstyles from nineteen seventy-four. My husband came in, picked up a picture of himself with long sideburns and a brown suit, and put it face down very quickly. We laughed until the tea went cold. That first evening is not work, dear. It is a visit with everybody you ever loved, all at the same table.
[ONE_B] Do this on a quiet evening, with the radio on and nobody in a hurry. Leave the table covered for one week if you can. Every time you walk past, you will pick up a picture and remember something, and that is part of the work too.
[CTA1] Quick, while I have you, dear. If this is your first time here, press subscribe. Every week I bring you one more Japanese way to keep a house calm and light. And all eleven rules for the photographs are written out in the description, so you can keep them next to your albums.
[TWO] Rule number two is the faces question. Hold each picture and ask yourself, do I know a face in this picture, and do I love it? Not, is it a nice picture. Many pictures are nice. A sunset, a mountain, a beach from a holiday in nineteen eighty-two. Those you can let go, because the mountain is still there, and anybody can photograph it. Keep the faces. My husband took two hundred pictures of the Grand Canyon on our trip. Two hundred. I kept three, the three where I am standing in front of it, squinting at him. The other ones went into the recycling bin, and I did not feel one bit sad about it. A picture without a face, dear, is a postcard, and nobody cries when a postcard goes.
[TWO_B] The same goes for pictures of food at a restaurant, of hotel rooms, of a garden somebody else planted. My mother had a whole envelope of cherry trees from a temple in Nara, and not one person in any of them. We kept one, for the blossoms, and the rest went with a thank you.
[THREE] Rule number three is to keep only the best of the same moment. In the old days, film was expensive, so we took one picture. Later, we took five of the same birthday cake, the same Christmas tree, the same baby on the same blanket. Put the five next to each other. Choose the one where the eyes are open and everybody is smiling. Keep that one. The other four can go. I had eleven pictures of my daughter blowing out the candles on her seventh birthday, eleven of them. I kept the one where she has frosting on her nose, and I sent it to her in the mail with a little note. She called me that evening laughing, and now it is on her refrigerator. Choosing one is a small sadness for one second, and then it feels like spring cleaning. The best picture shines brighter when it is not hiding behind its four sisters.
[FOUR] Rule number four is to write on the back, today. This is the most important rule of all, so I put it early. Turn every picture you keep over, and with a soft pencil, write three things. Who is in it, where it was, and the year. Not a pen, because a pen can bleed through and ruin the face. A soft pencil, very light. My mother never wrote on anything. My grandmother wrote on everything, in tiny letters, in the corner. And so, a hundred years later, I know the names of my grandmother's sisters and the name of the ferry they took to the island in summer, but I do not know the girl by the river. Write on the back, dear. It takes ten seconds a picture, and it is the difference between a family and a stranger.
[FOUR_B] Do twenty pictures every evening with your tea. In one month, the whole box is written. If you do not remember the year, write about. About nineteen sixty. That is still better than nothing. And if you do not remember the name, call your sister, or your old friend. Those are the best telephone calls of the whole year. My cousin in Kobe remembered every name in a picture from nineteen fifty-two, the whole school class, standing in three rows. We talked for two hours, and I wrote thirty-one names on the back of one picture.
[FIVE] Rule number five is one album for each child. Not one big family album that nobody can share. One thin album for each child, with the pictures of that child, from the first day to now. When my children were small, every picture went into a big brown album, all mixed. Now I am taking them out and making three small albums, one for my daughter, one for my son, and one for my youngest. Each one gets the pictures where they are the center. Their first steps, their school uniforms, their weddings, their own babies. When the time comes, nobody has to divide anything. Each child takes their own album home, and there is nothing to argue about. Inside the cover of each album I wrote a short letter in pencil, just a few lines, about the day that child was born and the weather outside the hospital window.
[SIX] Rule number six is to give the pictures away now. In Japan we say, it is better to give with warm hands than with cold ones. It means give while you are still alive, and see the happy face. Last summer my granddaughter visited, and I gave her twenty pictures of her mother as a little girl, in a small envelope. She sat on my sofa and went through them, one by one, and she laughed at her mother's haircut. Then she went quiet and said, Grandma, she looks just like me. She took a picture of each one with her phone and sent them to her mother right there on my sofa. Those twenty pictures are not in my box anymore. They are in her heart, and that is a much better place for them.
[SIX_B] Make a little pile for each person. The neighbor who was at your wedding. The old friend from the church choir. The nephew who never saw his grandfather young. Put them in an envelope with a short note and give them this month. Do not wait for a funeral. People should hear the stories while you are still here to tell them.
[CTA2] Let me stop for one second. If these rules are helping you, press subscribe and the little bell, because next week I show you my rules for the papers in the house, the drawer that every family is afraid to open. And remember, every rule is in the description, in order.
[SEVEN] Rule number seven is one box for the keepsakes. Not only pictures. A tiny knitted sock, a crayon drawing of a house with a purple sun, a report card with a gold star, the ribbon from the hospital bracelet. Those small things are the hardest of all, because they are so small, and they hide in every drawer of the house. Find them all, and put them in one box, a good wooden box or a nice strong paper box, the size of a shoebox or a little bigger. When the box is full, it is full, and something has to leave before something new can go in. My box has a lid with a crane painted on it. My mother kept her own keepsakes in a tin that used to hold rice crackers, and I still have that tin, with three things inside. Her wedding hairpin, a lock of my baby hair, and a ticket for the train to Kyoto from nineteen thirty-six.
[EIGHT] Rule number eight is the note on every keepsake. The pictures get writing on the back. The keepsakes get a little card. Every object in the keepsake box gets a small card, tied to it with string, with one sentence. Who it belonged to, and why it matters. A baby shoe without a card is an old shoe, and it goes in the bag. A baby shoe with a card that says, this is the shoe your father wore when he took his first step, in the kitchen in Ohio, in April nineteen seventy-five, that shoe stays in the family for another hundred years. I write my cards in pencil too, in big letters, so old eyes and young eyes can read them.
[NINE] Rule number nine is to let the copies go. Most of us keep the same picture three times. The original, the copy we ordered for grandma, the copy that came back from the store in double prints. And the school pictures, the same face twelve times on one big sheet. Keep one of each. Give one away if somebody wants it. The rest can go. I found nine copies of my son's school picture from the year he was ten, still in the envelope from the photographer. I kept one for his album, I gave one to each of his children, and I let the rest go. His children put their father's picture next to their own school picture on the refrigerator, and they look like brothers.
[TEN] Rule number ten is to keep the albums light and on a low shelf. Albums are heavy, and when they sit on the top shelf of a closet, nobody takes them down. My mother kept hers in the closet under the futons, and we found them only after she was gone. I keep mine now on the lowest shelf of the bookcase in the living room, where my grandchildren can sit on the floor and open them. Thin albums, not thick ones. Ten or twelve pages each. A thin album gets opened. A thick album gets carried to the curb.
[TEN_B] And keep them away from the basement and the attic. Damp ruins the pictures, and heat makes them curl. A dry shelf in the room where you live is the best place. Once a year, at New Year, I open every album and turn every page, so the pages do not stick together. My grandson helps me, and he asks a hundred questions, most of them about his grandfather's terrible hats. That is the best day of my whole year.
[ELEVEN] Rule number eleven is the little notebook of faces. This is the one I wish my mother had told me. I have a small notebook, the kind children use at school, with a blue cover. In it, I pasted one picture of every important face in our family, the grandparents, the aunts, the cousins, the old friends. Under each picture, I wrote the name, the year they were born, how they are related to us, and one small story about them. My great aunt Fumiko, who sold sweets at the station and slipped an extra one to any child who looked sad. My grandfather, who walked to work in the snow with no socks so his sons could have them. Forty faces, forty stories, in one thin blue notebook.
[ELEVEN_B] That notebook is the key to every box in this house. When my children open the shoeboxes one day, they will hold up a picture and look in the blue notebook, and they will find the face, and they will know. They will not argue for an hour like my sister and I did. Make yours with one picture a day, and if a face has no story yet, call somebody who remembers. In two months it is finished. And tell one person where the notebook is, so it is not a secret on the day it matters most.
[RECAP] Let me say them all again, so you can keep them. One, bring every picture to one table. Two, the faces question. Three, the best of the same moment. Four, write on the back today, in soft pencil. Five, one album for each child. Six, give the pictures away now. Seven, one box for the keepsakes. Eight, a note on every keepsake. Nine, let the copies go. Ten, light albums on a low shelf. Eleven, the little notebook of faces.
[HONEST] I want to be honest with you. You will not do all eleven this month, and that is fine. Start with one table tomorrow evening. And when you hold a picture that makes your throat close, sit down, have some tea, write the name on the back, and put it with the ones you keep. That is not giving up. That is deciding.
