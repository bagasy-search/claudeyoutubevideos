[HOOK] When I am gone, dear, my children will throw away nine out of every ten things in my closet. I know it, because I did it to my mother's closet in Osaka. One afternoon, my sister and me, and eleven bags at the curb. Most of those clothes she had not worn in thirty years. So at eighty, I follow eleven Japanese rules for my closet, and my children will not have to do what we did.
[HOOK_B] Maybe you have a closet you open only a little, so nothing falls on your head. Maybe your daughter looked inside last Christmas and said nothing, and you saw her face. It is not your fault. Nobody teaches us how to keep a closet light. We only learn how to fill it.
[PROMISE] So here is what I will show you. Eleven rules, the order to do them in, and one small card on the inside of the closet door that will save the clothes that really matter. The first rule takes ten minutes. And rule number eleven is the one I wish my mother had told me while she was alive.
[STORY] You know me. I am Sachiko, and I have kept this little house for fifty years. In Japan, this work has a name, seizen seiri, putting your things in order while you are still alive. My mother's closet had sliding paper doors, and inside, three kimono from her wedding, wrapped in thin paper, the camphor smell still in them. We did not know which one was the good one, or which one had belonged to her own mother, my grandmother, who sewed it by hand in nineteen twelve. Nobody had told us. My sister held one up to the window and cried, and I still do not know if she was holding the right one. That is why I made these rules.
[ONE] Rule number one is to empty one shelf to the wood. Not the whole closet. One shelf. Take everything off and put it on the bed, and look at that empty shelf for one minute. In Japan we say kara ni suru, make it empty. Most people tidy by pushing things to the side. That never works. When the shelf is empty, you see how much room you really have, and every piece that goes back has to ask permission. My first shelf took ten minutes, and four sweaters never went back on it. I put them in the give bag by the front door, and that same afternoon they went to the church clothing table. Two weeks later I saw a young mother at the bus stop wearing my blue one, and she looked better in it than I ever did. That is how you know a shelf rule works, dear. You feel lighter, and somebody else feels warmer.
[ONE_B] Do one shelf a day, in the morning, when you are fresh, with the window open and a cup of tea on the dresser. Never at night. At night everything looks precious, and you put it all back. My mother tidied only before nine in the morning, and she said the morning light tells you the truth about a sweater.
[CTA1] Quick, while I have you, dear. If this is your first time here, press subscribe. Every week I bring you one more Japanese way to keep a house calm and light. And all eleven closet rules are written out in the description, so you can keep them by your closet door.
[TWO] Rule number two is the shop question. Hold each piece and ask yourself, if I saw this in a shop today, at full price, would I buy it again? Not, is it still good. Everything is still good. Not, did it cost a lot. That money is already gone. Only, would I buy it today. My mother had a grey wool coat with big shoulders from nineteen sixty-four. Beautiful wool. She would never have bought it again, and she never wore it again. Thirty years it hung there, keeping the moths happy. If she had asked herself the shop question even once, that coat could have kept a young student warm through thirty winters in Osaka. So be honest with the shop question. If your answer starts with maybe, the answer is no. Maybe means you are being polite to a coat, and coats do not need your manners, dear.
[THREE] Rule number three is to keep clothes for the woman you are now. Not the size you were at fifty, not the size you hope to be after the diet. I had a box of skirts for when I lose ten pounds. I carried that box through two houses. One Saturday I opened it and laughed, because the skirts were not even in fashion anymore. In Japan we say, ima o ikiru, live in the now. The woman you are now deserves a closet that fits her today. When you open the doors, everything inside should fit, feel soft, and make you stand up straight. If a skirt makes you feel bad about yourself every morning, it is not a skirt anymore. It is a little judge hanging in your closet, and you do not need a judge at eighty. Give the skirts to a young woman who will wear them now, while they still have some life in them.
[FOUR] Rule number four is to fold standing up. This is the old Japanese way my mother used for her drawers. You fold each shirt into a small flat rectangle, then you stand it on its edge, like files in a box, not lying in a pile. When you open the drawer, you see every single piece at once, from above. Nothing hides at the bottom of the pile for ten years. And you know right away when a drawer is full, because nothing more will stand up in it. When the drawer is full, you stop buying. That is the whole secret. My mother kept her summer cottons in the top drawer and her winter wool in the bottom one, and she could tell you what was in each drawer with her eyes closed.
[FOUR_B] Folding a whole dresser this way takes one afternoon to fold a whole dresser this way. My grandson laughed at me, and then he did his own drawers the same way, because he could finally find his socks. Try it with one drawer this week, the T-shirt drawer, and you will see how much room was hiding in there.
[FIVE] Rule number five is the hanger rule. Count your hangers, choose a number, and never buy one more. I have forty. When something new comes into my closet, one old piece leaves the same day, and its hanger is waiting for the new one. One in, one out. Most of us have a pile of empty wire hangers on the floor of the closet, and every empty hanger is an invitation to buy another blouse. Take the wire ones back to the cleaner. Keep the good wooden ones. And stop at your number. Wooden hangers are kinder to your clothes, and when they are all the same, the closet looks calm, like a little shop. My mother used bamboo hangers her father made, and she had exactly thirty of them for her whole life. When one broke, she fixed it with string.
[SIX] Rule number six is one outfit for sad days and one for happy days. Every woman over seventy has a whole rail of black clothes for funerals and three dresses for weddings that never happen anymore. In Japan we have mofuku, the black clothes for mourning, and every family keeps one good set, not five. Keep one dark suit or dress that fits you well for the sad days, and one nice outfit for the happy days, a wedding, a birthday, a baptism. Hang them together at the end of the rail, inside a cloth cover, with the right shoes underneath, so on a hard morning you do not have to think. Check them once a year, in spring, and make sure they still fit, because our bodies change a little every year and nobody wants a surprise on a sad morning.
[SIX_B] When my husband passed, I did not have to think about what to wear. It was ready, cleaned and pressed, and I was so thankful to my younger self for that. My daughter came to help me that morning and did not have to search for anything. Later she told me something I will never forget. She said, you even took care of us that day. That is what a ready closet does.

[SEVEN] Rule number seven is one coat for each hook. Look at the hooks by your front door. Count them. That is how many coats you need. In the genkan of my mother's house, the little entry where we leave our shoes, there were three hooks. A coat for rain, a coat for cold, and a light jacket for spring. When a coat had no hook, it had to go. I have four hooks now, and the hall closet has room for my grandson's jacket when he visits. Coats are the heaviest thing your children will carry out, dear, and the hardest to give away, because nobody needs five old winter coats. A good coat you do not wear can keep a family warm at the shelter this winter. Take it in October, before the cold comes. And check the pockets first. I found a ticket from a concert in nineteen eighty-eight in mine, and I laughed for an hour.
[EIGHT] Rule number eight is the one basket for scarves and bags. Handbags multiply in the dark. So do scarves and belts. Find one basket, a nice one, and everything of that kind lives inside it. When the basket is full, nothing new comes in until something goes out. I counted them one day on the bed, and I had twenty-two. I kept six handbags out of twenty-two, and I still use only two of them. The other sixteen went to a thrift shop, and the lady there was very happy, because she said good leather bags never stay on her shelf for long. My mother kept her scarves in one wooden box, folded like little envelopes, and she had exactly twelve, one for every month. In January the dark blue one, in April the pink one with the cherry flowers, in August a thin white cotton one for the heat. She did not need more, because every month already had its scarf.
[NINE] Rule number nine is to give with warm hands. In Japan we say, it is better to give with warm hands than with cold ones. It means give while you are still alive, and see the happy face. My granddaughter loves my red cardigan from Osaka. Why should she wait for my funeral to wear it? I wrapped it in tissue paper and gave it to her last spring, and she wore it to her school concert and sent me a picture. She looked so pretty in it, and I could see my mother's smile in her face. That picture is on my refrigerator now, and the cardigan is having a second life. Make a little list: this coat for my niece, this sweater for my neighbor. Then give it this month, with a cup of tea. Do not wait for a birthday. Things given for no reason are the ones people remember. And if nobody in the family wants something, that is good to know now, while you can still find it a happy home yourself.
[TEN] Rule number ten is one box for the clothes that hold a memory. You will find clothes you cannot give away. My husband's brown sweater with the hole in the elbow. My mother's apron. My son's first little kimono. Those you keep, but only in one box, the size of a shoebox or a little bigger. Fold each one, and pin a small paper note to it with one sentence, who wore it and why it matters.
[TEN_B] The memory box has a limit. When the box is full, it is full. Mine has seven things in it now, and I check it every New Year, and sometimes one thing leaves, because the memory is in my heart and the sweater can go. A note that says, my husband wore this every Sunday for thirty years, will make your daughter keep it. Without the note, it is just an old sweater with a hole, and it goes in the bag with everything else. I write my notes on little cards with a pencil, because ink can run if the box ever gets damp in the basement.
[ELEVEN] Rule number eleven is the card on the closet door. This is the one I wish my mother had told me. On the inside of my closet door, there is a white card with tape on the corners. On it, in big letters, I wrote the five things in this closet that are worth something, in money or in love. The pearl necklace in the blue box, top shelf. My mother's wedding kimono in the paper, bottom drawer. The fur collar from my aunt. And next to each one, the name of the person who should have it. The pearls for my daughter. The kimono for my granddaughter, who wants to wear it at her wedding. The fur collar for my niece, who always touches it when she visits. Everything else in that closet can go to the church table, and the card says that too, so nobody feels guilty.
[ELEVEN_B] That card is ten minutes of work, and it saves the one good kimono from the bag at the curb. When my children open that door one day, they will not have to guess like we did. They will read the card, and they will hear my voice. Write yours with a big marker, so old eyes and young eyes can both read it. Change it when you change your mind. And tell one person where the card is, so it is not a secret on the day it matters most.
