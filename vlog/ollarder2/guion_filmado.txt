No truck, no store, no electricity in that cook shack, not even a wire. Forty men to feed every day until the spring breakup. That's five months.
[P1|] So whatever those men were going to eat in February, we had to put away in October. Every potato, every onion, every carrot, every apple. And if we did it wrong, we'd find out in January, with a cellar full of rot and forty men looking at an empty pot.

[P6|] First, the rules. I always start here, because cold storage, drying and fermenting are safe when they're done right, and these are the ways people get hurt. None of them are worth it.
[P7|] One. Never keep garlic, herbs or vegetables in oil at room temperature. I know it looks pretty on the counter. But oil shuts out the air, and that is exactly what botulism wants. Garlic in oil goes in the refrigerator, and you use it within four days, or you don't make it at all.
[P8|] Two. Never can anything without a tested recipe. Vegetables, meat, beans and soup have to be pressure-canned from a tested recipe. Water-bath canning them, or that so-called oven canning, can grow a poison you can't see, can't smell and can't taste. My book doesn't teach canning at all. For canning, you go to the tested recipes from the USDA.
[P9|] Three. Don't cure your own meat by guesswork. Salt pork and ham that keep without a refrigerator need exact amounts of curing salt and a tested method. In camp, we bought it cured. You should too.
[P10|] Four. Pickled eggs and cooked food stay in the refrigerator. Always. Pickled eggs left out at room temperature have made people very sick. And a cold porch doesn't count as a refrigerator for cooked food, because it warms up and cools down with the weather.
[P11|] And five. If it looks wrong, it goes out. Fuzzy mold, pink or black slime, a rotten smell, a bulging lid, cloudy foamy liquid where it ought to be clear. Out it goes, and you don't taste it first.
[P12|] And the one tool you need is a min-max thermometer. About twelve dollars. It tells you the coldest and the warmest your storage spot got since you last looked. Emil had one nailed inside the cellar door, and he read it every morning before he started the coffee.
[P13|] Now, the cellar. You don't need a hole in a hillside. A cellar is any spot that stays between freezing and about forty-five degrees, dark, with a little moisture in the air. Here's three ways to get one.
[P14|] The first is the basement corner. Pick the coldest corner of an unheated basement, the north or the east side, with two outside walls, away from the furnace. And here's the part people get backwards. Don't insulate those two outside walls. The cold earth on the other side is what cools the room. You build the two inside walls with studs and foam board, hang an insulated door, and run two vent pipes. One low, to let the cold air in, and one high, to let the warm air out. Wooden slat shelves and crates, never right up against the concrete. That runs sixty to a hundred and fifty dollars, and it works every winter for the rest of your life. That's page eight.
[P15|] The second is for folks with a yard and no basement. The buried barrel. A clean metal trash can, or a food-grade barrel, sunk in a spot that drains well, with the rim two to four inches above the ground and a little gravel in the bottom. You layer your vegetables in straw. Carrots, straw, beets, straw. Lid on, then a foot or two of straw over the top, then a tarp held down with bricks. That keeps roots crisp until spring for thirty or forty dollars. Page nine.
[P16|] And the third one costs next to nothing. No basement, no yard? An old chest cooler in an unheated garage, up off the concrete on a pallet. Crates of potatoes and roots inside, onions in their own bin. A thermometer inside the box, and you check it every few days. Here's the trick. In a hard cold snap, put a gallon jug of warm water in there overnight. It gives off its heat slow and keeps the box from freezing. Page ten.
[P17|] Now, what goes in. And the whole secret of a winter larder is this. Every food wants something different. Some want it cold and wet, some want it cold and dry, and some want it warm. Put each one where it's happy and it keeps. Put them all in one place and half of them rot.
[P18|] Potatoes. They keep longer than anything except dry grain, if you treat them right. Don't wash them. Just brush off the loose dirt. Then you cure them, one or two weeks in the dark at fifty to sixty degrees, with some air moving. The little cuts heal over and the skin gets tough. Sort out any cut or bruised or green ones and eat those first. Store only the perfect ones, in crates no more than two or three deep, covered against the light, at thirty-eight to forty-five degrees. Colder than thirty-eight and they turn sweet. Warmer than fifty, and they sprout. And every two or three weeks, go through and pull out any soft ones. Five to eight months, that's how long they'll last. Page eleven.
[P19|] Onions and garlic want the opposite of potatoes. Cold, but dry. In a damp cellar, they rot from the neck. So you cure them two to four weeks somewhere warm and shaded, until the necks are dry and the outer skins rustle when you touch them. Then hang them where the air moves. Emil kept ours in an old pair of his wife's stockings, hung from a nail by the back door. Drop in an onion, tie a knot, drop in another one, tie a knot. When you need one, you snip it off below a knot. Page twelve.
[P20|] Carrots, beets and parsnips. Damp sand. Twist the greens off and leave half an inch of stem, because the greens pull water right out of the root. Don't wash them. Wet some clean play sand until it barely holds together when you squeeze it, like a wrung-out sponge. Two inches of sand in a box, a layer of roots not touching, sand to cover, and again. Keep that box in the coldest, dampest part of the cellar. At thirty-two to forty degrees they stay crisp four to six months. And if the sand dries out over the winter, you mist it a little. Page thirteen.
[P21|] Cabbage. The old way kept cabbage until March. You pull the whole plant, roots and all, on a dry day, strip off the loose outer leaves, tie a string around the root and hang it upside down from a beam. Away from everything else, though, because cabbage smells strong, and the whole cellar will taste like it. When you want one, peel off the dried outer leaves, and the inside is fresh as the day you hung it. And a store-bought cabbage wrapped in newspaper at the back of your coldest shelf keeps for weeks, too. Page fourteen.
[P22|] Now apples. And here's where most people go wrong. Apples have to have their own room. A ripening apple gives off a gas that makes everything near it ripen faster. Potatoes sprout, carrots go bitter. That's where the old saying comes from, one bad apple spoils the barrel. It's true. So you take late keeping apples, Granny Smith, Fuji, Northern Spy, and you handle them like eggs. Wrap every single one in a square of newspaper, lay them one or two layers deep in shallow boxes, and keep them on the porch or out at the far end of the cellar, well away from the vegetables. Wrapped like that, one apple can't spoil the next. Two to six months. Page fifteen.
[P23|] Then there's the things that hate the cold cellar. Winter squash and pumpkins want a cool, dry bedroom, on a shelf, not touching. Cut the stem, never pull it, and leave two inches, or it rots from the top. And butternut gets sweeter after a month or two. Page seventeen. And sweet potatoes, those want it warm. Below fifty degrees they rot from the inside. A closet on an inside wall, wrapped in newspaper. Never in the cellar, and never in the refrigerator. Page eighteen.
[P24|] And the easiest cellar of all is the garden itself. Carrots, parsnips, leeks and turnips can stay right where they grew. After the first hard frosts, before the ground freezes, cut the tops to an inch and pile twelve to eighteen inches of straw over the rows, with a tarp on top and stakes to mark where they are. When you want some, you pull the straw back on a mild day and dig. And parsnips that sit through the frost get so sweet, the old farmers wouldn't dig them until after Christmas. Page nineteen.
[P25|] Now, a word about the cold porch, because everybody up north thinks of it. In deep winter a closed porch can hold raw vegetables, sealed drinks, butter in its wrapper and jars of kraut. But it is not a refrigerator. You put the thermometer right where the food sits and read it every morning for a week before you trust it. Between thirty-three and forty, you're fine. And on a sunny day, the sun through those windows can warm it up past forty before noon. So no cooked food, no meat, no leftovers out there. Page twenty-one.
[P26|] Next, the stove. Take the water out of food and nothing can grow in it. In camp we strung things up over the cookstove all fall.
[P27|] Apple rings on a string. Peel and core the apples, slice them a quarter inch thick, and thread them on a clean cotton string, not touching. Hang them above the stove, but not right over the cooktop where the steam comes up. They're done when they're leathery and no wet shows when you tear one. And here's the trick. Before you seal the jar, put them in it loose for a week and shake it every day. If drops form, they need more drying. That's called conditioning, and it's what keeps them from molding. Page twenty-four.
[P28|] Leather britches. That's what the hill people call dried string beans. You thread whole green beans on a heavy thread with a big needle and hang them up two or three weeks until they're brittle. In the winter you soak them overnight and simmer them three or four hours with a ham hock. They taste nothing like fresh beans. Deep, almost smoky. Page twenty-seven.
[P29|] And jerky, and this is another safety one. The step most old recipes skip is the one that makes it safe. Lean beef, a quarter inch thick, marinated overnight in the refrigerator. Then, before you dry it, you bring the strips up to a hundred and sixty degrees, either simmered in the marinade or in a low oven. That kills the germs that the low drying heat won't. Then you dry it at about a hundred and fifty, a hundred and sixty, until a strip bends and cracks but doesn't snap. Page twenty-nine.
[P30|] And then the crocks. Salt and time turn cabbage sour, and sour keeps. Twenty-five pounds of cabbage and about a cup of salt makes enough sauerkraut for a family all winter. Shred it thin, toss it five pounds at a time with three tablespoons of pickling salt and squeeze it until the juice runs, and pack it hard into a five-gallon crock. It has to be under at least an inch of its own juice. A plate on top, a jug of water on the plate for a weight, and a cloth over it. Three or four weeks in a warm room, and then it keeps four to six months below forty degrees. And if you see a white film on top of the brine, that's harmless yeast, you skim it off. But fuzzy colored mold, or kraut that turns pink, the whole batch goes. Page thirty-three.
[P31|] And pickled eggs. Those are good, cheap food. Boil a dozen eggs, peel them, pack them in a clean jar and pour a hot vinegar brine over them. But I'll say it again because it matters. Pickled eggs live in the refrigerator. Not on the counter, not on the porch, not for one single day. Page thirty-nine.
[P32|] The dry shelf, now. Flour weevils hatch from eggs that are already in the bag when you buy it. Cold kills them. When it's below zero outside for a few days, put the sealed bags in a closed metal box outdoors for four days. Otherwise, the freezer for four days. Then let them come back to room temperature still sealed, so no water forms inside, and pour them into tins with tight lids. Page forty-one.
[P33|] A big block of cheddar, dipped in cheese wax, keeps for months in a cold cellar. Cheese wax, now, not candle paraffin. Paraffin cracks in the cold and lets the mold in. Page forty-four. And lard. Strain it warm through cheesecloth so there's no bits of meat left, because those spoil first, and pour it into small jars filled right to the top. Small jars are better than one big one, because every time you open a jar, the air gets in and the clock starts ticking. Six months to a year below fifty degrees. Page forty-five.
[P34|] So now, the part I promised you. The order. Because a full larder is only worth what you cook out of it.
[P35|] Emil had a rule. You eat in the order things spoil. First the things that keep worst. The acorn squash, the bruised apples, the thick-necked onions, the last of the fresh greens. Then the cabbage and the celery. And last of all, the potatoes, the carrots in sand, the big Hubbard squash, and the dried goods. Do it backwards, and you'll be eating dried apples in November and throwing out soft squash in January.
[P36|] And once a week, a pot of roots. Carrots, rutabaga, potatoes and onions, simmered with a ham bone or a bit of salt pork. That pot was the backbone of a winter week, and the leftovers became hash and soup. And something sour on the table every day, kraut or pickles or pickled beets alongside the heavy food. That's what kept forty men from getting tired of beans and potatoes in the middle of February.
