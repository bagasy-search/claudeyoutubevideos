[HOOK] See this blue ring of fire? Every piece of metal around it came out of five old oil filters. The kind you unscrew from a truck and throw in a bucket behind the shop. And this little stove, made from that trash, takes my garage from cold enough to see your breath to shirt sleeves in under an hour.
[HOOK] Most people look at a used oil filter and see a dirty can of junk. But cut one open and there is something inside that stove builders pay good money for. A steel tube, already punched full of perfect little holes. Hundreds of them. That tube is the whole secret of a clean blue flame, and every filter in the world has one.
[HOOK] Today I build the whole stove in front of you, from five filters and a few pieces of scrap. Every cut, every measurement. Then we light it, we dial in the drip, and I show you the numbers. How much oil it burns in an hour, and what the thermometer on the garage wall did.
[HOOK] And stay for the part about the bottom of the stove. There is one piece of filter you must never put in the fire zone, and it is the first thing almost everybody tries.
[INTRO] If you are new here, this is the Scrap Workshop. I build heat out of things people throw away. Old oil, old metal, old parts. Winter is here, the mornings in the shop are already cold, and the cheapest heat there is, is heat made from something that was headed for the trash.
[INTRO] Oil filters are perfect for this. Every oil change makes one. Any truck shop, any farm, any place that changes oil has a barrel of them out back, and nobody wants them. You can usually walk away with a whole box just by asking.
[SAFETY] One quick word before the grinder comes out. This stove lives in a garage or a shop, with the chimney going outside. Never in a bedroom, never in a closed room. Carbon monoxide alarm on the wall, fire extinguisher by the door, and keep the oil jug away from the heat. That is it. Now let's build.
[MATERIALS] Here is everything on the bench. Five used oil filters. Four of them are the big ones off a diesel truck, about four and a half inches across and ten inches tall. The fifth one is the biggest you can find, the kind off a tractor or a big rig, close to six inches across. That one becomes the burn pot.
[MATERIALS] Then the scrap. A square of steel plate, a quarter inch thick, about ten by ten inches, for the base. Three old bricks. A plastic jug for the oil. About five feet of quarter inch copper line off a dead refrigerator. One small brass needle valve. A coffee can, a handful of pop rivets, and a few lengths of three inch stove pipe for the chimney.
[MATERIALS] Tools are simple. An angle grinder with a thin cutting disc, a drill, a step bit, a rivet gun, tin snips and a tape measure. No welding anywhere on this stove. If you can drill a hole and pull a rivet, you can build it.
[STEP1] Step one, drain the filters. Punch a hole in the dome of each one with a screwdriver, stand them upside down over a drain pan, and leave them overnight. You will be surprised. Each big filter gives you almost half a cup of oil, and that oil goes straight into the fuel jug. The stove starts paying for itself before we even cut anything.
[STEP1] Next morning, wipe them down with a rag and a little degreaser. You do not need them clean like a kitchen pot. You just need them dry enough that the grinder does not throw oil in your face.
[STEP2] Step two, open them up. Clamp the filter in a vise, gently, and run the cutting disc all the way around, about half an inch below the dome. Go slow and let the disc do the work. The can is thin, so it cuts like butter. The top comes off in one piece.
[STEP2] Inside you find the paper element, the pleated stuff, soaked in old oil. Pull it out with pliers. That paper goes in a sealed bag for the recycling center, never in the stove. And right in the middle of that paper is the gold mine. A steel tube, about one and a half inches across, full of round holes from top to bottom.
[STEP2] Look at this. Every hole the same size, perfectly spaced, all the way around. If you tried to drill this yourself it would take you an hour and a box of bits. The filter factory already did it for you. Four filters give you four of these tubes, and that is our burner.
[STEP3] Step three, the burner tube. Stand the four perforated tubes end to end. They fit almost perfectly, because they are all the same size. Where two tubes meet, wrap a strip of thin sheet metal from the can around the joint, two inches wide, and put three pop rivets through it. Now you have one long tube, about thirty six inches tall, with holes from top to bottom.
[STEP3] That is too tall, so we cut it down to twenty inches. The bottom four inches of the tube we leave solid, so wrap that part with another strip and rivet it closed. You want the holes to start above the flame, not down in the oil. Air coming in down low just blows the fire around and makes smoke.
[STEP3] Why does this tube work so well? When the stove is running, the chimney pulls hard. Air rushes in through every one of those little holes and mixes with the hot oil vapor rising inside. That mixing is what turns a lazy orange fire into a roaring blue ring. More holes, more air, cleaner flame. And these tubes have more holes than anything you would ever drill by hand.
[STEP4] Step four, the burn pot. This is the big tractor filter, the fifth one. Cut the dome off just like the others and pull out the paper. Now, here is the piece that matters. Leave the bottom of the can, the threaded base plate, right where it is. That plate is thick steel, much thicker than the walls, and it is going to be the floor of the fire.
[STEP4] Cut the can down so it is only five inches tall. Then drill a hole in the side, one inch up from the bottom, just big enough for the copper line. That is where the oil comes in. Stand the burner tube in the center of the pot. The threaded hole in the base plate holds it straight, like a socket. Two small screws through the side keep it from tipping.
[STEP4] Fill the bottom of the pot with half an inch of clean dry sand, around the tube. The sand soaks up the first drips, spreads them out, and keeps the thin walls from getting too hot in one spot. It is the cheapest fire liner there is.
[STEP5] Step five, the heat chamber. Take the four empty filter cans, the outer shells, and cut both ends off every one. Now you have four steel sleeves, all the same width. Slide them over the burner tube, one on top of the other, so they make a jacket around it with an inch of space all the way around.
[STEP5] Where two cans meet, the top of one goes inside the bottom of the next by about a half inch. Squeeze the edge a little with pliers so it slides in, then three rivets per joint. This jacket is your heat chamber. The fire roars up inside the tube, the hot gas spills over the top, and the whole outside of these cans gets hot enough to warm the room.
[STEP5] The bottom can sits right on top of the burn pot. Cut four little notches in its bottom edge, half an inch deep. Those notches let a bit of air into the pot to keep the first flame alive while the stove warms up.
[STEP6] Step six, the cap and the chimney. The coffee can goes upside down on top of the heat chamber like a lid. Cut a three inch hole in the center of it with the step bit and the snips, and fold the edges up to make a collar. The stove pipe slides right over that collar and goes up and out through the wall.
[STEP6] Do not skimp on the chimney. Eight feet of pipe, going up, with no long sideways runs. A tall straight chimney is the engine of this stove. It is what pulls the air through all those little holes. A short chimney gives you a short, smoky fire.
[STEP7] Step seven, the drip feed. The oil jug goes up on a shelf, at least three feet away from the stove and a couple feet higher than the burn pot. Drill a hole in the cap, push the copper line in, and seal it with a little stove cement. At the bottom of the line goes the needle valve, and then the copper runs down and into the hole in the side of the pot.
[STEP7] Bend the end of the copper so it drips onto the sand, about an inch from the tube, not on the wall. And put a little loop in the line before it gets to the stove. That loop stops the heat from walking back up the copper toward the jug.
[STEP8] Last step, the base. The quarter inch plate sits on three bricks, and the whole stove stands on the plate. That keeps the heat off the floor and gives you a steady, flat place to work. Check it with a level. A stove that leans drips oil to one side of the pot and burns crooked.
[FIRE] Now the good part. To start it, put a small rag in the pot, soak it with a splash of oil, and drop a little piece of paper on top. Light the paper, and let it burn for a couple of minutes with the valve still closed. You want that sand and that base plate warm before any real oil goes in.
[FIRE] Now open the valve, just a crack. One drop every two seconds. Listen. At first it is quiet and the flame is orange and lazy. After about five minutes the tube gets hot, the chimney starts to pull, and you hear it change. A low roar. Look in through the top of the tube. There it is. That blue ring sitting right at the holes.
[FIRE] That sound is the stove breathing. Every hole in that filter tube is pulling in air, and every drop of oil is turning into gas before it burns. When you hear that roar and see that blue, you know you built it right.
[TEST] Now the numbers. This was a test on a cold morning. The garage is about four hundred square feet, an old one, not very well insulated. The thermometer on the wall read thirty eight degrees when I lit the stove.
[TEST] With the drip at one drop every second, after twenty minutes the wall thermometer read fifty. After forty five minutes, sixty four degrees. Jacket off, sleeves rolled up, working on the bench. The outside of the heat chamber was so hot you could not hold your hand near it.
[TEST] And the oil. At that setting it burns about a third of a liter an hour. Turn it down to one drop every two seconds once the room is warm, and it just holds the heat, sipping even less. A ten liter jug, which is about two oil changes on a pickup, runs this stove for more than a whole day of work in the shop.
[TEST] For the cost, I put almost nothing into this. The filters came out of a bucket. The plate, the bricks and the pipe came from the scrap pile. The valve came off a dead compressor. The only thing I bought was a bag of rivets.
[WHY] So why does this little thing burn so clean, when a pot of oil with a rag in it just smokes? Three reasons. First, the hot sand and the thick base plate turn every drop into vapor fast. Oil does not burn as a liquid, it burns as a gas. Second, the tall chimney pulls hard. Third, those hundreds of factory holes feed air into the vapor all along the tube, so it all burns before it reaches the top.
[WHY] When you see black smoke, one of those three is missing. The pot is not hot yet, the chimney is too short, or you are giving it more oil than the air can burn. Fix that and the smoke goes away.
[OILS] What can you burn in it? Used motor oil is the best, it is what this stove was built for. Old hydraulic oil and gear oil work too, mixed half and half with motor oil, because they are thicker and drip slower. Used cooking oil from the fryer burns fine as well, it just smells a little like french fries.
[OILS] What you never put in the jug is gasoline, thinner, or anything that smells sharp. Those make vapor in the jug itself, and vapor near a fire is how people get hurt. Heavy oil, slow drip, hot pot. That is the whole recipe.
[MISTAKES] Now the mistakes, because I made most of them. Mistake number one, the one I warned you about. Do not use the thin can walls for the floor of the fire. People cut the base plate off because it looks ugly, and put the thin dome down there instead. That dome is so thin the fire eats a hole in it in one week. The thick threaded base plate is the only part of a filter made to take that heat.
[MISTAKES] Mistake number two, too much oil at the start. If you open the valve wide on a cold pot, the oil pools in the sand, and when it finally catches it all goes up at once with a big puff of smoke. Start slow. Warm it up. Then turn it up a little at a time.
[MISTAKES] Mistake number three, dirty oil straight from the drain pan. Let it sit in a jug for a few days so the water and the gunk settle to the bottom, and pour off the top. Then pour it through an old t shirt before it goes into the fuel jug. Grit in the oil plugs the needle valve, and a plugged valve means a stove that dies in the middle of the night.
[MISTAKES] Mistake number four, forgetting these walls are thin. Filter cans are not a cast iron stove. Never put anything heavy on top, and keep kids, dogs and anything that burns at least three feet away. Give it a look over every couple of weeks. When one of the cans starts to look thin and flaky, you just swap it for another filter. You have a bucket of them.
[CLEAN] Cleaning is easy. Once a week, when it is cold, lift the heat chamber off, scoop the old sand out of the pot and put fresh sand in. Tap the burner tube with a stick to knock the carbon out of the holes. Ten minutes and it is like new.
[COOK] And one bonus. The coffee can on top gets hot enough to warm a pot of coffee or a can of soup. Put a little steel grate on it so the pot does not sit right on the thin metal, and you have a lunch heater in the shop for nothing.
[CLOSE] So that is it. Five old oil filters, a few pieces of scrap, and the oil from your own oil changes. A stove that roars blue, warms a whole garage in under an hour, and burns about a third of a liter an hour doing it.
