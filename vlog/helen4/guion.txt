[INTRO] You know me, I am Helen. Forty-one years behind this counter, in a little meat market in Iowa, and my father cut meat here before me. Most of my customers are retired, cooking for one or two, on a fixed income. And a pot of beans with a good bone in it is the cheapest real supper I know. So get your biggest pot out, honey. Let us cook.
[STORY] Here is how the Friday line started. My father used to sell the smoked hocks for a nickel, and most people threw them in the trash. My mother would take the ones nobody wanted and put them in a pot of beans on the back stove, every Friday, while he worked the counter. The smell went right out the front door. People would come in for pork chops and ask, Pearl, what is in that pot. And she would give them a little cup and tell them how she did it.
[STORY_B] When I took over the market, I kept the pot going. Now I sell about sixty hocks every Friday, mostly to people over sixty-five. One lady told me that pot of beans is the reason she can eat meat every week on her Social Security. So everything I am about to tell you, I learned from my mother, and from forty years of people coming back to tell me what went wrong.
[T01] Number one is buying the beans. Dry beans do not last forever. A bag that sat in a warehouse for two years will never get soft, no matter what you do to it. So buy them where people buy a lot of beans, where the shelf gets emptied every week. Look through the bag. You want smooth, shiny beans, all about the same color. If you see a lot of wrinkled ones, cracked ones, or a little dust in the bottom of the bag, put it back. And once you open a bag at home, use it within a year.
[T01_B] Which beans? For a ham hock pot, I like navy beans or great northern beans. They are small and white and they turn creamy. Pintos are good too, a little earthier. A one pound bag is about two cups of dry beans, and it turns into about six cups of cooked beans. That is the number to remember when you are standing in front of the shelf. One bag, six cups, the same as five or six cans.
[T02] Number two. Pour the beans out on a baking sheet and look at them. Every so often there is a little stone in there, the same color as a bean, or a shriveled bean that is all black. My mother called it picking the beans, and she did it every time. A little stone will crack a tooth, and a dentist costs more than a lifetime of beans. Then put them in a colander and rinse them under cold water until the water runs clear.
[T03] Number three is the one almost nobody does. Salt the soaking water. For one pound of beans, use a big bowl with about four quarts of cold water and three tablespoons of salt. Stir it and leave the beans in there overnight, at least eight hours, on the counter if your kitchen is cool, or in the refrigerator. The salt gets into the skins and makes them soft and tender, so they stay whole instead of splitting open in the pot. Your mother told you salt makes beans hard. It is the other way around in the soak.
[T03_B] In the morning, the beans will be about twice as big. Pour off the soaking water, every drop of it, and rinse the beans one more time. That water has the extra salt and a lot of the stuff that gives people gas. You do not want it in your pot.
[T04] Number four is for when you forgot to soak them. It happens to me too. Put the rinsed beans in your pot, cover them with water by two inches, add the same three tablespoons of salt, and bring it to a boil. Let it boil for two minutes. Then turn off the burner, put the lid on, and walk away for one hour. That is almost as good as soaking all night. Pour off that water and rinse them, the same as before.
[T05] Number five is the bone, and this is where the two dollars goes. Ask for a smoked ham hock. It is the lower part of the pig leg, and it is mostly skin, bone and a little meat, smoked a long time. That is why it is cheap, and that is exactly why it is good. The skin and the bone are full of the stuff that makes soup thick and silky. Pick one that is heavy for its size, with a deep brown color, about one pound.
[T05_B] Here is the butcher trick. Ask the person behind the meat counter to cut it into three pieces on the saw. It takes them ten seconds, and most of us will do it for nothing. Three pieces give the water three times as much bone to work on, so your pot gets the flavor in half the time. If you do not have a hock, a smoked turkey wing works, or the bone from your Easter ham that you wrapped and froze. Never throw a ham bone away.
[T06] Number six. Make a little broth first. Put the hock pieces in your pot with eight cups of cold water, one onion cut in half, two cloves of garlic and one bay leaf. Bring it to a simmer and let it go for forty-five minutes, before the beans ever go in. The water turns gold and smells like smoke. That is your cooking liquid. That forty-five minutes is the difference between beans and Friday beans.
[T06_B] If you want to do it the way my mother did on a cold Friday, cook the onion first. Put one spoon of oil or bacon grease in the empty pot, chop the onion and a rib of celery small, and let them go on medium heat for five minutes until they turn soft and a little gold. Then the hock pieces and the water go right on top. Those five minutes put a sweetness in the pot that a raw onion never gives you. And save the bay leaf. You fish it out at the end.
[T07] Number seven is the whole title of this video. Never boil your beans in plain water. A bean is like a little sponge. Whatever you cook it in is what it tastes like. Plain water in, plain water out. That is why Earl's beans tasted like nothing. When you cook them in that smoky hock broth, every bean drinks it up from the inside. You can taste the difference with your eyes closed.
[T07_B] So now the soaked beans go into the hock broth. Add enough water so they are covered by about one inch. Do not add any more salt yet. The hock is already salty, and it lets more out the whole time it cooks. Salt goes in at the very end, and I will tell you when.
[T08] Number eight is the heat. Beans do not like a hard boil. A hard boil throws them around in the pot and splits the skins, and you get mush on the outside and hard in the middle. Bring it up to a boil, then turn it down until you see just a few lazy bubbles. Put the lid on, but leave it open a crack. Most beans take about one and a half to two hours like that. Stir from the bottom every half hour so nothing sticks.
[T08_B] One warning on red kidney beans. Raw kidney beans have something in them that will make you very sick, and a slow cooker on low does not get hot enough to kill it. So if you use red kidney beans, boil them hard for ten full minutes first, then turn them down. Navy beans, pintos and great northern beans are fine with a gentle simmer from the start.
[T09] Number nine. Keep the beans under the liquid the whole time. A bean that sticks up out of the water cooks slower than the others and stays hard. Look in the pot every time you stir. If the liquid is getting low, pour in a cup of hot water from the kettle. Never cold water, because cold water stops the cooking and the beans seize up.
[T10] Number ten is the one that keeps beans hard for hours. Acid. Tomatoes, vinegar, ketchup, barbecue sauce, molasses. If any of those go in at the start, the skins get tough and the beans just will not soften, no matter how long you wait. So if your recipe has tomatoes or a sweet sauce, they go in only after the beans are already soft. My mother learned that from a pot of baked beans she cooked for six hours on a church Sunday. They were still crunchy at supper.
[T10_B] The same goes for brown sugar and molasses in baked beans. Sugar is fine for flavor, but it slows the softening down too. So cook the beans soft first with the hock, just like I am showing you, and then mix in the sauce and put them in the oven for the last hour. You get the soft bean and the sticky sauce, and nobody at church crunches on supper.
[T11] Number eleven is for hard water. If you have a well, or white crust on your faucets, your water is hard, and hard water keeps beans from getting soft too. Add a quarter teaspoon of baking soda to the pot, no more. Just a pinch. Too much and they taste like soap and go to mush. A quarter teaspoon for a pound of beans, and that is all.
[T12] Number twelve. Do not test just one bean. Take five beans out with a spoon and bite every one. Some always cook faster than others. When all five are creamy all the way through, with no chalky little center, your beans are done. Another way my mother checked. She would blow on a spoonful of beans, and if the skins curled back and peeled, they were ready.
[T12_B] Give yourself time the first Friday. Start the soak Thursday night, start the hock broth at about noon, and the beans go in at one. By four o'clock you have a pot of beans, and the whole house smells like my store. If they are done early, turn off the burner and leave the lid on. Beans only get better sitting in their own liquid. They are even better the next day.
[T13] Number thirteen. Pick the hock. Take the pieces out on a plate and let them cool for ten minutes. Pull off the skin, pull the meat off the bone with a fork, and chop it small. Throw away the bone, the skin and the hard little pieces of fat. One hock gives you about one cup of real smoked meat, and it all goes back in the pot. Two dollars of pork, and now there is a little meat in every spoonful.
[T13_B] Then thicken the pot. Take out one cup of beans with some liquid, mash them with a fork, and stir them back in. In ten minutes the liquid turns thick and creamy, like gravy. My mother called it the pot liquor, and some Fridays people asked for a cup of just that. Now taste it, and only now add salt, a little at a time. And a small splash of vinegar at the very end, after the beans are soft, wakes the whole pot up.
[T14] Number fourteen is the freezer. One pot is eight big bowls. If you cook for one, you are not going to eat eight bowls this week, and you should not. Let the beans cool, then put two cups in each freezer bag, with plenty of the liquid, so they do not dry out. Squeeze the air out, lay them flat, and write the date on the bag. They keep about six months in the freezer, and four days in the refrigerator.
[T14_B] To warm them up, take a bag out of the freezer the night before and let it thaw in the refrigerator. Put the beans in a small pot with a splash of water and warm them slowly, with the lid on. Beans always drink up liquid while they sit, so the next day they look thicker than you left them. A little water brings them right back. Over a square of cornbread or a scoop of rice, that is a whole supper for about fifty cents.
[TROUBLE] Now, a few problems I hear about at the counter. If your beans are still hard after three hours, they are old beans, or you have hard water, or somebody put tomatoes in too early. Add the pinch of baking soda and keep simmering. If they split and turned to mush, you boiled them too hard, or you skipped the salt soak. It still tastes good. Mash it and call it bean soup. And if your pot is too salty, that hock was a salty one. Add a peeled potato cut in half, simmer twenty minutes, and take it out, or just add more water and more beans.
[TROUBLE_B] One more. If beans give you gas, the soaking helps, and pouring off that soaking water helps more. Eat a small bowl a few times a week instead of a big bowl once, and your body gets used to them. That is what my doctor told me, and it worked.
