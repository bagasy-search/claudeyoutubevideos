[HOOK] At three in the morning, the truckers didn't order off the menu. They just sat down at the counter, pushed back their caps, and said, the usual. And the usual was a sandwich. A hot one, most nights. Bread off the griddle, meat off the flat top, a cup of coffee, and back on the road in twenty minutes. Twenty-five of those sandwiches I made for fifty years, and most of them you won't find on any menu today. Tonight I'm going to give you every one of them.
[HOOK] A few weeks back I stopped at one of those big new travel centers on the interstate. Bright lights, forty pumps, a wall of sandwiches in plastic boxes. I picked one up. Turkey, cold, on bread that bent like a wet sponge. Nine dollars and forty cents. I put it back. I thought about the men who used to sit at my counter, and I thought, they'd never have paid nine dollars for that. Not in a hundred years.
[HOOK] Maybe your dad drove a truck. Maybe your grandfather stopped at a diner like mine on the way to work. You remember a sandwich, the smell of it, the way the plate was so hot you couldn't touch it, and you try to make it at home and it comes out flat and dry and sad. Listen to me. It was never your fault. Nobody ever wrote down the little things the night cooks did. That's what I'm here for.
[PROMISE] So here's how we're going to do it. Twenty-five sandwiches, counting down to number one. What it was, what it cost on my board, who ordered it, and the one trick that made it right. Number seven is a sandwich made out of last night's dinner, and the drivers fought over it. And number one is the plate that kept the night shift alive for fifty years. A couple of these, your doctor won't love. That's fine. We're not eating them every day.
[INTRO] You know me, I'm Lou. Fifty years behind a griddle. My diner sat right off the state highway, with a gravel lot big enough for six rigs. After midnight, the place belonged to the truckers. The jukebox was off, the radio was on low, and the coffee pot never got cold. Grab a cup. Let's go.
[N25] Number twenty-five. The fried egg sandwich. The simplest thing on the board. Two eggs fried hard in bacon grease, salt and plenty of black pepper, on white bread toasted on the griddle, with a slice of onion if you wanted it. Twenty cents in nineteen sixty-five. The young drivers ordered it when money was tight at the end of the month. The trick is to break the yolks with the spatula and let them set flat. A runny egg in a sandwich ends up on your shirt, and a trucker only packed two shirts.
[N24] Number twenty-four. The fried bologna. Thick bologna, cut a quarter inch, not those thin floppy slices from the package. You cut two little slits on the edge before it hits the griddle, otherwise the middle puffs up like a dome and only the edges brown. Fried dark, on soft white bread with yellow mustard and a slice of cheese melted on top. Thirty-five cents. Men from the coal country ordered it like it was steak, and in a way it was.
[N23] Number twenty-three. The egg salad on toast. Hard boiled eggs, chopped by hand with a fork, not mashed into paste. Mayonnaise, a spoon of mustard, a pinch of paprika and a little celery for the crunch. Twenty-five cents. This one we made in the afternoon for the drivers who wanted something cold to take in the cab. The trick is to salt the eggs while they're still warm. Cold eggs don't take the salt, and the whole thing tastes like nothing.
[N22] Number twenty-two. The liverwurst and onion. Now I know half of you just made a face. But at my counter, the older drivers swore by it. Liverwurst, sliced thick, on dark rye bread, with a thick slice of raw sweet onion and brown mustard. Thirty cents. The secret is the rye. Liverwurst on white bread is a mistake. The rye stands up to it, and the onion cuts right through the richness.
[N21] Number twenty-one. The pimento cheese. The southern drivers brought this one up the road with them. Sharp cheddar grated by hand, chopped red pimentos, mayonnaise and a little cayenne, spread thick on white bread. Twenty-five cents cold, thirty-five grilled. And grilled is the way. Butter on the outside, low heat on the griddle, until the cheese starts to run out of the edges. The trick is grating your own cheese. The bagged kind has a powder on it so it doesn't stick, and it never melts right.
[N20] Number twenty. The western, some folks called it the Denver. Two eggs scrambled on the griddle with diced ham, green pepper and onion, cooked flat like a little omelet, folded to fit the bread and laid on buttered toast. Forty cents. This was the four in the morning sandwich. A man had been driving all night and he wanted breakfast, but he didn't want to sit down with a fork. The trick is to cook the onion and the pepper first, for two minutes, before the eggs go on. Raw onion in eggs never cooks through.
[N19] Number nineteen. The tuna melt. Tuna salad with celery, onion and mayonnaise, on rye, with a slice of cheese on top, grilled on the flat top under a pot lid so the cheese melts before the bread burns. Forty-five cents. The trick is the pot lid, and a tablespoon of water on the griddle right next to the sandwich. The steam comes up under the lid and melts the cheese in thirty seconds. That's how a diner cook does it.
[N18] Number eighteen. The BLT. Everybody thinks they know this one. Most people get it wrong. Bacon, lettuce, tomato on toast with mayonnaise. Fifty cents. Here's what we did. The bread got toasted on the griddle in the bacon grease, not in a toaster. The tomato got salted and peppered on the cutting board, by itself, before it went in. And the bacon went on in a weave, so you got bacon in every bite and not three strips sliding out the back.
[N17] Number seventeen. The sloppy joe. Ground beef browned on the griddle with onion and green pepper, then a sauce of ketchup, a little mustard, brown sugar and a splash of vinegar, cooked down thick in a pot on the back of the stove. On a toasted bun. Forty cents. The drivers loved it but they hated the mess in the cab, so we served it open on a plate with a fork. The trick is the vinegar. Without it, the sauce is just sweet. With it, the whole thing wakes up.
[N16] Number sixteen. The hot dog on toast. No bun. We split the hot dog down the middle, fried it flat on the griddle until it curled and the edges went black, and put it on a slice of buttered toast folded around it, with mustard, onions and relish. Twenty cents. Somebody ran out of buns one night in nineteen sixty and the drivers liked it better. Splitting it is the trick. More of the meat touches the hot steel.
[N15] Number fifteen. The ham salad. Leftover ham ground fine with sweet pickles, a little onion, mayonnaise and a spoon of mustard. On soft white bread or crackers. Thirty cents. This is how a diner never threw anything away. The end of the ham from Sunday dinner became Tuesday's ham salad. The trick is the sweet pickle juice. A tablespoon of it in the mix and people asked what we put in it.
[N14] Number fourteen. The chopped steak sandwich. Ground beef, a quarter pound, shaped long and flat like a cube steak, seasoned with salt, pepper and a little onion, cooked on the griddle, and laid on a long roll with fried onions and a ladle of brown gravy. Fifty-five cents. This was the poor man's steak sandwich. The trick is shaping the meat long, so it fits the roll. A round patty on a long roll is a sandwich that's empty at both ends.
[N13] Number thirteen. The grilled cheese and tomato soup. I know, the soup isn't a sandwich, but nobody ordered one without the other. American cheese on white bread, buttered on the outside, cooked low and slow. Thirty-five cents with the cup of soup. The trick nobody knows is to put a little butter in the pan, not just on the bread. And cook it low. High heat burns the outside and leaves the cheese cold in the middle.
[N12] Number twelve. The fried fish sandwich. Friday nights. A piece of cod dipped in batter, fried golden, on a soft bun with tartar sauce, shredded lettuce and a slice of cheese. Sixty cents. The Catholic drivers ordered it every Friday like clockwork. The trick is the tartar sauce. We made it every morning. Mayonnaise, chopped pickle, a little onion, lemon juice. The jar kind tastes like nothing.
[N11] Number eleven. The Reuben. Corned beef, sauerkraut, Swiss cheese and Russian dressing, on rye, grilled. Seventy-five cents. Expensive for us, but a big trucker eating a Reuben at two in the morning was a happy trucker. The trick is the sauerkraut. Squeeze it dry in your fist before it goes on. Wet sauerkraut makes soggy bread, and soggy bread is the end of a Reuben.
[N10] Number ten. The meatloaf sandwich. Cold meatloaf from the blue plate the night before, sliced thick, fried on the griddle until both sides got a crust, and put on white toast with ketchup and a slice of raw onion. Forty cents. Some of the drivers wouldn't eat meatloaf hot on a plate, but they'd eat this every night. The trick is frying the slice. Cold meatloaf in a sandwich is just bread with more bread inside.
[N9] Number nine. The patty melt. A beef patty, shaped oval, on rye bread with grilled onions and Swiss cheese, all of it grilled together until the cheese glues the whole thing shut. Sixty-five cents. Most people think this is just a burger on different bread. It's not. The onions are the whole sandwich. Cook them slow on the side of the griddle for twenty minutes, with a pinch of salt, until they're brown and soft and sweet.
[N8] Number eight. The Monte Cristo. Now this was the fancy one. Ham, turkey and Swiss cheese on white bread, the whole sandwich dipped in egg batter like French toast and fried on the griddle, with a dust of powdered sugar and a little cup of jelly on the side. Eighty-five cents. The young drivers thought it was a joke until they tried it. The trick is a thin batter, eggs and a splash of milk, and a quick dip. Soak it and it falls apart.
[N7] Number seven. And here's the one made from last night's dinner. The turkey dinner sandwich. Leftover roast turkey, a scoop of stuffing, a spoon of cranberry sauce and gravy, all on thick white bread. Sixty cents. We only had it on the days after we roasted turkeys, and the word got around on the CB radio. Drivers would plan their route to hit my lot that morning. The trick is warming the stuffing on the griddle until it gets a crust. That crust is what made them come back.
[N6] Number six. The club sandwich. Three slices of toast, turkey, bacon, lettuce, tomato and mayonnaise, cut in four triangles and held together with toothpicks. Ninety cents. It was the most expensive sandwich on the board, and the drivers ordered it on payday. The trick is the middle slice of toast. It holds the whole thing up. And toast all three slices at once, so they're all warm.
[N5] Number five. The hot beef sandwich. Thin sliced roast beef, warmed in the pan juices, on soft white bread, with a ladle of brown gravy over the whole thing and mashed potatoes on the side. Seventy cents. This is where the diner started to feel like home for a man who'd been gone three weeks. The trick is the gravy. Make it with the drippings from the roast, not from a powder. Flour cooked in the fat until it smells like toast, then the stock, slow.
[N4] Number four. The fried pork tenderloin. A pork loin cutlet, pounded flat as a dinner plate, dipped in egg and cracker crumbs and fried until it hangs off the bun on every side. Pickles, mustard, onion. Seventy-five cents. The drivers from the Midwest wouldn't eat anything else. The trick is crushed saltine crackers instead of bread crumbs. They fry up crispier and they hold on better.
[N3] Number three. The hot turkey open face. White bread, a big pile of sliced roast turkey, a scoop of mashed potatoes right on top of the bread, and the whole plate covered in turkey gravy. Eighty cents. You ate it with a knife and fork. A trucker once told me it was the only thing that tasted like his mother's house. The trick is the bread goes on the plate first and soaks up the gravy. Don't skip it, it's the best part.
[N2] Number two. The steak and egg sandwich. A thin cube steak, fried quick on the hot griddle, with a fried egg on top, runny this time, on a toasted roll with a little butter and salt and pepper. One dollar and ten cents. This was the sandwich for the driver who'd just been paid for a long haul. The trick is to cook the steak hot and fast, one minute a side, and let it rest on the bun while the egg cooks.
[N1] And number one. The sandwich that kept the night shift alive. The fried egg and ham on a griddle biscuit with sausage gravy poured over the top. A biscuit made that morning, split and toasted on the flat top, a thick slice of ham, a fried egg, and white pepper gravy with crumbled sausage. Sixty cents. Every driver who came through my door at three in the morning ordered it at least once, and most of them never ordered anything else again. That was the usual. The trick is the gravy. Brown the sausage, leave the fat in the pan, sprinkle flour over it and cook it two minutes, then whole milk, slow, and a lot of black pepper.
[STORY] Now why did all of these go away? It wasn't that people stopped liking them. It was the highway. The new roads went around the little towns, and the big stations came in with the pumps and the showers and the sandwiches in boxes, made somewhere far away and trucked in. You can't fry a bologna sandwich in a box. And the drivers stopped having time to sit down. The clock started running their lives.
[STORY] But I'll tell you something. Every one of these sandwiches still works in your kitchen. You need a flat pan or a cast iron skillet, a spatula, some butter, and a little patience. And that's it. No machine. Most of these cost you less than two dollars to make today, and they'll taste better than anything you'll buy at a counter on the interstate.
