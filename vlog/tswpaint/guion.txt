[INTRO] Big oil stoves are great in a big garage. But a lot of you told me you have a small shed, a little workshop, a hunting shack, or a corner of a barn. You do not need a stove made from a brake drum for that. You need something small that heats and cooks. That is this one.

The tuna can and the tall can came out of the kitchen trash this week. The pipe is a cut off piece from a muffler shop. The only thing that might cost you anything is the little brass valve, and you can pull one off almost any dead air compressor.
[SAFETY] One quick word before the snips come out. This stove is small, but it is still a real fire. Chimney goes outside, never in a bedroom, never in a closed room. Carbon monoxide alarm on the wall, fire extinguisher by the door, and keep the oil bottle away from the heat. That is it. Let's build.
[MATERIALS] Here is everything on the bench. One empty steel paint can, one gallon, with its lid. Burn the old paint out first, outside, with a small wood fire, then wire brush it. You want bare steel inside, not old paint that smokes every time you light it.
[MATERIALS] Then the small stuff. One tuna can, the short wide kind. One tall food can, about four inches across, from tomatoes or beans. A short piece of two inch steel pipe, about twenty inches long, off an old exhaust. A piece of old oven rack. A plastic bottle for the oil, a few feet of quarter inch copper line, and one small brass needle valve.
[MATERIALS] Tools. A drill with a quarter inch bit, a step bit, tin snips, a marker and a tape measure. That is the whole list. No welding, no grinder needed if your snips are sharp.
[STEP1] Step one, the burn pot. That is the tuna can. Fill it halfway with clean dry sand and set it in the middle of the bottom of the paint can. The sand drinks up the first drops of oil, spreads them out, and keeps the thin bottom from getting too hot in one spot.
[STEP1] Before the sand goes in, punch three small dents in the bottom of the tuna can with a nail, from the inside out, so it stands on three little feet. That lifts it a hair off the floor of the paint can. Air can slip under it, and the heat does not sit right on the thin bottom of the paint can.
[STEP1] Why a tuna can? Because it is short and wide. A short pot keeps the fire low, right where the air comes in. A tall pot hides the fire down in a hole and it smokes.
[STEP2] Step two, the burner. That is the tall food can. Cut both ends off with the can opener and the snips so it becomes a tube. Then mark three rings around it with the marker, one inch apart, starting one inch from the bottom.
[STEP2] On each ring, drill ten holes with the quarter inch bit. That is thirty holes. Go slow and push the drill gently, a thin can wants to fold if you push hard. Put a block of wood inside the can as a backer and it drills clean.
[STEP2] That tube stands on top of the tuna can, centered, like a chimney inside the stove. Those thirty holes are where the air comes in and mixes with the oil vapor. That mixing is what gives you the blue flame.
[STEP2] One trick for the holes. Before you drill the tall can, fill it with water and freeze it overnight. The ice holds the thin metal round and stiff, and every hole comes out clean and round, with no dents. When you are done, run it under warm water, the ice falls out, and you have a perfect burner tube.
[STEP3] Step three, the air holes in the body. Around the bottom of the paint can, one inch up from the floor, drill a ring of eight holes with the step bit, three quarters of an inch across. This is where the fire gets its fresh air.
[STEP3] And one more hole on the side, just above the tuna can, for the oil line. Make that one the size of your copper line, no bigger. A loose hole there pulls in cold air right where you do not want it.
[STEP4] Step four, the lid. Here is the hole I warned you about. Most people cut the chimney hole right in the middle of the lid, straight above the burner. Do not do that. The middle of the lid is your cooking spot. That is where the hottest gas hits, and that is where your pot goes.
[STEP4] Instead, cut a two inch hole off to the side, near the edge of the lid, with the step bit. That is for the chimney. Then in the middle of the lid cut a round opening, three inches across, right above the burner tube. The flame comes up through that opening and hits the bottom of your pot.
[STEP4] So the hot gas does two jobs. First it hits the pot and cooks. Then it rolls across the lid and goes out the chimney on the side. Heat and a kitchen, out of the same flame. That is the two in one.
[STEP5] Step five, the cooking rack. A piece of old oven rack, cut with the snips to sit across the lid. Bend the ends down a little so it sits steady. Your pot sits on the rack, half an inch above the opening. That little gap is important. If the pot sits right on the hole, it chokes the fire.
[STEP6] Step six, the chimney. The two inch exhaust pipe goes into the side hole of the lid. Fold four little tabs of the lid up around it with pliers, so it stands straight. Then it goes up and out through the wall. For a small stove, six feet of chimney is plenty, but it has to go up, not sideways.
[STEP7] Step seven, the drip. The plastic bottle goes upside down on a hook on the wall, a foot higher than the stove and two feet off to the side. Push the copper line into the cap and seal it with a little stove cement. The needle valve goes in the line, and the end of the copper goes into the side hole and stops right over the sand.
[STEP7] Put one small loop in the copper before the stove. That loop stops the heat from creeping up the line. And poke one tiny hole in the bottom of the bottle, which is now the top, so air can get in and the oil flows steady.
[STEP8] Last step, the base. The paint can stands on two bricks, on a steel plate or a flat stone. Never right on a wooden floor or a wooden bench. Check it with a level so the oil lands in the middle of the sand, not on the side.
[FIRE] Now the fun part. Lift the lid, drop a small oily rag in the tuna can, and a piece of paper on top. Light it, put the lid back, and wait two minutes with the valve closed. You want that sand warm before any oil comes in.
[FIRE] Open the valve a crack. One drop every three seconds. This is a small stove, it needs less oil than the big ones. At first the flame is orange and quiet. Then the burner tube warms up, the chimney starts to pull, and you hear it. A soft roar. Look through the opening in the lid. Blue flame around the holes.
[FIRE] Once it is roaring, you can go to one drop every two seconds. More than that, and you see smoke at the chimney. So you turn it back. The flame tells you exactly where it wants to be.
[TEST] Now the numbers. This was a cold morning in my small shed, about ten by twelve feet, old boards, not much insulation. The thermometer on the wall read thirty five degrees when I lit it.
[TEST] First the kitchen. A liter of cold water in a small pot on the rack. It came to a full boil in nine minutes. That is about the same as a camping stove, and this one runs on oil you were going to throw away.
[TEST] Then the heat. After thirty minutes, the shed was at fifty. After one hour, fifty eight. Jacket off, working at the bench, coffee in hand. For a little can, that is a lot of heat.
[TEST] And the oil. At one drop every two seconds, it burns about a fifth of a liter an hour. A two liter bottle runs it for about ten hours. One oil change from a small car is a whole weekend in the shed.
[TEST] I also ran it overnight once, turned down to one drop every four seconds, just to see. At six in the morning the shed was still at forty eight degrees while the frost was white on the grass outside, and there was still oil left in the bottle. For a stove that cost almost nothing, that surprised me.
[TEST] Your numbers will be different. Your shed, your weather, your oil. But if you build it the way I showed you, with the chimney on the side and the drip slow, you will see that little can do a lot more than you expect.
[WHY] Why does such a small thing burn so clean? The same three reasons as every good oil stove. The hot sand turns every drop into vapor, because oil burns as a gas, not as a liquid. The chimney pulls air in through the holes. And the burner tube mixes that air into the vapor before it reaches the top.
[WHY] The only difference is the size. Everything is smaller, so everything happens faster. It lights faster, it warms faster, and it cools down faster when you shut it off. That is perfect for a small space you only use a few hours a day.
[POWER] And think about the night the power goes out. The electric heater is dead, the kitchen stove is dead, the coffee maker is dead. This little can keeps right on going. No wire, no switch, no motor. Gravity feeds it, the chimney pulls it, and you still get hot coffee in the morning.
[POWER] That is why a lot of people keep one of these in the shed even if they have other heat. It is small, it stores on a shelf, and it works when nothing else does. Keep a bottle of old oil next to it and you are ready.
[OILS] What can you burn? Used motor oil is the best. Old cooking oil from the fryer works great in this one too, and it smells a lot nicer in a small shed. Old hydraulic oil works if you mix it half and half with motor oil.
[OILS] What never goes in the bottle is gasoline, thinner, or anything with a sharp smell. Those make vapor in the bottle itself, and vapor next to a fire is how people get hurt. Heavy oil, slow drip, hot sand.
[MISTAKES] Now the mistakes. Number one, the chimney in the middle of the lid. I said it already, but it is the big one. The pot blocks the chimney, the fire chokes, and you get smoke in your face. Chimney on the side, cooking in the middle.
[MISTAKES] Number two, leaving the old paint inside. The first time you light it, the paint burns off with a nasty smell and black smoke. Burn the can out outside first, before you build anything.
[MISTAKES] Number three, too much oil. A small stove floods fast. If you see oil pooling on the sand, close the valve, let it burn down, and start again slower.
[MISTAKES] Number four, a pot that is too big. A huge pot covers the whole lid and chokes the chimney too. Use a pot no wider than the lid. A small kettle, a coffee pot, a little saucepan.
[MISTAKES] Number five, cans that are too thin. Some food cans today are very light. If you can bend the can with two fingers, it is too thin for the burner tube. Look for a heavier can, or use a short piece of steel pipe instead. A burner tube that sags in the heat makes a crooked, smoky fire.
[CLEAN] Cleaning is ten minutes. Once a week, when it is cold, lift the lid, pull the burner tube, and dump the old sand. Tap the tube to clear the holes, put in fresh sand, and you are ready again.
[COOK] And here is what it cooked this week. Coffee every morning. A can of soup at lunch. Eggs in a little skillet. Even a pot of beans on a cold afternoon, simmering away while the shed stayed warm.
[COOK] One more cooking tip. Because the flame hits the middle of the pot and then rolls to the side, the middle cooks hot and the edge stays gentle. So when the coffee is done, slide the pot toward the chimney side and it stays warm without boiling over. It is a little stove with a hot spot and a warm spot, just like the old wood cook stoves.
[COOK] And if you want more heat in the room and less on the pot, just take the pot off. Then the whole lid gets hot and throws heat into the shed. Pot on, it is a kitchen. Pot off, it is a heater.
