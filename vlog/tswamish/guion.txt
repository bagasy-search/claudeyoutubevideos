[HOOK] No gas line. No electric heater. No fan, no pump, no plug anywhere. Just an old water heater tank somebody dragged to the curb, a jug of used motor oil on a shelf, and a blue flame roaring inside it. This is how I heat my workshop all winter.
[HOOK] People who live off the grid have always known this. If your heat needs electricity, your heat stops the night the power stops. So you build a stove that runs on gravity and air. Nothing to break, nothing to plug in, nothing to pay for every month.
[HOOK] Today I build the whole thing in front of you, step by step, with every measurement. Then we light it, set the drip, and I show you the numbers. How warm it gets the shop, how much oil it burns, and how much it cost me. Hint, almost nothing.
[HOOK] And stay for the brick ring at the end. It is the oldest trick there is for keeping a room warm, and it doubles how long this stove keeps giving heat.
[INTRO] If you are new here, this is the Scrap Workshop. I build heat out of things people throw away. Old oil, old metal, old parts. Winter is coming, and the cheapest heat there is, is heat made from something that was headed for the trash.
[INTRO] Think about how folks heated a shop a hundred years ago. Plain farm families, and plenty of people still today, including Amish families, keep their heat simple. Wood, kerosene, gravity. Things you can fix with your own hands. No circuit board, no app, no service call.
[INTRO] I took that same way of thinking and pointed it at the one fuel every garage already has. Old motor oil. And the body of the stove comes from the most common piece of scrap in any town. An old water heater.
[SAFETY] One quick word before the grinder comes out. Drain the tank and take the water heater apart outside, and if it was a gas heater, make sure the gas line was shut and capped by someone who knows what they are doing. Chimney goes outside, never in a bedroom, a carbon monoxide alarm on the wall and an extinguisher by the door. That is it. Let's build.
[MATERIALS] Here is everything on the bench. One old electric water heater, forty gallons. Strip off the sheet metal jacket and the foam, and inside you find a heavy steel tank about sixteen inches across. That tank is the stove.
[MATERIALS] Then the small stuff. One old steel cooking pot, about eight inches across, for the burn pot. A piece of three inch steel pipe, twenty inches long, for the burner. A plastic jug, a few feet of quarter inch copper line and one small brass needle valve. A few lengths of four inch stove pipe. And about thirty old red bricks.
[MATERIALS] Tools. An angle grinder, a drill, a step bit, a tape measure and a marker. No welding needed for any of it.
[MATERIALS] Getting the tank out is the dirty part. Unscrew the top panel, pull the two elements and the thermostat covers, then peel the thin outer jacket off with tin snips. The yellow foam underneath comes off in chunks with a putty knife. Bag it and keep it out of the burn pile. Twenty minutes and you are down to bare steel.
[STEP1] Step one, cut the tank. Stand it up and measure thirty inches from the bottom. Mark a line all the way around and cut it with the grinder. The bottom part with its curved floor is your stove body. The top part with the curved dome is your lid.
[STEP1] Water heater tanks have glass lining inside, like a thin coat of enamel. It will crackle and flake the first time you fire it, so the first burn goes outside, with the door open, and you let it smoke off. After that it is clean steel.
[STEP1] Before you cut, pull the long rod out of the top of the tank. That is the anode rod, a steel wire with a soft metal coat. Unscrew it with a big socket and throw it in the scrap bucket. Then plug the two side holes where the heating elements were with two short bolts and big washers. The stove has to be sealed everywhere except the door and the chimney.
[STEP2] Step two, the door. On the side, three inches up from the bottom, cut a window five inches wide and four inches tall. That is where you light it and where you look at the flame. Keep the piece you cut. Two bolts and a strip of steel make it a hinged door.
[STEP2] File the edges of the window smooth with a flat file. A sharp cut edge on a stove you open every morning is a cut finger waiting to happen, and a smooth edge lets the little door close tight against the tank.
[STEP3] Step three, the burn pot. The old steel cooking pot goes in the center of the floor of the tank. Put two inches of dry sand in it. The sand catches the first drops, spreads them out, and soaks up the heat so every drop that lands turns into vapor.
[STEP3] Why a cooking pot? Thick steel bottom, straight sides, and every thrift store has a bin of them for a couple of coins. It holds the fire low and in the middle, right where the air comes in.
[STEP4] Step four, the burner. That is the three inch pipe. Mark five rings around it, one inch apart, starting two inches from the bottom. Eight holes on each ring with the quarter inch bit. That is forty holes.
[STEP4] The pipe stands in the middle of the pot on three short bolts, so it sits a little above the sand. Air comes in through the forty holes, it meets the oil vapor rising from the pot, and that mix is what burns blue.
[STEP4] Drilling a thick pipe goes faster if you start every hole with a small punch mark and a drop of oil on the bit. Clamp the pipe in a vise, go slow, and let the bit do the work. Forty holes takes about fifteen minutes and one cup of coffee.
[STEP5] Step five, the lid and the chimney. The dome goes back on top. Most water heaters have a fitting right in the center of the dome. Open it up to four inches with the step bit, and the stove pipe goes right there.
[STEP5] The burner pipe rises up under that hole, and the stove pipe slides down over it. Tall and straight, eight to ten feet, going up and out through the wall. A tall chimney pulls hard, and a hard pull is what makes the blue flame. No fan needed.
[STEP5] Where the pipe goes through the wall, use a metal wall thimble or a piece of sheet steel with a wide ring of clearance around it. Keep at least eighteen inches between the stove pipe and anything made of wood. The first three feet of pipe above the lid get hotter than you would think.
[STEP6] Step six, the drip. The oil jug goes on a shelf on the wall, two feet higher than the stove and three feet off to the side. Drill a hole in the cap, push in the copper line, and seal it. A tiny hole in the top of the jug so air can get in. The needle valve goes in the line, and the end of the copper goes in through the side of the tank and stops right over the pot.
[STEP6] Put one loop in the copper before it goes into the tank. That loop keeps heat from climbing back up the line. Gravity pushes the oil, the valve sets the drops, and that is the whole fuel system. Not one wire.
[STEP7] Step seven, the stand. The stove sits on four bricks on a steel plate, never right on a wooden floor. Put a level on the lid. If it leans, the drops land on the side of the pot and it smokes.
[FIRE] Now the fun part. Open the little door, drop an oily rag in the pot, put a piece of paper on top and light it. Close the door and let it burn three minutes with the valve closed. You want that sand hot before any oil comes in.
[FIRE] Now open the valve a crack. One drop every two seconds. At first the flame is orange and lazy. Then the pot gets hot, the burner pipe gets hot, the chimney starts to pull, and you hear it. A deep steady roar. Look through the door. Blue flame all around those holes.
[FIRE] Once it roars, turn it up to one drop a second. Any more than that and you will see smoke at the top of the chimney. So you turn it back. The flame tells you exactly where it wants to be.
[TEST] Now the numbers. This was a frosty morning in my workshop, six hundred square feet, an old pole barn with thin walls. The thermometer on the wall read thirty six degrees when I lit it.
[TEST] After thirty minutes, the wall read fifty one. After one hour, sixty five. I had my jacket off and was working at the bench. The whole tank was warm all the way around, and the lid on top was hot enough to boil a kettle.
[TEST] The oil. At one drop a second, it burns about half a liter an hour. Once the shop is warm, I turn it down and it holds the heat on less. A ten liter jug runs it for a long day of work.
[TEST] And the cost. The water heater was sitting at the curb. The pot came from a thrift store. The bricks came off an old chimney a neighbor tore down. The only thing I bought was the little brass valve and the copper line. And the fuel is the oil I drain out of my own truck and tractor.
[TEST] Then I tried the real test. I switched off the main breaker for the whole shop for an evening, just to see. The lights went out, the radio went quiet, and the stove did not care one bit. It kept roaring blue, the jug kept dripping, and I sat there by the glow with a mug of coffee. That is the whole point of a stove like this.
[BRICKS] Now the brick ring. This is the old trick I promised you. Stack the bricks in a loose ring around the stove, four inches away from the tank, three layers high. Leave the door side open so you can light it and look in.
[BRICKS] Those bricks soak up heat the whole time the stove runs. When you turn the valve off at the end of the day, the tank cools down fast, but the bricks keep giving heat back for hours. It is the same idea as a big masonry stove in an old farmhouse. Heavy things hold heat.
[BRICKS] With the ring, one hour after I shut it off, the shop was still at fifty nine. Without the ring, it dropped to fifty in the same hour. Same stove, same oil, just a pile of old bricks.
[BRICKS] And bricks are everywhere. Every time somebody tears down an old chimney, a garden wall or a barbecue, there is a pile of them going to the dump. Ask, and most people will help you load them, just to have them gone.
[WHY] Why does this burn so clean with no fan and no pump? Three reasons. The hot sand in the pot turns every drop into vapor, because oil burns as a gas, not as a liquid. The tall chimney pulls air in through the holes. And the burner pipe mixes that air into the vapor before it burns.
[WHY] When you see black smoke, one of those three is missing. The pot is still cold, the chimney is too short, or the holes are clogged with carbon. Fix that one thing and the blue comes back.
[OILS] What can you burn? Used motor oil is the best. Old hydraulic oil and gear oil work if you mix them half and half with motor oil. Old cooking oil from the fryer works too.
[OILS] What never goes in the jug is gasoline, thinner, or anything that smells sharp. Those make vapor in the jug itself, and vapor next to a fire is how people get hurt. Heavy oil, slow drip, hot pot.
[MISTAKES] Now the mistakes. Number one, firing it inside the first time. The glass lining inside the tank burns off with smoke the first time. Do that first burn outside.
[MISTAKES] Number two, too much oil on a cold pot. If you open the valve before the sand is hot, the oil pools, and when it finally catches it all goes up at once. Always warm it up first, then drip slow.
[MISTAKES] Number three, dirty oil. Let your old oil sit in a jug for a week so the sludge sinks, and pour off the top through an old t-shirt. Grit plugs the needle valve and you get a stove that starves and then floods.
[MISTAKES] Number four, the bricks right against the tank. Leave that four inch gap. Air has to move between the tank and the bricks, or the bricks just sit there cold on the outside.
[CLEAN] Cleaning takes ten minutes. Once a week, when it is cold, lift the lid, pull the burner pipe, and scrape the carbon out of the pot. Tap the pipe with a stick to knock the holes clear, put fresh sand in the pot, and you are ready for another week.
[CLEAN] Once a year, at the end of winter, take the chimney down and knock the soot out of the pipe outside. A clean chimney pulls better, and a stove that pulls well is a stove that burns blue.
[COOK] And a bonus. The dome on top gets hot enough to cook on. Coffee in the morning, a pot of beans at lunch, a skillet of potatoes on a cold afternoon. Heat and a kitchen out of one old water heater.
[CLOSE] So that is how you heat a workshop with no gas and no electricity. An old water heater tank, a cooking pot, a piece of pipe, a pile of old bricks and a jug of used oil. A stove that roars blue, warms six hundred square feet in an hour, and keeps going when the power is out.
[CLOSE] Next time you see a water heater sitting at the curb, ask for it. And keep your old oil in jugs. By the first real cold night, you will be glad you did.
