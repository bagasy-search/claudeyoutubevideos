[HOOK] Never buy boneless chicken breasts again. Last year I took them out of my meat case, and I have not sold one since. A lady named June was standing right where you are, with a two pound pack in her cart from the big store. Nine dollars and eighteen cents. Two breasts. She said, Helen, that is supper for me for four nights, if I am careful. Right next to my scale I had a whole chicken, almost five pounds, for nine dollars and six cents. Twelve cents less.
[HOOK_B] I told her, June, give me five minutes and one knife. I cut that whole chicken in front of her. Two big breasts, the same as her pack. Two thighs. Two drumsticks. Two wings. And a back and a frame that make two quarts of good broth. For less money than her two breasts. She put the pack back. Today I am going to show you how to do exactly what I showed June, at your kitchen table, with the knife you already own. Twice the suppers, for the same money.
[PROMISE] I am going to give you twelve steps and tricks, in the order you do them. Number four is the one joint that makes the whole chicken come apart like it wants to, and most people never find it. Number eight turns one big breast into two suppers, and if you cook for one, that alone pays for this whole video. And number nine is the part the store throws away, and it is the best part of the bird. Some of the people who sell you the little trays of breasts are not going to like this one. That is all right.
[INTRO] You know me, I am Helen. Forty-one years behind this counter, in a little meat market in Iowa, and my father cut meat here before me. Most of my customers are retired, cooking for one or two, on a fixed income. And chicken is what they buy the most. So grab a cutting board, honey. Let us get our money back.
[STORY] Here is why I stopped selling boneless breasts. Every week I watched the same thing. My customers paid me four dollars and fifty cents a pound for the breasts, and right next to them, the whole birds sat there at a dollar seventy-nine. Nobody bought the whole birds. When I asked why, they all said the same thing. I do not know how to cut it up. That is not a reason to pay more than double, honey. That is a reason to learn. So I took the breasts out of the case, and I put a sign up that says ask me, I will show you.
[STORY_B] My father taught me on a Saturday morning when I was eleven years old, on that same block back there. He put a chicken in front of me and he said, Helen, a chicken has seams. You do not cut a chicken, you open it where it already wants to open. It took me twenty minutes that first time. Now it takes me three. You will not be that fast the first time either, and that is fine.
[T01] Number one. Read the little number on the sticker. Not the price, the price per pound. That is the only number that tells you the truth. Boneless skinless breasts, most weeks, are somewhere around four dollars and fifty cents a pound. A whole chicken is usually under two dollars a pound. Bone-in breasts with the skin on are often around two dollars. So the boneless pack costs you more than twice as much per pound. You are paying somebody to take out a bone that takes you one minute to take out yourself.
[T01_B] And here is the thing nobody tells you. The bone and the skin you are paying to have taken away are not trash. They are what make the broth, and the broth makes the soup, and the soup makes two more suppers. When you buy boneless, you are paying more money for less food. When you buy the whole bird, the store is giving you the broth for nothing.
[T02] Number two is picking the bird. Look for one between four and five pounds. Bigger birds have more meat for the bones. Pick it up. It should feel plump and cold, and the package should be tight, with no tears and not a lot of liquid sloshing around in the bottom. Check the date on the sticker and buy the one with the most days left. And if the store has a family pack with two birds, that is usually the lowest price per pound in the whole case. One for this week, one for the freezer.
[T02_B] And if you really do not want to cut, here is the middle road. Ask the person behind the meat counter to cut the whole bird up for you. Most of us will do it for nothing, in two minutes, while you wait. You still pay the whole bird price per pound, and you still take home the back for the broth. Just say, can you cut this in eight pieces and give me the back. That is the whole sentence.
[T03] Number three. Do not wash your chicken. I know your mother did, and so did mine. But rinsing raw chicken in the sink splashes the germs all over your sink, your counter and your dish towel, and the cooking kills them anyway. Just take it out of the package and pat it dry with paper towels. Put a big cutting board on the table, a bowl for the bones, and a plate for the pieces. Throw the paper towels away right away, and wash your hands and the board with hot soapy water when you are done.
[T03_B] Now, the knife. You do not need a fancy one. You need a sharp one. A boning knife is nice, but a regular eight inch kitchen knife works, as long as it is sharp. A dull knife is the one that slips. If yours is dull, run it a few times on the back of a coffee mug, on that rough ring on the bottom. That is the poor man's sharpener, and it works.
[T04] Number four is the joint that changes everything. Lay the chicken on its back, legs toward you. Take one leg and pull it away from the body, gently. You will see the skin stretch between the leg and the breast. Cut only that skin. Now take the whole leg in your hand and bend it back, away from the body, like you are opening a book, until you hear a little pop. That pop is the hip joint coming out of its socket. Now your knife goes right through that joint, with no bone to cut at all.
[T04_B] And before you take that leg off, turn the chicken over. On the back, right above where the thigh was, there is a little round piece of meat sitting in a hollow of the bone. That is the oyster, and it is the most tender bite on the whole bird. Push your thumb in and scoop it out with the leg. Restaurants charge for that. You get it for nothing.
[T05] Number five. Split the leg in two. Lay the whole leg skin side down. Look at the line of yellow fat between the thigh and the drumstick. That line is the map. Bend the leg a little and feel for the joint with your finger, then put your knife right on that fat line and press down. If you hit hard bone, move a quarter inch and try again. When you are on the joint, the knife goes through like butter. Now you have a thigh and a drumstick.
[T05_B] And while we are on the legs, let me tell you a secret. The thigh is the best piece on the whole chicken, and it is the hardest one to ruin. It has a little more fat, so it stays juicy even if you leave it in the oven ten minutes too long. Put four thighs skin side up on a sheet pan with some potato chunks, salt and pepper, and roast them at four hundred twenty-five degrees for about forty minutes, until the skin is crisp. That is a Sunday supper for about two dollars.
[T06] Number six is the wings. Pull the wing out away from the body, the way you would stretch your own arm. Feel for the shoulder joint where it meets the breast. Cut around it and through it, and the wing comes off. You can cook it whole, or cut it at the two joints into a flat, a drumette and the tip. The tip has almost no meat on it, so it goes into your bone bowl for the broth.
[T07] Number seven, the breasts. Stand the chicken up, or lay it on its back with the neck end away from you. Find the hard ridge of bone that runs down the middle of the breast. Put your knife right next to that bone and cut straight down, about half an inch deep. Then lean the knife toward the ribs, keep the blade touching the bone, and use short, gentle strokes while you pull the meat away with your other hand. The breast comes off in one piece. Do the same on the other side. Those are the same two boneless breasts June paid nine dollars for.
[T07_B] Underneath each breast there is a little strip of meat that pulls loose by itself. That is the tenderloin. Save those two strips in a bag in the freezer, and after a few chickens you have a whole supper of chicken strips. And if all of this sounds like too much, here is the easy way. Leave the bone in. Cut the whole breast off the back with kitchen shears and split it down the middle. Bone-in breasts stay juicier in the oven, and you skip the hardest part.
[T08] Number eight is the trick for people who cook for one. A breast today is huge. Most of them are three quarters of a pound, sometimes a whole pound. That is two suppers, not one. Lay the breast flat on the board, put your hand on top of it, and slice it through the middle, the long way, with the knife flat, like you are cutting a bun. Now you have two thin cutlets. They cook in about four minutes on each side instead of twenty, they do not dry out, and one breast feeds you twice.
[T08_B] What do you do with a thin cutlet? Everything. Dust it in flour with salt and pepper and fry it in a spoon of butter for chicken with gravy. Dip it in egg and crushed crackers for a little fried chicken cutlet. Or cut it in strips for a stir fry with whatever vegetables you have. A thick breast is hard to cook right. A thin cutlet is almost impossible to ruin.
[T09] Number nine is the part the store throws away. The back, the frame, the neck and the wing tips. Put them in your big pot with a carrot, an onion cut in half, a stick of celery and a couple of bay leaves. Cover it all with about three quarts of cold water. Bring it up just to a simmer, not a hard boil, and let it go with the lid tilted for two or three hours. Strain it, and you have about two quarts of real chicken broth. A quart of broth at the store costs two or three dollars. You just made two quarts from the part you used to pay somebody to take away.
[T09_B] Cool the broth fast. Do not leave the big pot on the stove overnight. Set the pot in a sink of cold water, or pour the broth into a few shallow containers, and get it in the refrigerator within two hours. The next morning the fat on top will be solid. Lift it off with a spoon. The broth keeps three or four days in the refrigerator, or a few months in the freezer.
[T10] Number ten. The cooked meat on that frame is supper too. When the broth is done, let the bones cool until you can hold them. Pick off every little bit of meat from the back and the neck. It is usually a good cup of meat. Put it back in a quart of your broth with some noodles or rice and a handful of frozen vegetables, and that is a pot of chicken soup that feeds you two or three times. From the part of the chicken nobody wanted.
[T11] Number eleven is how you freeze it. Do not throw all the pieces into one bag. Put them in portions for one supper. Two thighs in a bag, two drumsticks in a bag, one cutlet in a bag. Press the air out, lay the bags flat, and write the date and what is inside with a marker. Raw chicken pieces keep well in the freezer for about nine months. And thaw them in the refrigerator, never on the counter.
[T12] And number twelve is the truth about the first time. The first chicken you cut up is going to take you fifteen or twenty minutes, and it is not going to look pretty. That is fine. The second one takes ten. By the fifth one, you will do it in five minutes while the coffee is brewing. Every one you do is about five dollars back in your purse. So do not judge yourself on the first one. My first one looked like it had been in a fight.
[TROUBLE] Now, a few problems I hear about at the counter. If you tore the breast while you were taking it off, you pulled before you cut. Keep the knife against the bone and let the knife do the work. If you cut into bone and the knife will not go through, you are not on the joint. Wiggle the piece with your hand, feel where it moves, and cut there. If your knife keeps slipping on the skin, your knife is dull or your chicken is wet. Pat it dry and sharpen the knife. And if you end up with a few ragged little pieces, do not throw them away. Chop them small for fried rice or a stir fry.
[TROUBLE_B] One more. Chicken is safe when the thickest part reaches one hundred sixty-five degrees on a meat thermometer. Thighs and drumsticks are actually better a little past that, around one hundred seventy-five, because the meat gets tender. A cheap thermometer costs a few dollars, and it is the best thing in my kitchen drawer. No more cutting it open to check and losing all the juice.
[MONEY] Let me do the arithmetic, because that is what my customers care about. June's pack of boneless breasts was about two pounds, for nine dollars and eighteen cents. That is four suppers for one person. The whole chicken was nine dollars and six cents. From that bird she gets the same two breasts, split into four cutlets, so the same four suppers. Then two more suppers from the thighs and drumsticks, a supper from the wings, two quarts of broth and a pot of soup that feeds her two or three more times. That is about ten meals instead of four, for twelve cents less. Twice the suppers, and then some.
[MONEY_B] Now say you used to buy two packs of boneless breasts a month. Buy two whole chickens instead. You spend the same money, and you get the thighs, the drumsticks, the wings and four quarts of broth on top. Buying those separately would cost you around fifteen dollars a month. That is about one hundred eighty dollars a year, just for changing which package you pick up. On a fixed income, that is a whole month of groceries.
[CLOSE] So here is your homework. Do not try to be fast. Buy one whole chicken this week, between four and five pounds. Pat it dry, find the pop in the hip joint, and take the legs off. That is all. If the rest feels like too much, roast the rest whole. Then come back and tell me in the comments: what is the price per pound on boneless breasts at your store this week? I want to see who is paying the most.
