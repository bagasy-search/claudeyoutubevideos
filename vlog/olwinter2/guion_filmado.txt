[P6|] Number one. The Potato, Onion and Egg Supper.
[P7|] This is the one Emil made the night before payday, when the money was gone and the next truck hadn't come up the road yet. Three potatoes, one onion, four eggs and a spoonful of bacon fat. For four plates, it comes to about two dollars and sixty cents. That's sixty-five cents a plate.
[P8|] You heat three tablespoons of bacon fat in a big twelve-inch skillet, over medium. Slice the potatoes thin, about an eighth of an inch, and lay them in with the onion and half the salt. Now here's the part everybody gets wrong. You put a lid on it. Ten minutes covered, so the potatoes steam tender. Then you take the lid off, turn 'em, and give 'em ten more minutes to brown in spots.
[P9|] Salt, pepper, a little paprika. Then you make four little hollows with the back of a spoon, crack an egg into each one, put the lid back on, and four or five minutes later the whites are set and the yolks are still soft.
[P10|] Emil used to say a man can tell how much you care about him by whether his egg yolk runs. I didn't understand that at sixteen. I do now.
[P11|] Number two. Haluski. Cabbage and noodles.
[P12|] We had a sawyer named Stan Kowalczyk whose mother came over from Poland, and this was his supper. He showed me how to make it my second winter, standing right there at the stove with his hat still on.
[P13|] One green cabbage, two onions, six tablespoons of butter, or half butter and half bacon fat, and twelve ounces of wide egg noodles. That's about eighty cents a plate.
[P14|] If you've got a little bacon or kielbasa, brown it first and set it aside, and keep the fat. Melt the butter in that fat, add the onions and give them ten minutes. Then in goes the sliced cabbage with the salt, and this is where you need patience. Twenty, twenty-five minutes over medium, turning it now and then, until it's soft and sweet and browned in spots. Boil your noodles, drain them, and toss everything together with a lot of black pepper.
[P15|] Stan's rule was, don't you dare rush the cabbage. If it's only wilted, it tastes like nothing but water. Low and slow, until it's golden and tastes almost like caramel. That's the whole dish, right there.
[P16|] Number three. Chicken noodle soup from leg quarters.
[P17|] This is the one on page thirty, and if you only make one thing from tonight, make this one. Because two chicken leg quarters make the broth and the meat both. A big pot of soup for less than what one quart costs at the deli counter. About eighty-five cents a bowl.
[P18|] Two leg quarters into twelve cups of cold water with a tablespoon of salt, a bay leaf and half an onion. You simmer it fifty minutes. Simmer, not boil. A hard boil makes a cloudy broth. Skim off the foam that comes up.
[P19|] Then you lift the chicken out, pull the meat off the bones and chop it, and put the bones right back in for another twenty minutes. That's where the real flavor comes from. Strain the bones out. In go two onions, three carrots and three ribs of celery, chopped, with a little thyme and pepper, for fifteen minutes. Then six ounces of egg noodles and the chicken, eight more minutes.
[P20|] Here's the trick that saves you a lot of mushy soup. Only cook the noodles for what you're gonna eat tonight. Keep the rest of the broth in the cold, and cook fresh noodles each time you heat it up. Noodles sitting in soup overnight swell up like little sponges.
[P21|] In camp, that broth was medicine. A man came in with a cold, Emil gave him a bowl of this and sent him to his bunk early, and nine times out of ten he was back on the saw the next morning.
[P22|] Now, before I give you number four, let me show you something that surprised me when I sat down and added it up. A supper for four, the potato and egg one I just showed you, costs about two dollars and sixty cents at home. Four breakfast combos at the drive-through, about thirty-four dollars. You keep thirty-one dollars and forty cents. The haluski, three dollars and twenty cents, against about thirty-eight dollars for four pasta plates delivered. Cook five of these suppers a week instead of eating out twice, and a family of four keeps something like seventy dollars a week. That's close to thirty-six hundred dollars a year. That's a lot of heating oil.
[P23|] Number four. Depression macaroni and tomatoes.
[P24|] My mother called this goulash, and nobody in our house ever argued with her about it. It came out of the hard years, when a pound of macaroni and a can of tomatoes had to feed the whole family. About seventy cents a plate.
[P25|] You heat the oven to three seventy-five. Boil a pound of elbow macaroni two minutes less than the box says, and drain it. It's gonna finish in the oven, so if you cook it all the way now, it comes out mush. In an oven-safe skillet, cook a chopped onion in three tablespoons of butter for six minutes. Add a big can of diced tomatoes, a spoonful of sugar, salt and pepper, and let it simmer five minutes. Stir in the macaroni, cover the top with a cup of grated cheddar, and bake it twenty minutes, until it's bubbling at the edges.
[P26|] Now that spoonful of sugar. People always ask me about that. It doesn't make it sweet. You won't taste sweet. What it does is take that tinny edge off the canned tomatoes, so all you taste is tomato.
[P27|] Number five. Hamburger gravy over potatoes.
[P28|] This was the supper my crew asked for most on payday week, when the money was running thin and they knew it. One pound of ground beef feeds four men with this. About a dollar ten a plate.
[P29|] Brown the beef with a chopped onion, and don't drain all the fat off. You need about two tablespoons of it left in there. Sprinkle a quarter cup of flour over the top and stir it a full two minutes. If you skip those two minutes, the gravy tastes like paste. Then you whisk in two cups of milk and a cup of beef broth, slow, a little at a time. Simmer it five minutes, until it's thick.
[P30|] And then the pepper. A lot of black pepper. A whole teaspoon, and then some more. This is a gravy that ought to bite you back a little. You ladle it over mashed potatoes, or rice, or a slice of toast, and I've seen grown men go back three times.
[P31|] Number six. Lentil shepherd's pie.
[P32|] Now, in camp we'd have made this with mutton when we could get it. But a pound and a half of brown lentils does the same job for half the money, and it fills a man up every bit as much. About ninety cents a plate.
[P33|] You simmer a cup and a half of brown lentils in water twenty minutes, just until they're tender, and drain them. In a pan, an onion and two carrots, diced, in a little oil, eight minutes. Stir in two tablespoons of tomato paste and a tablespoon of flour for a minute, then three cups of broth, some thyme and a spoon of Worcestershire. Let it simmer until it's thick. That part matters. If the gravy's thin going into the oven, the pie comes out soupy. Add the lentils and a cup of frozen peas.
[P34|] Spread it in a baking dish, cover it with four cups of mashed potatoes and a little cheese, and bake it at four hundred for twenty-five minutes.
[P35|] The trick here is a fork. Before it goes in the oven, rough up the top of the potatoes with a fork, make little ridges all over. Those ridges brown and go crisp, and that's the best part. The men used to fight over the corners.
[P36|] Number seven. Salmon patties and skillet potatoes.
[P37|] Friday was fish day in a lot of camps, and fresh fish in a Minnesota January was not happening. So it was a can of salmon. One fourteen-ounce can makes eight patties. About a dollar sixty a plate, which makes it the most expensive supper I'm giving you tonight, and it's still cheaper than a fish sandwich.
[P38|] Drain the salmon, pull off the skin and the big bones, and flake it in a bowl. Mix it with an egg, half a cup of crushed crackers, a quarter of an onion grated right in, a spoon of lemon juice and pepper. Shape eight little patties and let them sit in the cold ten minutes, so they hold together. Then fry them in oil over medium, three or four minutes a side, until they're crisp outside and hot through.
[P39|] Now, those little soft bones in canned salmon. Don't pick them out. Mash them right into the fish. They're soft, you can eat them, and you'll never notice they're there. My grandmother did it that way every Friday all winter, and so did Emil.
[P40|] Number eight. Camp onion soup with cheese toast.
[P41|] Five onions, a little butter, and patience. That's the whole grocery list, near enough. About ninety-five cents a bowl, and under a slice of toast and melted cheese it eats like a feast.
[P42|] Melt four tablespoons of butter, add five big onions sliced thin, a teaspoon of salt and a teaspoon of sugar. And then you cook them over medium-low for forty-five minutes. I mean it. Forty-five minutes. Stir them now and then, until they're a deep brown. Stir in two tablespoons of flour for two minutes, then six cups of beef broth, a bay leaf and some thyme, and let it simmer twenty minutes.
[P43|] Here's the old trick. When the onions start to stick to the bottom of the pot, you add a spoonful of water and scrape it all up. Every time you do that, the soup gets darker and it gets better. Ladle it into bowls, put a slice of toasted bread on top, cover it with grated cheese, and put it under the broiler two or three minutes until it bubbles.
[P44|] We'd make this on the coldest nights, when the men came in so stiff they couldn't hold a fork right. A bowl of hot onion soup, you hold it in both hands.
[P45|] Number nine. Colcannon with a fried egg.
[P46|] Our blacksmith was an Irishman named Dennis, and this was what he called a real supper. Mashed potatoes and buttered cabbage stirred together, with a fried egg on top. About seventy cents a plate.
[P47|] Boil two and a half pounds of potatoes until they're tender, drain them, and let them dry out in the pot a minute. Meanwhile, melt half the butter in a pan and fry three cups of shredded cabbage for six minutes. Fry it, don't boil it. Boiled cabbage makes the whole thing watery. Add some sliced green onions. Mash the potatoes by hand with warm milk, the rest of the butter, salt and pepper. Mash them by hand, now, too much mashing and they go gluey. Fold the cabbage in.
[P48|] And then the way Dennis did it. Every plate gets a fried egg on top, and you make a little hollow in the middle and drop a lump of butter into it. He said that's how they eat it in Ireland, and I never had any reason to doubt him.
[P49|] And number ten. The one I promised you. Spaghetti with garlic and toasted crumbs.
[P50|] Fifty-five cents a plate. Twenty minutes. And it's made out of the one thing every kitchen throws away, the stale end of the bread. The Italians in the mining towns up on the Iron Range called it poor man's parmesan.
[P51|] You boil a pound of spaghetti in well-salted water, and before you drain it, you save a cup of that cloudy pasta water. Meanwhile, warm half a cup of oil in a skillet and toast a cup and a half of coarse crumbs from stale bread, with a little salt, stirring, until they're golden. Tip them out.
[P52|] Put the rest of the oil in the skillet with six cloves of garlic sliced thin and a pinch of red pepper flakes, over low heat, until the garlic is pale gold. Pale gold, now. If it goes brown, it goes bitter. Toss the spaghetti in that garlic oil with a splash of the pasta water, and watch, it turns from oil into a glossy sauce that clings to every single strand. That's the starch in the water doing it.
[P53|] And the crumbs go on at the table, scattered thick over the top. If you put them on early, they go soggy. At the table, they crunch.
[P54|] When I was a young cook and I was broke at the end of the month, which was most months, I lived on this. I still make it at least once a month, because I like it better than most of the expensive things I've eaten.
[P55|] So there's your ten. But now let me show you the thing that saved us more money than any one recipe.
[P56|] Emil never cooked one supper at a time. He cooked a week at a time. Sunday's pot became Monday's start. In my book I wrote it out the way he did it, four weekly menus, each one with its own shopping list, one trip to the store and that's it.
[P57|] Here's week one, the soup-pot week. Sunday, a sheet pan of chicken legs and potatoes. Monday, the chicken noodle soup, out of the leftover bones and meat from Sunday. Tuesday, haluski. Wednesday, hamburger gravy over potatoes. Thursday, Spanish rice and pinto beans. Friday, salmon patties. Saturday, the potato, onion and egg supper. For a family of four, the whole week comes to about twenty-nine dollars and seventy cents. That's about a dollar and six cents a plate. The end-of-the-month week in the back of the book comes to a dollar and four.
[P58|] And the rule behind all of it is the one Emil told me the first winter. Meat flavors, it doesn't fill. Half a pound of sausage in a pot of potatoes feeds six. A pound of sausage on a plate feeds two.
[P59|] One more word about keeping it safe, because a big pot of soup is a lot of food to look after. Cooked food goes in the cold within two hours of coming off the heat. And when you heat it back up, bring it all the way back to steaming.
