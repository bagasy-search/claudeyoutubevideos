How do we know?

Wait, though. How can anyone look at a pile of bones that are tens of thousands of years old and say how old that person was when they died? It's a fair question, and the honest answer is: with clues, and with a margin of error.

For children, it's surprisingly precise. Teeth grow on a very regular schedule. Baby teeth come in, adult teeth form inside the jaw and push their way out at known ages. Look at a child's jaw and you can often tell their age within a year or two. The long bones help too. The ends of the bones fuse to the shaft at fairly predictable ages, all through the teenage years and into the early twenties.

For adults, it gets harder. Researchers look at how worn down the teeth are, which depends on age but also on diet. They look at the joints of the pelvis, which change slowly over the decades. They look at the seams of the skull, which gradually close up. Each clue on its own is rough. Put together, they give a range. This person was probably between thirty five and fifty. This one was probably over sixty.

And that's important, because older skeletons are often harder to age than younger ones. Some studies have found that the usual methods tend to underestimate the ages of older people. Which means that some of the Stone Age folks who have been labelled as forty might actually have been older than that.

This led to an idea called the grandmother hypothesis. It suggests that humans evolved to live long after they stop having children because grandmothers who helped feed their grandchildren allowed their daughters to have more babies, and allowed more of those babies to survive. It's still a hypothesis, and scientists argue about how much of human longevity it can really explain. But here's a fact that makes it hard to ignore.
