[HOOK] One liter of used motor oil. That is about a quart. I poured it into that tank at six o'clock last night, and at two in the morning this little stove was still burning a clean blue flame, and my garage was sitting at sixty-two degrees with twenty-eight outside.
[HOOK] No gas line. No propane bottle. The only electricity it uses is a tiny twelve volt fan out of a dead computer, about the same as a phone charger. And the fuel is the black stuff that comes out of an engine at every oil change, the stuff most shops pay somebody to haul away.
[PROMISE] Today I am going to build the whole thing in front of you, in eight steps, with every measurement. The pot, the air tube with the exact holes, the fan, the drip you can count by the drop, the chimney, and how to light it so it burns blue instead of black. And at the end I will show you the numbers from that night, hour by hour, so you know exactly what one liter buys you.
[PROMISE] Two of those steps are where almost everybody goes wrong. Step number three, the holes in the air tube, and step number six, the drip. Get those two right and this stove runs all night without you touching it. Get them wrong and you get smoke, soot, and a stove that coughs at you.
[STORY] Here is why I built this one. Last winter I was burning through a twenty pound propane bottle about every five days out here, just to keep my hands working. By January that bottle was the most expensive tool in the shop, and it did not even fix anything.
[STORY] I already had a waste oil burner. My first one was a brake drum with a coffee can drip and no fan at all. It made heat, I will give it that. But it drank oil like an old truck, a liter every hour, it smoked when it was cold, and it only had one speed, which was too much.
[STORY] So this time I gave myself a rule. Small, slow and clean. A stove that sips instead of gulps. Something I could set at six in the evening and walk away from. And the part that made that possible was not bigger. It was a fan the size of a coaster.
[STORY] Before we touch a tool, one thing you have to understand. Used motor oil does not burn like you think. Pour some in a jar lid and hold a match to it, and the match goes out. What burns is the vapor coming off hot oil, mixed with plenty of air. So everything we build today does two jobs. It makes the oil hot, and it feeds it air in the right place. That is the whole secret.
[ONE] Step number one is the oil itself, and this is the step people skip. They pour straight out of the drain pan into the stove, with water and grit and little bits of gasket in it. Then the stove pops and spits, the drip line clogs, and they decide waste oil is junk.
[ONE] Here is the fix, and it costs nothing but patience. Pour the oil into a five gallon bucket and let it sit for a week. Water is heavier than oil, so it sinks to the bottom with the grit. Then you pour off the top two thirds, slowly, and leave the bottom alone.
[ONE] Then filter it. An old cotton t-shirt stretched over a funnel, held on with a rubber band. It is slow, a gallon takes about twenty minutes, so let it drip while you work. What comes through is clean, dark and smooth. Keep it in a metal can with a lid, never an open pail next to the stove.
[TWO] Step number two is the burner pot, the heart of the stove. Most people use a coffee can or a thin paint can here, and it works for about a week. Then the bottom burns through, and you have hot oil on the floor of your stove.
[TWO] I used a piece of four inch steel pipe, threaded on both ends, six inches tall. On the bottom I screwed a four inch steel pipe cap, the kind plumbers throw in the scrap bin. Before I tightened it, I buttered the threads with high temperature furnace cement. No welding, and it does not leak.
[TWO] Why steel pipe and not a can? Because the pot has to get hot and stay hot. Thick walls hold heat, so when a drop of oil lands on that floor it turns to vapor right away instead of making a puddle. A hot pot is a clean pot. A cold pot is a smoky one.
[THREE] Step number three is the air tube, and this is where most stoves go wrong. It is a piece of one and a quarter inch steel pipe, about ten inches long, that stands right in the middle of the pot. The fan blows into the top of it, and the air comes out through little holes along the bottom half.
[THREE] Here is what most people do. They drill a few big half inch holes because it is faster. The air comes out in three fat jets, it blows the flame around, it cools the pot, and you get a lazy orange fire with black smoke off the top.
[THREE] Here is what works. Four rows of holes, eight holes in every row, each hole one eighth of an inch, that is three millimeters. The first row starts one inch above the bottom, and each row is half an inch above the last one. Thirty-two small holes instead of three big ones.
[THREE] Then you crimp the bottom end of the tube shut with a hammer, so all the air has to come out of the sides. Lots of small jets, spread out, give the vapor air from every direction at once. That is what turns the flame blue. When you drill them, use a drop of oil on the bit and go slow. Steel pipe eats cheap bits.
[FOUR] Step number four is the fan, and this is the part that changed everything for me. It is an eighty millimeter twelve volt fan out of an old desktop computer. Every dead computer has one, and most people throw it out with the case.
[FOUR] The mistake is mounting the fan right on the stove. The plastic softens, the bearing dries out, and in a week your fan is a melted pancake. So my fan never touches the stove. It sits at the end of an eighteen inch steel elbow off the top of the air tube, out in the cool air, held on with a tin can funnel and a hose clamp.
[FOUR] To control it I wired in a little speed knob, the kind with a dial that costs a few dollars. Low is for lighting. Medium is for running. It pulls about two watts. That means it will run off a twelve volt wall plug, off a car battery for days, or off a small solar panel when the power is out.
[FOUR] And here is why the fan matters so much. Without it, the stove only gets as much air as the chimney pulls, and that changes with the wind and the weather. With the fan, you decide how much air goes in. You control the fire instead of the weather controlling it. That is the difference between a stove that drinks a liter an hour and one that sips a liter all night.
[FIVE] Step number five is the body. I used two old fifteen inch steel wheels off a junk car, the plain stamped kind. The bottom wheel lies face down, so the center becomes the floor of the stove. The top wheel sits on it face up, and I bolted the two together through the rims with four bolts. Still no welding.
[FIVE] The hole in the center of the top wheel is about three inches across, and that is exactly the size of the chimney. I drilled one more hole in the top for the air tube, and I used one of the lug holes for the drip line. Then I set the burner pot right in the middle of the bottom wheel, on the steel, with the air tube coming down into it.
[FIVE] The last thing on the body is a little door. With a grinder I cut a window three inches by four in the side of the bottom wheel, and covered it with a scrap of steel on a hinge. That is where you light it and where you look at the flame. If you cannot see the flame, you are tuning blind.
[SIX] Step number six is the drip, the second place people get it wrong. The tank is an old one gallon steel can, cleaned out, hung on the wall about three feet above the stove and four feet off to the side. Never right over the top of it.
[SIX] From the bottom of the can, a length of three sixteenths copper brake line runs down to a small brass needle valve, and from the valve into the stove through the lug hole. The end of the line sits right over the pot, so every drop lands on the hot floor of the pot, not on the air tube.
[SIX] Now the number. Forty drops a minute. That is all. Look at your watch and count for fifteen seconds. If you count ten drops, you are right on it. Forty drops a minute is about one liter in eight hours. That is the setting that gives you the title of this video.
[SIX] And here is the mistake. People open the valve wide because they want heat fast. In a pot that is not hot yet, the oil makes a puddle. The puddle sits there soaking up heat, then it all flashes at once, and the stove goes whump. That is not oil burning. That is too much oil, too early. Slow drip, always.
[SEVEN] Step number seven is the chimney. Three inch single wall steel stove pipe, from the center of the top wheel straight up, at least eight feet, then out through the wall with a proper wall thimble and a cap on top. Keep it a good eighteen inches from any wood.
[SEVEN] The mistake here is long sideways runs, because it is easier to snake the pipe across the ceiling to a window. Sideways pipe collects soot and kills the draft. Up is your friend. A tall straight chimney pulls on its own, and it works together with the fan instead of fighting it.
[EIGHT] Step number eight is lighting it and tuning it to blue. This is the most important step, so do it the same way every time. Pour a quarter cup of diesel or kerosene into the cold pot and drop in a twist of newspaper. Never gasoline. Not once, not a little.
[EIGHT] Light the paper through the door, fan on low, and let that burn for about five minutes. You are not making heat yet. You are heating the pot. When I point my infrared thermometer at the side of the pot and it reads over six hundred degrees, it is ready.
[EIGHT] Now open the needle valve to about sixty drops a minute for the first ten minutes, and bring the fan up to medium. Watch through the door. You will see the flame go from orange and lazy to a roaring blue ring around the air tube. You will hear it too. It changes from a crackle to a steady hiss, like a gas burner.
[EIGHT] Once it is blue and hissing, bring the drip back down to forty drops a minute, ten in fifteen seconds, and leave it alone. If it starts to go orange again, give it a touch more fan. Air first, oil second. That is the whole tuning, and after a couple of times you will do it without thinking.
[TEST] So here are the numbers from that night. I measured one liter into the can at six in the evening. Twenty-eight degrees outside. Inside my one car garage it was thirty-nine. The walls have old insulation, the overhead door does not.
[TEST] Seven o'clock, the thermometer on the bench said fifty-one. Eight o'clock, fifty-eight. By ten it was sixty-two, and it held there the rest of the night. The top of the stove was reading about four hundred and fifty degrees, hot enough to keep a kettle going the whole time.
[TEST] At two in the morning I came back out in my slippers. Still a clean blue flame, and about a finger of oil left in the can. It finally ran dry a little after half past two. That is eight and a half hours on one liter, and the fan used less power than a night light.
[TEST] Now let me be straight with you. That is the low drip. It keeps a one car garage at working temperature. It is not going to heat a whole house. If you open it up to about a hundred drops a minute, it goes through a liter in around three hours, and you will be working in a t-shirt. You pick.
[FIX] A few problems you will run into. If the flame is orange and there is black smoke off the chimney, you do not have enough air. Turn the fan up a notch before you touch anything else. Nine times out of ten that is it.
[FIX] If the stove puffs or goes whump, there is a puddle in the pot. Close the drip, leave the fan on, and let it burn the puddle off. Then start again with a slower drip. Never look in the door while it is puffing. Stand to the side.
[FIX] If the drip slows down by itself in the middle of the night, the oil got cold and thick. Hang the tank on the warm side of the shop, or use thinner oil in deep winter. And if the flame keeps dying when you turn the fan up, your holes are too big or too low, and the air is blowing the fire out.
[FIX] And when you shut it down, do it in the right order. Close the drip first, and leave the fan running for about five minutes so it burns the last of the oil in the pot clean. Then switch the fan off. Do it the other way around and the oil left in the pot just smokes, and your whole shop smells like a burnt engine in the morning.
[FIX] Once a month, with the stove cold, knock the soot out of the pipe and scrape the crust out of the bottom of the pot. Ten minutes. A clean stove burns cleaner and lasts for years.
[SAFETY] One line on safety and then I will leave it alone. This goes in a garage or a shop with a real chimney to the outside, never in a bedroom or a living space, with a fire extinguisher and a carbon monoxide alarm within reach.
[MONEY] Now the money. The fan came out of a dead computer, so that was nothing. The speed knob was about four dollars, the needle valve about eight, the brake line about six, the furnace cement about eight, and the stove pipe and the cap were the big one, about forty. The wheels, the pipe and the can came out of the scrap pile. Call it sixty-five dollars all in.
[MONEY] Last winter the propane was costing me about twenty-five dollars a week. This stove paid for itself in less than three weeks. And the oil? A lot of small shops are happy to give it to you if you bring your own clean cans and come back regularly. Ask nicely.
[RECAP] So, the whole build in one breath. Settle and filter the oil. A thick steel pot that holds heat. An air tube with thirty-two small holes, crimped at the bottom. A computer fan kept away from the heat, with a speed knob. Two old wheels for the body, with a door you can see through. A drip at forty drops a minute. A tall straight chimney. And light it hot, air first, oil second.
