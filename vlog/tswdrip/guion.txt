[HOOK] No plug. No fan. No pump. No gas bottle. Just a jug of old motor oil, a drip, and this. A stove made from two old truck wheels and a brake rotor, roaring away in the corner of my garage, with a blue flame you can hear from across the room.
[HOOK] People spend a fortune every winter heating a garage. Electric heaters that trip the breaker. Propane tanks that run out on the coldest night. And the whole time, there is a jug of used oil sitting on the shelf from the last oil change, holding more heat than a tank of propane.
[HOOK] Today I build a drip stove that runs on nothing but gravity and old oil. Every part comes off a scrap pile. Every measurement, every hole, step by step. Then we light it, set the drip, and I show you how long one jug lasts and what it did to the temperature in here.
[HOOK] And stay for the drip plate. It is one small piece of steel, and it is the difference between a stove that smokes you out of the shop and one that burns clean all night.
[INTRO] If you are new here, this is the Scrap Workshop. I build heat out of things people throw away. Old oil, old metal, old parts. The cold is here, and the cheapest heat you will ever have is heat made from something that was headed for the trash.
[INTRO] This stove has no electric parts at all. That matters. When the power goes out in a storm, this one keeps running. The oil flows down by gravity, the chimney pulls the air by itself, and the fire does the rest. That is why people call it unlimited. As long as you have oil, you have heat.
[SAFETY] One quick word before we start. This stove goes in a garage or a shop, with the chimney going outside. Never in a bedroom, never in a closed room. A carbon monoxide alarm on the wall, a fire extinguisher by the door, and the oil jug at least three feet from the stove. That is the whole sermon. Let's build.
[MATERIALS] Here is everything on the bench. Two old steel wheels off a pickup truck, sixteen inch, the plain steel kind, not aluminum. Any tire shop has a stack of them out back. One old disc brake rotor, about twelve inches across. One old cast iron frying pan, ten inches, the kind with a crack in the handle that nobody cooks with anymore.
[MATERIALS] Then a few small things. A short piece of steel angle, about eight inches long. A piece of two inch steel pipe, fourteen inches long. A plastic jug for the oil. About six feet of quarter inch copper line off an old refrigerator. One brass needle valve. A handful of long bolts with nuts, and a few lengths of four inch stove pipe for the chimney.
[MATERIALS] Tools. An angle grinder, a drill with a quarter inch bit, a step bit, two wrenches and a tape measure. No welding on this stove. Everything goes together with bolts.
[STEP1] Step one, the body. The two wheels go face to face, the way they would sit on a truck, so the two centers touch and the rims make a round drum. Line up the lug holes. Five lug holes on each wheel, and they match perfectly, because they were made to fit the same hub.
[STEP1] Put a long bolt through each lug hole, with a nut on both sides, and tighten them down. Now you have one heavy steel drum, about sixteen inches across and fourteen inches tall, with a solid steel wall in the middle where the two centers meet. That wall in the middle is in the way, so it has to come out.
[STEP1] Before you bolt them, take the grinder and cut out the center of the top wheel. Leave the lug ring, cut out the big hole in the middle, about eight inches across. The bottom wheel keeps its center. That solid steel center becomes the floor of the stove. Thick steel, made to carry a truck. It will take the heat for years.
[STEP1] A quick word on picking the wheels. Take a magnet with you to the tire shop. Steel wheels grab the magnet hard. Look for two that match, same size and same lug pattern, and pass on any that are bent or cracked around the center. A little rust on the outside does not matter at all. The fire will take care of the paint in the first hour anyway, so run it the first time with the big door open.
[STEP2] Step two, the door. On the side of the bottom wheel, cut a window, four inches wide and three inches tall, with the grinder. Keep the piece you cut out. Two small bolts and a strip of steel make a hinge, and that piece becomes a little door. This is how you light the stove, and how you peek at the flame.
[STEP3] Step three, the burn pan. Cut the handle off the cast iron frying pan, right at the edge. Now you have a heavy iron dish. It sits on the floor of the stove, in the center, right below the hole in the top wheel. Cast iron holds heat like nothing else. Once it is hot, every drop of oil that lands in it turns to vapor in a second.
[STEP3] Fill the bottom of the pan with half an inch of clean dry sand. The sand spreads the first drops out so they do not pool in one spot, and it keeps the flame steady when the drip changes.
[STEP4] Step four, the drip plate. This is the part I told you about. The short piece of steel angle goes in at an angle, from the side of the drum down toward the pan, like a little slide. The oil line drips onto the top end of it, and the oil runs down the slide into the pan.
[STEP4] Why bother? Because that slide sits right over the fire. By the time a drop of oil runs down it, the drop is already hot, thin and half way to vapor. Cold oil dropped straight into a fire smokes. Warm oil off a hot slide flashes into gas and burns clean. One piece of scrap angle, and the smoke is gone.
[STEP4] Drill two holes through the side of the drum and bolt the angle in place, with the top end four inches above the pan and the bottom end right over the edge of it. Then drill one more hole above the top end for the copper line to come in.
[STEP5] Step five, the burner tube. The two inch pipe stands straight up in the middle of the pan, with the bottom resting on the sand. Mark four rings around it, one inch apart, starting two inches from the bottom. Six holes on each ring, quarter inch. That is twenty four holes.
[STEP5] Those holes are where the stove breathes. The chimney pulls air in through them, the air mixes with the oil vapor rising from the pan, and that is where the blue flame lives. No fan needed. The chimney does the work of the fan, for nothing, all night long.
[STEP6] Step six, the top. The brake rotor sits on top of the drum like a lid. It already has a big hole in the center where it bolted onto the hub. That hole is just right for the burner tube to poke through and for the hot gas to come out. And the rotor is thick, so it becomes a cooking plate.
[STEP6] On top of the rotor goes a short piece of stove pipe, held down with three bolts through the rotor holes. That is the collar for the chimney. Then the chimney goes up, straight, eight feet, and out through the wall. Tall and straight, no long sideways runs. A short chimney means a weak pull, and a weak pull means smoke.
[STEP7] Step seven, the drip line. The oil jug goes on a shelf on the wall, a couple of feet higher than the stove and at least three feet away from it to the side. Drill a hole in the cap, push the copper line through, and seal it with a little stove cement. A tiny hole in the top of the jug lets air in so the oil flows steady.
[STEP7] The needle valve goes in the line down low, where you can reach it easily. Then the copper goes into the hole in the side of the drum and ends right over the top of the slide. Put one loop in the copper before it reaches the stove, so the heat does not creep back up the line.
[STEP7] That is the whole system. Gravity pushes the oil, the valve sets how many drops, the slide warms them, the pan turns them to gas, the tube feeds the air, the chimney pulls it all. Not one wire in the whole thing.
[STEP8] Last step, the stand. The stove sits on four bricks on the concrete, so the floor stays cool and the air can move under it. Put a level on the rotor. If it leans, the oil runs to one side of the pan and the fire burns crooked. A brick shim fixes it.
[FIRE] Now the fun part. Open the little door, put a rag in the pan, soak it with a splash of oil, and lay a piece of paper on top. Light the paper and close the door. Let it burn for two or three minutes with the valve closed. You want the pan and the slide hot before any real oil arrives.
[FIRE] Now open the valve, a crack. One drop every two seconds. At first the flame is orange and quiet. Watch the slide. In a few minutes the drops start to sizzle as they run down it. Then the tube gets hot, the chimney starts pulling, and you hear it. That low roar. Look through the door. There it is. Blue flame all around the holes of the tube.
[FIRE] Once it roars, you can turn the drip up to one drop a second. Any more than that and the air cannot keep up and you start to see smoke at the chimney. So you turn it back a little. The flame tells you everything. Blue and roaring means you got it right.
[TEST] Now the numbers. This was a cold morning in a five hundred square foot garage, an old building with a big metal door. The thermometer on the wall read forty degrees when I lit it.
[TEST] At one drop a second, in thirty minutes the wall read fifty four. In one hour, sixty six. That big steel drum gets hot all over, and the rotor on top gets so hot that a pot of water on it comes to a boil in about fifteen minutes.
[TEST] And the oil. At that setting it burns a bit less than half a liter an hour. Once the room is warm, I turn it down to a drop every two seconds and it just holds the heat. A ten liter jug at the low setting runs this stove for close to a full day and night.
[TEST] Think about where that oil comes from. A pickup takes about five liters at an oil change. Two oil changes, yours and a neighbor's, and you have a day of heat. A small shop that changes oil every week has more than it could ever burn. That is what unlimited means here.
[POWER] Now think about the night the power goes out. Ice on the lines, the whole street dark. The electric heater is dead. The furnace fan is dead. And this stove does not care. There is no wire in it, no switch, no motor. The jug is up high, the valve is set, the chimney is pulling, and the fire keeps going exactly the same as it did the night before.
[POWER] That is the real reason I like a gravity drip. Fans and pumps make a stove burn hotter, but they also give it something to fail. Every part on this one is either steel or brass, and none of them has to move. The only thing you have to do is keep the jug full.
[WHY] So why does this burn clean when a can of oil with a rag in it just smokes? Three reasons. The hot slide warms the oil before it lands. The hot iron pan turns it to vapor instantly, because oil does not burn as a liquid, it burns as a gas. And the tall chimney pulls air through the holes in the tube, so the gas has enough air to burn completely.
[WHY] When you see black smoke, one of those three is missing. The pan is still cold, the chimney is too short, or there is more oil than air. Fix that one thing and the smoke goes away.
[OILS] What can you burn? Used motor oil is the best. Old hydraulic oil and gear oil work if you mix them half and half with motor oil, because they are thick and drip slow. Old cooking oil from the fryer works too, it just smells a little like a diner.
[OILS] What you never put in the jug is gasoline, thinner, or anything with a sharp smell. Those give off vapor right in the jug, and vapor next to a fire is how people get hurt. Heavy oil, slow drip, hot pan.
[MISTAKES] Now the mistakes, so you do not make them. Number one, aluminum wheels. They look nicer, but aluminum softens in this kind of heat. Steel wheels only. If a magnet does not stick to it, it does not go in the stove.
[MISTAKES] Number two, too much oil on a cold pan. If you open the valve wide before the iron is hot, the oil pools in the sand, and when it finally catches it all goes up at once with a big puff of smoke. Warm it up first, then turn it up slowly.
[MISTAKES] Number three, dirty oil. Let your old oil sit in a jug for a few days so the water and the grit settle to the bottom. Pour off the top and run it through an old t shirt. Grit plugs the needle valve, and a plugged valve means the stove dies at three in the morning.
[MISTAKES] Number four, leaving it alone too long at the start. The first time you run it, stay with it for an hour. Learn how your drip behaves as the jug empties and the flow gets slower. After that, it is the most boring stove in the world. It just runs.
[CLEAN] Cleaning takes ten minutes. Once a week, when it is cold, lift the rotor off, pull the tube, and scrape the carbon out of the pan. Fresh sand in, tap the tube with a stick to clear the holes, and you are ready for another week.
[COOK] And the bonus. That brake rotor on top is a real cooking plate. Coffee in the morning, a pot of beans at lunch, a skillet of eggs if you want. Heat and a kitchen in the same piece of scrap.
[CLOSE] So that is it. Two old truck wheels, a brake rotor, a cracked frying pan and a jug of used oil. No electric, no gas, no fan. A stove that roars blue, warms a five hundred foot garage in an hour, and runs a whole day on a ten liter jug.
